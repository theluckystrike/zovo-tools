Where Your Paycheck Goes in Oregon

Oregon workers face a trade-off. You won't pay sales tax on anything you buy in Oregon, but the state makes up for it with one of the highest income tax rates in the country. The top marginal rate of 9.9% kicks in at relatively modest income levels compared to other high-tax states. Here is a visual snapshot for a $75,000 salary.

The chart above shows how a typical $75,000 Oregon salary divides between take-home pay, federal income tax, Oregon state income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and the Statewide Transit Tax. I've found that most Oregon workers keep approximately 67-71% of their gross salary. While that sounds steep, remember that you won't lose another 5-10% to sales tax on purchases like residents of Washington or California do.

Oregon Tax Brackets Explained

Oregon uses a four-tier progressive income tax. The rates range from 4.75% to 9.9%, and the top bracket starts at a relatively low income threshold compared to other high-tax states. This means middle-income earners in Oregon pay proportionally more state tax than they would in states like California where the high brackets don't kick in until much higher incomes.

Oregon's standard deduction for 2025 is approximately $2,745 for single filers and $5,495 for married filing jointly. This is notably lower than most other states and much lower than the federal standard deduction. Oregon also allows you to deduct your federal tax liability from your state taxable income, which is an unusual provision that most states don't offer. This federal tax subtraction can significantly reduce your Oregon tax bill. According to the Wikipedia entry on state income taxes, Oregon is one of only a few states that still allow this federal deduction.

Oregon Statewide Transit Tax

to income tax, Oregon levies a Statewide Transit Tax (STT) of 0.1% on all wages. This was introduced to fund public transit improvements across the state. While 0.1% sounds small, it adds up. On a $75,000 salary that is $75 per year. Employers are required to withhold this from every paycheck. Some areas like the Portland metro also have additional local taxes (Multnomah County's Preschool for All tax and Metro's Supportive Housing Services tax), but those aren't included in this calculator since they vary by location.

Oregon's No Sales Tax Advantage

Oregon is one of only five states with no statewide sales tax (along with Montana, New Hampshire, Delaware, and Alaska). This is a significant financial advantage that many salary comparisons overlook.

Consider this: if you earn $75,000 and spend roughly $40,000 on taxable goods and services, a state with 6% sales tax would cost you $2,400 in sales tax alone. In Oregon, that $2,400 stays in your pocket. When comparing take-home pay between Oregon and a state like Washington (which has no income tax but charges 6.5%+ sales tax), the real-world difference is smaller than the income tax numbers suggest.

I've found this to be one of the most misunderstood aspects of Oregon taxes. People see the 9.9% top rate and assume Oregon is terrible for workers. But when you factor in zero sales tax, the effective tax burden is competitive with many states that look cheaper on paper. Of course, if you can work remotely from a state with neither income tax nor sales tax, that is the ideal scenario. A thread on Hacker News about remote work tax covered this exact comparison extensively.

Oregon Tax Tips and Strategies

Oregon's high income tax rates make tax planning especially valuable. Here are strategies I've researched for Oregon residents.

Pre-Tax Retirement Contributions

With a top state rate of 9.9% on top of federal rates, every dollar of pre-tax 401(k) contribution saves you significantly in Oregon. A $1,000 contribution in the 8.75% state bracket plus 22% federal bracket saves $307.50 in taxes. The 2025 401(k) limit is $23,500 ($31,000 if over 50). Oregon workers benefit more from pre-tax contributions than residents of low-tax states simply because the state tax savings are larger.

Oregon's Federal Tax Subtraction

Oregon uniquely allows you to subtract your federal tax liability from your state taxable income (capped at $7,250 single / $14,500 married for 2025). This effectively gives you a discount on state tax based on what you already pay federally. Don't overlook this. It is automatically applied in this calculator but you should verify it appears on your actual tax return. Many tax preparers miss this Oregon-specific benefit.

Oregon Earned Income Tax Credit

Oregon offers its own Earned Income Tax Credit at 12% of the federal EITC for most filers (or 9% if you have no qualifying children). If your income qualifies, this credit can substantially reduce your state tax bill. Combined with the federal EITC, low-to-moderate income workers can see significant tax relief.

Consider Total Tax Burden

When comparing Oregon to other states, always factor in the zero sales tax. A Stack Overflow discussion on tax calculations highlighted how developers building payroll systems often forget to account for sales tax in total-burden comparisons. If you spend $30,000-$50,000 annually on taxable goods, Oregon's zero sales tax saves you $1,800-$4,500 compared to a state with 6-9% sales tax. That narrows the income tax gap substantially.

Remote Work Tax Considerations in Oregon

Oregon's tax field creates interesting dynamics for remote workers. Here is what you know if you work remotely in Oregon or are considering it.

Working Remotely for an Out-of-State Employer

If you live in Oregon and work remotely for a company based in another state, you owe Oregon income tax on your wages. Your employer may or may not withhold Oregon taxes depending on whether they have a payroll presence in Oregon. If they don't withhold, you are responsible for making quarterly estimated tax payments to Oregon. Many remote workers discover this the hard way when they owe a large balance at tax time. I recommend setting up estimated payments proactively if your employer isn't withholding Oregon state tax.

Oregon Residents Working in Washington

A common scenario in the Portland metro area is living in Oregon and working in Washington (or vice versa). If you live in Oregon, you owe Oregon income tax regardless of where your employer is located. However, if you live in Washington and work in Oregon, you owe Oregon income tax on the wages earned while physically working in Oregon. Oregon has no reciprocal tax agreement with Washington, so this can create complications for cross-border commuters. The no-income-tax advantage of Washington residency only applies if you actually perform your work in Washington.

Digital Nomads and Split Residency

If you split time between Oregon and another state, the allocation of income can get complicated. Oregon uses a formula based on the ratio of days worked in Oregon to total days worked. Keep detailed records of your work location if you travel frequently. Oregon is aggressive about claiming tax revenue from workers who have any nexus with the state. A popular approach among tech workers is maintaining Washington residency while working in Oregon offices, but Oregon can challenge this if you spend significant time working from an Oregon location.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Oregon state income tax rate for 2025?

Oregon has four progressive income tax brackets for 2025. The rates are 4.75% on income up to $4,050 (single), 6.75% from $4,051 to $10,200, 8.75% from $10,201 to $125,000, and 9.9% on income over $125,000. These are marginal rates so you only pay each rate on the income within that bracket. Most Oregon workers with moderate incomes will have an effective state tax rate between 7% and 8.5%.

Does Oregon have sales tax?

No. Oregon is one of five states with no statewide sales tax. This means you pay zero tax on retail purchases, restaurant meals, clothing, electronics, and most other goods and services. This is a significant benefit that offsets Oregon's high income tax rates. When comparing Oregon to other states, always factor in the sales tax savings to get a fair comparison.

What is the Oregon Statewide Transit Tax?

The Oregon Statewide Transit Tax (STT) is a 0.1% tax on all wages earned in Oregon. It was enacted in 2018 to fund public transit improvements across the state. All employees working in Oregon are subject to this tax regardless of where they live. On a $75,000 salary it amounts to $75 per year. Employers are required to withhold it from each paycheck.

Can I deduct federal taxes from my Oregon state taxes?

Yes. Oregon is one of the few states that allows you to subtract your federal tax liability from your Oregon taxable income. For 2025, the maximum subtraction is $7,250 for single filers and $14,500 for married filing jointly. This means if you pay $7,000 in federal tax and file as single, your Oregon taxable income is reduced by $7,000. This is a valuable benefit that many Oregon residents overlook.

How does Oregon compare to Washington for total tax burden?

Washington has no state income tax but charges a 6.5% state sales tax (plus local additions, often totaling 8-10%). Oregon has high income tax (up to 9.9%) but zero sales tax. For most middle-income workers, the total tax burden is surprisingly similar. If you earn $75,000 and spend $40,000 on taxable goods, Washington's sales tax costs roughly $3,200-$4,000 while Oregon's income tax costs roughly $4,800-$5,200. The gap is narrower than most people expect. High earners tend to fare better in Washington while moderate earners may prefer Oregon.

Are Portland residents subject to additional taxes?

Yes. Portland metro area residents may owe additional local taxes beyond what this calculator shows. Multnomah County has the Preschool for All tax (1.5% on income over $125,000 single / $200,000 joint, with an additional 1.5% over $250,000 / $400,000). Metro's Supportive Housing Services tax adds 1% on income over $125,000 / $200,000. These are not included in this calculator but can significantly increase the tax burden for high earners in the Portland area.

Is this calculator accurate for self-employed workers?

This calculator is designed for W-2 employees. Self-employed workers in Oregon owe the same income taxes but also pay the employer's share of FICA (an additional 7.65%), making their total FICA burden 15.3%. Self-employed individuals should also account for quarterly estimated tax payments and the self-employment tax deduction. I recommend consulting a tax professional for self-employment calculations.

Oregon Cost of Living Context

Your take-home pay only matters in the context of what it costs to live where you are. Oregon has significant cost-of-living variation depending on whether you are in the Portland metro, smaller cities, or rural areas.

Portland Metro Area

Portland is where most Oregon workers are concentrated, and it has become increasingly expensive over the past decade. Median home prices in the Portland metro range from $450,000 to $550,000 depending on the neighborhood. Rents for a one-bedroom apartment average $1,400-$1,800 in the urban core and $1,200-$1,500 in outer suburbs like Beaverton, Hillsboro, and Gresham. The cost of living is lower than Seattle (just 170 miles north) but higher than most Midwest and Southern cities. Portland residents may also owe additional local taxes not captured in this calculator (Multnomah County and Metro taxes for high earners).

Salem, Eugene, and Bend

Oregon's other major population centers offer more affordable living. Salem (the state capital) has median home prices around $350,000-$400,000. Eugene (home of the University of Oregon) is similar. Bend has become popular with remote workers and outdoor enthusiasts, pushing prices higher ($500,000+) but still below Portland. These cities offer a lower cost of living while keeping access to Oregon's natural amenities. If you work remotely and earn a Portland or national salary while living in Salem or Eugene, your effective purchasing power increases significantly.

Rural Oregon

Eastern Oregon and the southern coast offer dramatically lower housing costs, with homes available for $200,000-$300,000 in many areas. However, job opportunities are limited, wages are typically lower, and access to services (healthcare, shopping, entertainment) requires longer drives. For remote workers, rural Oregon can be an excellent value proposition, combining Oregon's natural beauty with low housing costs. The income tax stays the same regardless of location within the state.

The No Sales Tax Lifestyle

Living in Oregon means you never think about sales tax. Every price tag is the final price. This psychological and financial benefit is hard to quantify but real. Major purchases like electronics, furniture, appliances, and vehicles cost exactly the sticker price. For a household spending $50,000 annually on goods and services, the sales tax savings compared to Washington (10.25% in Seattle) would be over $5,000 per year. Many Washington residents drive to Portland specifically for large purchases to avoid their state's sales tax.

Oregon Tax Calendar and Important Dates

Staying on top of Oregon tax deadlines helps you avoid penalties and plan your cash flow throughout the year. Here are the key dates every Oregon taxpayer should know.

January 15: Fourth quarter estimated tax payment due for the prior tax year. If you owe estimated taxes to Oregon, this is the last quarterly payment before filing your return.

April 15: Oregon income tax return filing deadline (Form OR-40) and first quarter estimated payment for the current year. Oregon follows the federal filing deadline. If you need an extension, Oregon automatically grants one if you file a federal extension, giving you until October 15 to file (but you must still pay any tax owed by April 15).

June 15: Second quarter estimated tax payment due. This is also the extended filing deadline if you are a U.S. citizen living abroad.

September 15: Third quarter estimated tax payment due.

October 15: Extended filing deadline for Oregon returns (if extension filed).

Oregon charges interest and penalties on underpayment of estimated taxes if you owe more than $1,000 at filing time. To avoid this, ensure your withholding and estimated payments cover at least 90% of your current year liability or 100% of your prior year liability. If your income varies significantly from year to year, pay close attention to estimated tax requirements to avoid surprise penalties.

Best Industries and Employers in Oregon

Oregon's economy has diversified significantly over the past two decades, and understanding which industries pay the most helps put your salary calculator results in context. The technology sector anchors the state's high-wage employment. Intel's massive campus in Hillsboro employs over 22,000 workers and has been the state's largest private employer for years. The semiconductor manufacturer's presence has created a broader tech ecosystem in the Portland metro area, attracting companies like Lattice Semiconductor, Mentor Graphics, and dozens of startups. Software and cloud computing also thrive here, with Puppet, Jama Software, and Vacasa all headquartered in Portland.

Nike's world headquarters in Beaverton employs roughly 12,000 people locally and anchors Oregon's athletic and outdoor industry cluster. Columbia Sportswear, adidas North America, and Keen Footwear all maintain major operations in the Portland area. These companies offer competitive salaries, particularly in product design, marketing, and supply chain management. A mid-level product manager at Nike can expect a total compensation package well above $120,000, though Oregon's income tax will take a meaningful share of that.

Healthcare is the state's fastest-growing employment sector. Providence Health, OHSU (Oregon Health and Science University), Kaiser Permanente, and Legacy Health are among the top employers in the Portland metro area. Registered nurses in Oregon earn a median salary of approximately $98,000, which is well above the national median of $81,000. Physicians, pharmacists, and healthcare administrators earn correspondingly higher wages. For healthcare workers using this calculator, the combination of high wages and zero sales tax makes Oregon competitive with neighboring Washington despite the income tax difference.

Oregon's timber and natural resources industry, while smaller than its historical peak, still provides well-paying jobs in rural parts of the state. Weyerhaeuser, Roseburg Forest Products, and several smaller mills employ thousands of workers across southern and eastern Oregon. Agriculture is another pillar, with the Willamette Valley producing wine grapes, hazelnuts, berries, and grass seed. Oregon is the nation's largest producer of hazelnuts, Christmas trees, and several grass seed varieties. While agricultural wages tend to be lower, management and technical roles in agribusiness can be quite competitive.

Portland Metro Tax and the Statewide Transit Tax in Detail

While this calculator handles the Statewide Transit Tax at 0.1%, workers in the Portland metropolitan area should be aware of additional local taxes that can significantly affect take-home pay. These local taxes are not included in this calculator because they vary by exact location, but understanding them is essential for anyone working or living in the tri-county area of Multnomah, Washington, and Clackamas counties.

The Multnomah County Preschool for All (PFA) tax applies to residents of Multnomah County who earn above $125,000 (single) or $200,000 (married filing jointly). The rate is 1.5% on income above the threshold, with an additional 1.5% surtax on income exceeding $250,000 (single) or $400,000 (married). For a single filer earning $200,000 in Multnomah County, this adds $1,125 to their annual tax bill on top of all other state and federal obligations. The PFA tax was approved by voters in 2020 and funds universal preschool programs in the county.

Metro's Supportive Housing Services (SHS) tax is another layer that affects higher earners in the Portland metro region. It applies a 1% tax on income above $125,000 (single) or $200,000 (married filing jointly) for residents of Clackamas, Multnomah, and Washington counties. Unlike the PFA tax, SHS applies across all three metro counties. For someone earning $175,000 as a single filer in the Portland metro, the SHS tax adds $500 per year. Combined with the PFA tax in Multnomah County, a $300,000 earner could owe an additional $4,375 in local taxes beyond what this calculator shows.

The practical impact of these layered taxes is that high earners in Portland face a combined state and local marginal income tax rate that can exceed 13.5% when you add Oregon's 9.9% top bracket, the 0.1% transit tax, the 1.5% PFA tax, and the 1% SHS tax. This rivals California's top rate of 13.3% and makes Portland one of the most heavily taxed metro areas in the country for high-income workers. If you earn above $250,000 and live in Multnomah County, I strongly recommend working with a CPA who understands all of these overlapping tax obligations. The annual compliance burden alone justifies professional tax preparation at those income levels.

Oregon Retirement Planning and Tax-Advantaged Strategies

Oregon's high income tax rates make tax-advantaged retirement planning unusually valuable compared to low-tax states. Every dollar you contribute to a pre-tax retirement account like a 401(k) or traditional IRA reduces both your federal and Oregon state taxable income. In the 8.75% Oregon bracket and 22% federal bracket, a $1,000 pre-tax contribution saves you $307.50 in taxes immediately. Over a 30-year career, consistent pre-tax contributions can save Oregon workers tens of thousands of dollars compared to equivalent workers in states with no income tax.

The Oregon Savings Growth Plan is the state's 457(b) deferred compensation plan available to state and local government employees. It allows contributions up to $23,500 in 2025 ($31,000 if over 50), and these contributions reduce Oregon taxable income. Unlike a 401(k), 457(b) plans don't have a 10% early withdrawal penalty if you separate from service before age 59.5, making them an attractive option for public sector workers considering early retirement. Oregon also participates in PERS (Public Employees Retirement System), which provides a defined benefit pension for qualifying employees. Understanding how PERS interacts with Social Security and personal retirement savings is important for long-term planning.

For workers in the private sector, Roth conversions deserve careful consideration in Oregon. Converting traditional IRA funds to a Roth IRA triggers income tax at your current rate, but all future growth and withdrawals are tax-free. If you plan to retire in Oregon (where the income tax remains), a Roth conversion may not save you much unless you expect to be in a lower bracket in retirement. However, if you plan to retire in a zero-income-tax state like Washington, Nevada, or Florida, doing Roth conversions while working in Oregon and then moving for retirement can lock in decades of tax-free growth. This strategy is particularly popular among tech workers in Portland who plan to relocate after their peak earning years.

Health Savings Accounts (HSAs) are another useful tool for Oregon workers enrolled in high-deductible health plans. HSA contributions are deductible from both federal and Oregon state income, the money grows tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are also tax-free. The 2025 contribution limits are $4,300 for individual coverage and $8,550 for family coverage. For an Oregon worker in the 8.75% state bracket and 22% federal bracket, maxing out a family HSA saves approximately $2,630 in annual taxes. Over time, HSA funds can be invested and grown similarly to a retirement account, making it one of the most tax-efficient savings vehicles available to Oregon residents.
