Colorado Flat Tax Explained (2026)

Colorado is one of a growing number of states that uses a flat income tax rate. Unlike graduated tax states where different income levels are taxed at different rates, Colorado applies one flat rate to all taxable income. Colorado taxable income is calculated starting from your federal taxable income, then applying Colorado-specific additions (e.g. State tax refunds) and subtractions (e.g., SALT cap adjustment, pension exclusion). How Colorado State Income Tax Works

Colorado's tax computation is refreshingly simple compared to most states. Rather than maintaining its own set of deductions, exemptions, and brackets, Colorado begins with your federal taxable income and applies a flat 4.4% rate. This means the federal standard deduction ($14,600 for single filers, $29,200 for married filing jointly in 2025) effectively serves as your Colorado deduction as well.

Here's the calculation for a single filer earning $75,000 with a 6% 401(k) contribution and $200/month health insurance:

  1. Start with gross income: $75,000
  2. Subtract pre-tax deductions: $75,000 - $4,500 (401k) - $2,400 (health) = $68,100 (adjusted gross)
  3. Subtract federal standard deduction: $68,100 - $14,600="$53,500" (federal taxable income)
  4. Apply $53,500 x 4.4% = $2,354 (Colorado state tax)

That's it. No graduated brackets to navigate, no state-specific standard deduction to calculate, no municipal tax layer to worry about. The effective Colorado state tax rate on $75,000 gross is approximately 3.14% ($2,354 / $75,000). The simplicity of this flat-rate system means your state tax burden is completely predictable - for every additional dollar you earn above the standard deduction threshold, you know exactly 4.4 cents goes to Colorado.

Colorado's Flat Tax The Voter-Driven Rate Reduction

Colorado's tax rate has been reduced twice in recent years through ballot measures - a unique feature of Colorado's Taxpayer's Bill of Rights (TABOR). Here's the trajectory I've tracked:

The rate was 4.63% from 2000 through 2019. In 2020, voters passed Proposition 116, reducing it to 4.55%. Then in 2022, voters passed Proposition 121, reducing it further to 4.40%. Each reduction was driven by the TABOR refund mechanism - when state revenue exceeds TABOR limits, the excess must be refunded to taxpayers. Propositions 116 and 121 chose to implement permanent rate reductions rather than one-time refunds.

This voter-driven approach means further rate reductions are possible if future ballot measures are proposed and passed. Some advocacy groups have discussed pushing for a 4.25% or even 4.0% rate. Whether these efforts materialize depends on the political space and state revenue trends. For now, 4.4% appears stable through at least 2026.

FAMLI Colorado's Newest Paycheck Deduction

The biggest change to Colorado paychecks in recent years is the FAMLI (Family and Medical Leave Insurance) program, which began deducting premiums from paychecks on January 1, 2024, with benefits becoming available starting January 1, 2024. Here's what I've found through our testing and research:

The total FAMLI premium is 0.9% of wages, split equally between employer (0.45%) and employee (0.45%). For a $75,000 salary, that's $337.50 per year from the employee's paycheck - about $13 per biweekly paycheck. While not a massive deduction, it's a new line item that didn't exist before 2024.

In return, FAMLI provides up to 12 weeks of paid leave (16 weeks for pregnancy/childbirth complications) for qualifying events including:

  • Birth, adoption, or foster placement
  • Serious health condition: Your own or a family member's
  • Family member's active duty
  • Domestic violence, stalking, or sexual assault situations

FAMLI benefits replace up to 90% of your average weekly wage, subject to a maximum weekly benefit. For 2025, the maximum weekly benefit is approximately $1,100. This means workers earning up to about $63,500 annually receive nearly full wage replacement, while higher earners receive a declining percentage. On a $75,000 salary, FAMLI would provide about $960-$1,000 per week during leave - roughly 70% wage replacement.

Self-employed individuals can opt into FAMLI voluntarily. There's no opting out for employees of covered employers (10+ employees), though some private plans can substitute if they meet or exceed FAMLI's benefits. I've found that many Colorado workers don't fully understand this new deduction on their paychecks, which is one reason I it into this calculator.

The No-Local-Income-Tax Advantage

One of Colorado's most significant tax advantages is the complete absence of local or city income taxes. This is a bigger deal than most people realize. Compare Colorado to these states with local income taxes:

  • Most cities levy 2.0-2.75% municipal income tax (Columbus: 2.5%, Cleveland: 2.5%)
  • Local earned income tax up to 3.9% (Philadelphia: 3.75%)
  • NYC income tax adds 3.078-3.876% on top of state tax
  • County income taxes range from 0.5% to 3.38%
  • Detroit charges 2.4% city income tax

For a $75,000 earner, the absence of local income tax saves $1,125 to $2,900 annually compared to working in a major city in one of these states. In Denver, despite the high cost of living, you pay zero local income tax. This means Colorado's effective state-level income tax burden is often lower than states with nominally lower state rates but mandatory local taxes. I've found this to be the single most underappreciated aspect of Colorado's tax structure.

Your Take-Home Pay in Colorado

Based on our analysis and original research, here are the most effective strategies for Colorado workers:

1. pre-tax retirement contributions. In Colorado, every dollar contributed to a traditional 401(k) reduces both federal and state taxes. At a combined marginal rate of approximately 26.4% (22% federal + 4.4% Colorado), a $23,000 contribution saves roughly $6,072 in taxes. If your employer matches, the total value proposition is even stronger.

2. Use an HSA aggressively. Colorado fully conforms to federal HSA rules, making it one of the best tax-advantaged accounts available. The 2025 limits are $4,150 (self-only) and $8,300 (family). The triple tax benefit (pre-tax contributions, tax-free growth, tax-free qualified withdrawals) is fully honored by Colorado.

3. Consider the cost-of-living map when choosing where to live. Unlike states with local income taxes where your city choice directly affects your tax rate, in Colorado your city choice only affects cost of living - but the impact is enormous. The same $75,000 salary buys you a completely different lifestyle in Colorado Springs versus Boulder. If you can work remotely or have geographic flexibility, living in a lower-cost Colorado city dramatically increases your effective purchasing power.

4. Understand the Colorado SALT cap workaround. Colorado allows a subtraction from federal taxable income for certain taxes that were limited by the $10,000 SALT cap at the federal level. If you pay significant state and local taxes (particularly property taxes), this Colorado-specific subtraction can reduce your state tax liability. Check DR 104 Schedule for details.

5. Take advantage of Colorado 529 deductions. Contributions to any state's 529 plan (not just Colorado's) are deductible on the Colorado return up to $20,000 per taxpayer per year ($40,000 for married filing jointly). This is one of the most generous 529 deductions in the country and can save 4.4% of your contribution in state tax. On a $20,000 contribution, that's $880 in Colorado tax savings.

Colorado Payroll Taxes vs. Other Mountain West States

Colorado's position in the Mountain West region is interesting from a tax perspective. It's sandwiched between no-tax states (Wyoming to the north, no income tax on wages in Nevada and Washington) and higher-tax states (New Mexico at 5.9%, Montana at 6.75%). Here's my analysis of the trade-offs:

Moving from Colorado to Wyoming saves you 4.4% in state income tax - on $75,000, that's $2,640 annually. But Wyoming's economy is much smaller, with fewer job opportunities in most sectors. The salary differential alone may not offset the tax savings. Colorado's median household income ($87,598) is significantly higher than Wyoming's ($68,002). If moving to Wyoming means accepting a lower salary, the tax savings evaporate quickly.

Conversely, moving from California to Colorado can yield dramatic savings. California's top marginal rate of 13.3% (effective rate of about 4.5% at $75,000) is comparable to Colorado's 4.4%, but at higher income levels the gap widens enormously. A $200,000 earner pays approximately $13,500 in California state tax versus $8,150 in Colorado - a $5,350 annual difference. Combined with lower housing costs in most Colorado cities versus comparable California metros, the financial case for Colorado over California is compelling for higher earners.

The Cost of Living Reality Check

I've found that the cost of living variance within Colorado is one of the most important factors that salary calculators typically ignore. Let me put it in concrete terms. A $75,000 salary in Denver, after all taxes and FAMLI, yields approximately $57,000-$58,000 in take-home pay. In Denver, where the median rent for a one-bedroom apartment is around $1,800/month ($21,600/year), housing alone consumes about 37% of your after-tax income. That leaves roughly $36,000 for everything else.

That same $75,000 salary in Pueblo yields the same take-home pay (since Colorado has no local income tax), but the median one-bedroom rent is around $1,000/month ($12,000/year) - consuming only 21% of after-tax income. That leaves about $46,000 for everything else - $10,000 more in disposable income per year, or roughly $833 more per month.

Colorado Springs offers a middle ground: comparable take-home pay with a median one-bedroom rent of about $1,350/month. Fort Collins and Boulder are closer to Denver pricing. your real financial wellbeing in Colorado depends as much on which city you choose as it does on your gross salary. This is why I've integrated cost-of-living comparisons directly into this calculator's output.

Colorado-Specific Tax Credits and Deductions

Colorado offers several state-specific tax benefits worth knowing about. I've compiled the most impactful ones from our research:

  • Child Care Colorado allows a credit of 50% of contributions to qualified child care organizations, up to $100,000. This is one of the most generous child care credits in any state.
  • Colorado 529 Deduct up to $20,000 per taxpayer ($40,000 MFJ) for contributions to any 529 plan. No requirement to use Colorado's own plan.
  • Renewable Colorado provides credits for residential solar energy and other renewable energy installations. Credits vary by year and installation type.
  • Colorado Earned Colorado provides a refundable earned income tax credit equal to 25% of the federal EITC. This benefits lower-income workers significantly.
  • Retirement Taxpayers age 55-64 can exclude up to $20,000 of pension/annuity income. Those 65+ can exclude up to $24,000. Social Security is fully excluded for those 65+.
  • Colorado allows a subtraction for state and local taxes paid that exceed the $10,000 federal SALT cap, providing relief for high-property-tax payers.

The Flat Tax Advantage Simplicity and Predictability

Colorado's flat tax system offers a benefit beyond just the rate itself: predictability. In a graduated tax state, calculating the impact of a raise, bonus, or additional income requires navigating bracket thresholds. In Colorado, the math is always straightforward - every additional dollar of income above the standard deduction is taxed at exactly 4.4%. Period.

This simplicity has practical implications for financial planning. If your employer offers a $5,000 bonus, you can instantly calculate the $5,000 x 4.4% = $220. If you're considering contributing more to your 401(k), every additional $1,000 saves exactly $44 in Colorado tax. There's no bracket-jumping, no phase-out calculations, no AMT (alternative minimum tax) interactions at the state level. I've found this predictability to be one of Colorado's underappreciated advantages for personal financial planning.

Remote Work and Colorado's Tax Advantage

The remote work revolution has highlighted Colorado's tax competitiveness. If you work remotely for a company based in a high-tax state (like California or New York), your tax liability is determined by where you live, not where your employer is headquartered. A software engineer earning $150,000 who works remotely from Colorado pays 4.4% state tax instead of California's effective rate of approximately 7-8% at that income level.

, Colorado's lack of local income tax means remote workers in Colorado don't face the double-taxation issues that affect remote workers in states like Ohio or Pennsylvania, where both your home city and work city may claim taxing rights. In Colorado, there's one income tax - period. This clean structure makes Colorado particularly attractive for remote workers.

Colorado's TABOR The Taxpayer's Bill of Rights

Colorado's Taxpayer's Bill of Rights (TABOR), passed in 1992, is a constitutional amendment that limits how much state revenue can grow each year. When revenue exceeds the TABOR limit, the excess must be refunded to taxpayers. This mechanism has led to the series of rate reductions (Propositions 116 and 121) that brought the rate from 4.63% to 4.4%.

TABOR is both praised and criticized. Supporters say it keeps taxes low and government accountable. Critics argue it constrains state spending, particularly on education and infrastructure. Regardless of your political perspective, the practical effect for salary calculation purposes is clear: TABOR creates downward pressure on the income tax rate over time. If state revenue continues to outpace TABOR limits, future ballot measures could reduce the rate further.

Understanding TABOR also explains Colorado's TABOR refunds - periodic payments or credits sent to taxpayers when revenue exceeds the limit. These refunds are separate from your normal tax liability and are not reflected in this calculator, but they represent additional money back in your pocket in eligible years.

Colorado Paycheck Timing and Frequency

Colorado law requires employers to pay employees at least monthly, with specific requirements for different pay frequencies. The state also mandates that final paychecks be paid immediately upon termination (or within 6 hours for involuntary termination during business hours). These protections are among the strongest in the nation.

For paycheck planning purposes, the flat tax makes Colorado unusually predictable across pay frequencies. paid weekly, biweekly, semi-monthly, or monthly, the per-period tax calculation is simply your per-period taxable income multiplied by 4.4%. There are no bracket-jumping surprises from receiving a larger monthly paycheck versus smaller weekly ones.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Colorado state income tax rate for 2025?
Colorado has a flat income tax rate of 4.4% for 2025. This was reduced from 4.55% by Proposition 121, which voters approved in November 2022. The flat rate applies to all Colorado taxable income, which starts from your federal taxable income with certain Colorado-specific adjustments. Because Colorado ties to the federal return, the federal standard deduction effectively serves as your Colorado standard deduction.
Does Colorado have local or city income taxes?
No, Colorado has no local or city income taxes anywhere in the state. This is a significant advantage compared to states like Ohio, Pennsylvania, and New York where municipal income taxes can add 1-3% to your total burden. In Colorado, you only pay the flat 4.4% state income tax regardless of which city you live or work in. This makes paycheck calculations simpler and more predictable.
What is FAMLI and how does it affect my paycheck?
FAMLI (Family and Medical Leave Insurance) is Colorado's paid family and medical leave program. Employees pay 0.45% of their wages as a FAMLI premium (employers pay a matching 0.45%). On a $75,000 salary, the employee share is $337.50 per year. In return, FAMLI provides up to 12 weeks of paid leave for qualifying events including new child, serious health condition, military deployment, or domestic violence situations.
How does Colorado's cost of living compare across cities?
Colorado's cost of living varies significantly. Denver (COL index ~121) and Boulder (~133) are the most expensive, primarily due to housing. Colorado Springs (~102) is near the national average. Fort Collins (~112) and Aurora (~115) are moderate. Pueblo (~91) and Grand Junction (~94) are the most affordable. The same salary buys vastly different lifestyles depending on which city you choose.
How does Colorado compare to Utah and Arizona for taxes?
Colorado's flat 4.4% rate falls between Arizona's flat 2.5% and Utah's flat 4.65%. None of these states have local income taxes. On a $75,000 salary, you'd pay approximately $1,510 in Arizona, $2,640 in Colorado, and $2,810 in Utah for state income tax. Arizona is the most tax-friendly, but its job market is more limited in certain sectors compared to Colorado's strong tech and aerospace industries.
What pre-tax deductions reduce my Colorado taxes?
Because Colorado taxable income starts from federal taxable income, any pre-tax deduction that reduces your federal taxes also reduces your Colorado taxes. This includes 401(k) contributions (up to $23,000 in 2025), HSA contributions ($4,150 single/$8,300 family), health insurance premiums, and FSA contributions ($3,200). At the 4.4% flat rate, every $1,000 in pre-tax deductions saves exactly $44 in Colorado state tax.
Does Colorado tax Social Security benefits?
It depends on your age. Taxpayers 65 and older can fully exclude Social Security benefits from Colorado taxable income. Those aged 55-64 receive a partial exclusion. Younger taxpayers must include Social Security benefits to the same extent as on their federal return. Colorado has been gradually expanding this exclusion, making the state increasingly attractive for retirees.
