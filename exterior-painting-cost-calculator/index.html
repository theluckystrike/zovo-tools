Understanding Exterior Painting Costs

Painting the exterior of a house is one of the most cost-effective ways to protect your home from weather damage and increase its curb appeal. The total cost depends on four primary factors: the size of the paintable area, the quality of paint and primer selected, whether you hire professionals or do it yourself, and the condition of the existing surface.

The national average for a professional exterior paint job on a two-story home ranges from $5,000 to $12,000. Single-story homes typically fall between $3,000 and $7,000. These figures include labor, materials, standard surface preparation, and two coats of paint. Homes with significant repair needs, difficult access, or premium paint selections will exceed these averages.

I find that most homeowners underestimate the amount of surface preparation required before any paint goes on the wall. A thorough prep job includes pressure washing, scraping loose paint, sanding rough areas, filling holes and cracks with exterior-grade caulk, and priming bare or stained surfaces. Prep work accounts for 30% to 50% of a professional painter's total labor hours, and skipping it is the most common reason paint jobs fail prematurely.

How Paintable Square Footage Is Calculated

The calculator above uses a straightforward method: the perimeter of your house (2 x length + 2 x width) multiplied by the wall height per story, multiplied by the number of stories. Windows (approximately 15 square feet each) and doors (approximately 20 square feet each) are subtracted from the total since those areas are not painted as part of the siding.

Paintable Area = (Perimeter x Wall Height x Stories) - (Windows x 15 sqft) - (Doors x 20 sqft)

This formula works for rectangular homes. If your home has an L-shape, T-shape, or other complex footprint, measure each wall section individually and add them together. Gable ends on homes with pitched roofs add additional area that this basic calculation may underestimate by 10% to 20%. For the most accurate estimate, measure each exterior wall from ground to roofline.

Exterior Paint Types and Quality Levels

Exterior paint comes in three broad quality tiers, and the differences in performance between tiers are significant enough to affect how often you repaint and how well the paint protects the underlying surface.

Economy Paint ($25 to $35 per gallon)

Economy exterior paints use lower concentrations of pigment and resin (the binder that holds pigment to the surface). They cover adequately in two coats but tend to fade faster, chalk more quickly in direct sunlight, and have shorter lifespans of 3 to 5 years. These paints work for rental properties, outbuildings, or situations where the budget is the primary constraint.

Standard Paint ($35 to $50 per gallon)

Standard-grade paints from major manufacturers (Sherwin-Williams SuperPaint, Benjamin Moore Regal, Behr Premium Plus Ultra) offer a good balance of durability, coverage, and cost. Higher resin content provides better adhesion and flexibility, and improved UV stabilizers extend fade resistance. Expected lifespan is 7 to 10 years with proper surface preparation. This is the tier I recommend for most homeowner projects.

Premium Paint ($50 to $70 per gallon)

Premium paints (Sherwin-Williams Duration, Benjamin Moore Aura, PPG Manor Hall) contain the highest quality resins, maximum pigment loading, and advanced additives for mildew resistance, self-priming capability, and extreme weather durability. Some premium paints offer legitimate one-coat coverage on previously painted surfaces. Lifespan extends to 10 to 15 years. The higher per-gallon cost is offset by potentially needing fewer coats and longer intervals between repainting.

Primer Selection

Exterior primer serves a different function than finish paint. Its job is to bond to the surface and provide a uniform base for the topcoat to adhere to. Use an oil-based primer (Kilz Original, Zinsser Cover Stain) on bare wood, over stains, and over chalky surfaces. Use a latex primer (Kilz 2, Zinsser Bulls Eye 123) on previously painted surfaces in good condition and over drywall repairs. Primer costs approximately $20 to $35 per gallon and covers roughly 350 square feet per gallon.

Surface Preparation Before Painting

Surface preparation is the single most important factor in determining how long a paint job lasts. A $70-per-gallon premium paint applied over poorly prepared surfaces will fail faster than a $30-per-gallon economy paint on a properly prepped surface. Professional painters know this, which is why prep work consumes the largest portion of their labor hours.

Pressure Washing

Start with a thorough pressure washing of the entire exterior. Use 1,500 to 2,500 PSI for wood siding and 2,500 to 3,000 PSI for masonry and stucco. Add a house wash detergent to remove mildew, algae, dirt, and chalking. Allow the surface to dry completely (usually 24 to 48 hours) before proceeding with repairs or painting. Pressure washing alone costs $200 to $500 if you hire it out, or $50 to $100 per day for equipment rental.

Scraping and Sanding

All loose, peeling, or flaking paint must be removed. A paint scraper, 5-in-1 tool, or oscillating multi-tool handles most scraping work. After scraping, sand the edges where old paint meets bare surface to create a smooth transition. Wire brushes work well on textured surfaces like stucco. On homes with paint applied before 1978, test for lead paint before scraping and follow EPA RRP (Renovation, Repair, and Painting) guidelines if lead is present.

Caulking and Repairs

Inspect all joints where siding meets trim, around windows and doors, and at corner boards. Remove old cracked caulk and apply fresh exterior-grade acrylic latex caulk (not silicone, as silicone does not accept paint). Fill nail holes and small cracks with exterior wood filler. Replace any rotted wood sections before painting. This is the step most DIY painters skip or rush, and it shows within a year when gaps open up and moisture infiltrates behind the paint film.

Lead Paint Considerations

Homes built before 1978 may have lead-based paint on exterior surfaces. Federal law requires EPA-certified contractors for renovation work that disturbs lead paint. Lead paint testing kits cost $10 to $30 for DIY swab tests, or you can hire a certified inspector for $200 to $400. If lead is present, containment measures, specialized removal techniques, and proper disposal are required, adding $2,000 to $5,000 to the project cost.

Professional vs. DIY Labor Costs

The labor component typically represents 60% to 70% of a professional exterior painting estimate. Understanding what drives labor costs helps you evaluate bids and decide whether DIY is practical for your situation.

Professional Painter Rates

Professional exterior painters charge by the square foot, by the hour, or by the project. Square-foot rates range from $1.50 to $4.00 for paintable wall area, with $2.00 to $2.50 being the most common range for standard prep and two coats. Hourly rates range from $25 to $75 per painter per hour. Most professionals prefer quoting per project after an on-site estimate, which accounts for the specific challenges of each home.

Factors that increase labor costs include multi-story homes requiring ladders or scaffolding (add 20% to 40%), extensive scraping and repair work, complex architectural details like Victorian trim, difficult access due to landscaping or steep terrain, and lead paint abatement requirements. Geographic location also matters significantly: painters in San Francisco or New York charge two to three times what painters in rural Mississippi charge.

DIY Cost Savings

Painting your own house exterior saves $3,000 to $8,000 in labor costs on an average home. Your costs include paint, primer, brushes, rollers, extension poles, painter's tape, drop cloths, caulk, a ladder or scaffolding rental, and a pressure washer rental. Budget $500 to $2,000 for a complete DIY materials and rental setup.

The trade-off is time and risk. A professional crew of 2 to 3 painters finishes an average home in 3 to 5 days. A homeowner working alone or with a helper should expect 2 to 4 weekends. Multi-story homes introduce fall risk, and improper prep work leads to premature paint failure.

Regional Cost Variations

Labor rates for exterior painting vary substantially by region. The Northeast and West Coast are the most expensive markets ($2.50 to $4.00 per square foot), followed by the Midwest ($2.00 to $3.00), the Southeast ($1.50 to $2.50), and rural areas ($1.25 to $2.00). These variations reflect local cost of living, labor supply, and seasonal demand patterns.

Factors That Affect Exterior Painting Cost

Home Size and Stories

The most obvious cost driver is the total paintable area. A 1,200 square foot ranch-style home has roughly 1,500 square feet of exterior wall area. A 2,400 square foot two-story colonial has approximately 2,800 square feet of wall area. More area means more paint, more labor hours, and higher total cost. Multi-story homes also require more expensive equipment access.

Current Surface Condition

A previously painted surface in good condition (minimal peeling, no bare spots, no wood rot) requires minimal prep and accepts new paint readily. A surface with extensive peeling, bare wood, wood rot, or multiple failing paint layers requires substantial prep that can double the labor hours. Severely deteriorated surfaces may require chemical stripping ($3 to $8 per square foot) before repainting.

Siding Material

Different siding materials have different painting requirements. Wood clapboard is the most common and straightforward to paint. Cedar shingles require more paint per square foot due to their textured surface (budget for 250 to 300 sqft per gallon instead of 350). Stucco absorbs more paint and may require elastomeric coating ($40 to $80 per gallon) instead of standard exterior paint. Brick and masonry require specialized masonry paint and often a block filler primer.

Color Change

Painting the same color or a similar shade requires fewer coats than a dramatic color change. Going from dark to light typically requires one coat of tinted primer plus two coats of finish paint (three total coats). Staying in the same color family often allows two coats of finish paint without a separate primer coat.

Trim and Detail Work

Window frames, door frames, fascia boards, soffits, shutters, and decorative trim all require additional time and often a different paint type (semi-gloss or gloss for trim versus flat or satin for siding). Homes with extensive trim work, multiple colors, or ornate architectural details add 20% to 50% to the labor cost compared to a simple one-color siding job.

When to Hire a Professional Painter

I recommend hiring professional painters in several specific situations where the investment clearly pays for itself in quality, safety, or time savings.

Homes over two stories present genuine safety risks. Working on extension ladders at heights above 20 feet requires training, proper equipment, and ideally a partner. Falls from ladders are one of the most common home improvement injuries, and multi-story exterior painting is one of the highest-risk DIY activities.

Homes with lead paint require certified handling. DIY lead paint disturbance without proper containment violates federal law and creates health hazards for your family and neighbors. Certified lead-safe painters know containment procedures, proper removal techniques, and disposal requirements.

Extensive wood rot or structural damage should be addressed before painting. Professional painters either handle these repairs in-house or coordinate with carpenters. Painting over rotted wood without repairing it wastes money because the paint will fail within months as the rot continues to spread.

If your time has a high opportunity cost and you would rather spend weekends with family than on a ladder, the $3,000 to $8,000 in labor savings may not justify the time investment. A typical DIY exterior paint project consumes 80 to 160 hours of work spread over multiple weekends.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to paint the exterior of a house?
The average cost ranges from $3,000 to $7,000 for a single-story home and $5,000 to $12,000 for a two-story home when hiring professionals. DIY projects typically cost $500 to $2,000 in materials. The total depends on home size, paint quality, surface condition, and local labor rates.
How many gallons of paint do I need for my house exterior?
One gallon covers approximately 350 square feet per coat on smooth surfaces. Calculate your paintable area (perimeter times height, minus windows and doors), divide by 350, and multiply by the number of coats. A typical 2,000 square foot paintable area with two coats needs about 12 gallons of paint plus 6 gallons of primer.
Do I need primer for exterior painting?
Primer is recommended when painting bare wood, covering dark colors with lighter ones, painting over stains, switching from oil-based to latex, or painting chalky surfaces. Repainting the same color over a surface in good condition may allow you to skip primer, though a primer coat always improves adhesion and coverage.
How long does exterior paint last?
Quality exterior paint lasts 7 to 10 years on wood siding, 5 to 7 years on stucco, and 15 to 20 years on properly painted aluminum or vinyl siding. Premium paints with higher resin content tend to last longer. Surface preparation quality and sun exposure are the biggest factors in paint longevity.
What is the best time of year to paint a house exterior?
Late spring through early fall provides the best conditions. Ideal temperatures range from 50 to 85 degrees Fahrenheit with low humidity and no rain forecast for 24 to 48 hours. Avoid painting in direct afternoon sun, which causes the paint to dry too quickly and can lead to blistering and poor adhesion.
Should I paint my house myself or hire a professional?
DIY saves 60% to 70% of total cost by eliminating labor. However, professionals deliver faster completion, better prep, proper equipment for multi-story homes, and warranty coverage. Hire pros for homes over two stories, homes with lead paint, or when significant repairs are needed before painting.
How much does a professional painter charge per square foot?
Professional rates typically range from $1.50 to $4.00 per square foot of paintable area, with the national average around $2.50. This includes labor, standard prep, and two coats. Rates vary by region, with coastal and urban areas at the higher end.
What type of paint is best for house exteriors?
100% acrylic latex paint is the best choice for most exterior surfaces. It offers excellent adhesion, flexibility to expand and contract with temperature changes, UV resistance, mildew resistance, and easy cleanup. Use semi-gloss or gloss finishes for trim areas that need extra durability and moisture resistance.
How do I calculate the paintable square footage of my house?
Measure the perimeter and multiply by wall height per story times the number of stories. Subtract approximately 15 square feet per window and 20 square feet per door. For a 50x30 foot house with 9-foot walls, two stories, 12 windows, and 3 doors: (160 x 9 x 2) - (12 x 15) - (3 x 20) = 2,880 - 180 - 60 = 2,640 square feet.
What is included in a professional painting estimate?
A thorough estimate includes pressure washing, scraping loose paint, sanding, caulking gaps, priming bare spots, two coats of finish paint, masking non-painted surfaces, and cleanup. Some estimates include minor wood repair. Always get itemized estimates from at least three painters to compare scope and pricing.
Does the color of paint affect the cost?
Deep and vivid colors cost $5 to $15 more per gallon because they require more pigment. Changing from a dark to a light color may require an extra primer coat and paint coat, increasing both material and labor costs. Staying within the same color family minimizes additional expense.
How much does surface preparation cost?
Surface prep accounts for 30% to 50% of professional labor time. Pressure washing runs $200 to $500. Scraping and sanding add $0.50 to $1.50 per square foot for badly deteriorated surfaces. Minor wood repairs average $50 to $200 per area. Proper prep is the biggest factor in how long the paint job lasts.
