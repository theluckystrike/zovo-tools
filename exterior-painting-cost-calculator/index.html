Understanding Exterior Painting Costs

Painting the exterior of a house is one of the most cost-effective ways to protect your home from weather damage and increase its curb appeal. The total cost depends on four primary factors: the size of the paintable area, the quality of paint and primer selected, whether you hire professionals or do it yourself, and the condition of the existing surface.

The national average for a professional exterior paint job on a two-story home ranges from $5,000 to $12,000. Single-story homes typically fall between $3,000 and $7,000. These figures include labor, materials, standard surface preparation, and two coats of paint. Homes with significant repair needs, difficult access, or premium paint selections will exceed these averages.

I find that most homeowners underestimate the amount of surface preparation required before any paint goes on the wall. A thorough prep job includes pressure washing, scraping loose paint, sanding rough areas, filling holes and cracks with exterior-grade caulk, and priming bare or stained surfaces. Prep work accounts for 30% to 50% of a professional painter's total labor hours, and skipping it is the most common reason paint jobs fail prematurely.

How Paintable Square Footage Is Calculated

The calculator above uses a straightforward method: the perimeter of your house (2 x length + 2 x width) multiplied by the wall height per story, multiplied by the number of stories. Windows (approximately 15 square feet each) and doors (approximately 20 square feet each) are subtracted from the total since those areas are not painted as part of the siding.

Paintable Area = (Perimeter x Wall Height x Stories) - (Windows x 15 sqft) - (Doors x 20 sqft)

This formula works for rectangular homes. If your home has an L-shape, T-shape, or other complex footprint, measure each wall section individually and add them together. Gable ends on homes with pitched roofs add additional area that this basic calculation may underestimate by 10% to 20%. For the most precise estimate, measure each exterior wall from ground to roofline.

Exterior Paint Types and Quality Levels

Exterior paint comes in three broad quality tiers, and the differences in performance between tiers are significant enough to affect how often you repaint and how well the paint protects the underlying surface.

Economy Paint ($25 to $35 per gallon)

Economy exterior paints use lower concentrations of pigment and resin (the binder that holds pigment to the surface). They cover adequately in two coats but tend to fade faster, chalk more quickly in direct sunlight, and have shorter lifespans of 3 to 5 years. These paints work for rental properties, outbuildings, or situations where the budget is the primary constraint.

Standard Paint ($35 to $50 per gallon)

Standard-grade paints from major manufacturers (Sherwin-Williams SuperPaint, Benjamin Moore Regal, Behr Premium Plus Ultra) offer a good balance of durability, coverage, and cost. Higher resin content provides better adhesion and flexibility, and improved UV stabilizers extend fade resistance. Expected lifespan is 7 to 10 years with proper surface preparation. This is the tier I recommend for most homeowner projects.

Premium Paint ($50 to $70 per gallon)

Premium paints (Sherwin-Williams Duration, Benjamin Moore Aura, PPG Manor Hall) contain the highest quality resins, maximum pigment loading, and modern additives for mildew resistance, self-priming capability, and extreme weather durability. Some premium paints offer legitimate one-coat coverage on previously painted surfaces. Lifespan extends to 10 to 15 years. The higher per-gallon cost is offset by potentially needing fewer coats and longer intervals between repainting.

Primer Selection

Exterior primer serves a different function than finish paint. Its job is to bond to the surface and provide a uniform base for the topcoat to adhere to. Use an oil-based primer (Kilz Original, Zinsser Cover Stain) on bare wood, over stains, and over chalky surfaces. Use a latex primer (Kilz 2, Zinsser Bulls Eye 123) on previously painted surfaces in good condition and over drywall repairs. Primer costs approximately $20 to $35 per gallon and covers roughly 350 square feet per gallon.

Surface Preparation Before Painting

Surface preparation is the single most important factor in determining how long a paint job lasts. A $70-per-gallon premium paint applied over poorly prepared surfaces will fail faster than a $30-per-gallon economy paint on a properly prepped surface. Professional painters know this, which is why prep work consumes the largest portion of their labor hours.

Pressure Washing

Start with a thorough pressure washing of the entire exterior. Use 1,500 to 2,500 PSI for wood siding and 2,500 to 3,000 PSI for masonry and stucco. Add a house wash detergent to remove mildew, algae, dirt, and chalking. Allow the surface to dry completely (usually 24 to 48 hours) before proceeding with repairs or painting. Pressure washing alone costs $200 to $500 if you hire it out, or $50 to $100 per day for equipment rental.

Scraping and Sanding

All loose, peeling, or flaking paint must be removed. A paint scraper, 5-in-1 tool, or oscillating multi-tool handles most scraping work. After scraping, sand the edges where old paint meets bare surface to create a smooth transition. Wire brushes work well on textured surfaces like stucco. On homes with paint applied before 1978, test for lead paint before scraping and follow EPA RRP (Renovation, Repair, and Painting) guidelines if lead is present.

Caulking and Repairs

Inspect all joints where siding meets trim, around windows and doors, and at corner boards. Remove old cracked caulk and apply fresh exterior-grade acrylic latex caulk (not silicone, as silicone does not accept paint). Fill nail holes and small cracks with exterior wood filler. Replace any rotted wood sections before painting. This is the step most DIY painters skip or rush, and it shows within a year when gaps open up and moisture infiltrates behind the paint film.

Lead Paint Considerations

Homes built before 1978 may have lead-based paint on exterior surfaces. Federal law requires EPA-certified contractors for renovation work that disturbs lead paint. Lead paint testing kits cost $10 to $30 for DIY swab tests, or you can hire a certified inspector for $200 to $400. If lead is present, containment measures, specialized removal techniques, and proper disposal are required, adding $2,000 to $5,000 to the project cost.

Professional vs. DIY Labor Costs

The labor component typically represents 60% to 70% of a professional exterior painting estimate. Understanding what drives labor costs helps you evaluate bids and decide whether DIY is practical for your situation.

Professional Painter Rates

Professional exterior painters charge by the square foot, by the hour, or by the project. Square-foot rates range from $1.50 to $4.00 for paintable wall area, with $2.00 to $2.50 being the most common range for standard prep and two coats. Hourly rates range from $25 to $75 per painter per hour. Most professionals prefer quoting per project after an on-site estimate, which accounts for the specific challenges of each home.

Factors that increase labor costs include multi-story homes requiring ladders or scaffolding (add 20% to 40%), extensive scraping and repair work, complex architectural details like Victorian trim, difficult access due to landscaping or steep terrain, and lead paint abatement requirements. Geographic location also matters significantly: painters in San Francisco or New York charge two to three times what painters in rural Mississippi charge.

DIY Cost Savings

Painting your own house exterior saves $3,000 to $8,000 in labor costs on an average home. Your costs include paint, primer, brushes, rollers, extension poles, painter's tape, drop cloths, caulk, a ladder or scaffolding rental, and a pressure washer rental. Budget $500 to $2,000 for a complete DIY materials and rental setup.

The trade-off is time and risk. A professional crew of 2 to 3 painters finishes an average home in 3 to 5 days. A homeowner working alone or with a helper should expect 2 to 4 weekends. Multi-story homes introduce fall risk, and improper prep work leads to premature paint failure.

Regional Cost Variations

Labor rates for exterior painting vary substantially by region. The Northeast and West Coast are the most expensive markets ($2.50 to $4.00 per square foot), followed by the Midwest ($2.00 to $3.00), the Southeast ($1.50 to $2.50), and rural areas ($1.25 to $2.00). These variations reflect local cost of living, labor supply, and seasonal demand patterns.

Factors That Affect Exterior Painting Cost

Home Size and Stories

The most obvious cost driver is the total paintable area. A 1,200 square foot ranch-style home has roughly 1,500 square feet of exterior wall area. A 2,400 square foot two-story colonial has approximately 2,800 square feet of wall area. More area means more paint, more labor hours, and higher total cost. Multi-story homes also require more expensive equipment access.

Current Surface Condition

A previously painted surface in good condition (minimal peeling, no bare spots, no wood rot) requires minimal prep and accepts new paint readily. A surface with extensive peeling, bare wood, wood rot, or multiple failing paint layers requires substantial prep that can double the labor hours. Severely deteriorated surfaces may require chemical stripping ($3 to $8 per square foot) before repainting.

Siding Material

Different siding materials have different painting requirements. Wood clapboard is the most common and straightforward to paint. Cedar shingles require more paint per square foot due to their textured surface (budget for 250 to 300 sqft per gallon instead of 350). Stucco absorbs more paint and may require elastomeric coating ($40 to $80 per gallon) instead of standard exterior paint. Brick and masonry require specialized masonry paint and often a block filler primer.

Color Change

Painting the same color or a similar shade requires fewer coats than a dramatic color change. Going from dark to light typically requires one coat of tinted primer plus two coats of finish paint (three total coats). Staying in the same color family often allows two coats of finish paint without a separate primer coat.

Trim and Detail Work

Window frames, door frames, fascia boards, soffits, shutters, and decorative trim all require additional time and often a different paint type (semi-gloss or gloss for trim versus flat or satin for siding). Homes with extensive trim work, multiple colors, or ornate architectural details add 20% to 50% to the labor cost compared to a simple one-color siding job.

When to Hire a Professional Painter

I recommend hiring professional painters in several specific situations where the investment clearly pays for itself in quality, safety, or time savings.

Homes over two stories present genuine safety risks. Working on extension ladders at heights above 20 feet requires training, proper equipment, and ideally a partner. Falls from ladders are one of the most common home improvement injuries, and multi-story exterior painting is one of the highest-risk DIY activities.

Homes with lead paint require certified handling. DIY lead paint disturbance without proper containment violates federal law and creates health hazards for your family and neighbors. Certified lead-safe painters know containment procedures, proper removal techniques, and disposal requirements.

Extensive wood rot or structural damage should be addressed before painting. Professional painters either handle these repairs in-house or coordinate with carpenters. Painting over rotted wood without repairing it wastes money because the paint will fail within months as the rot continues to spread.

If your time has a high opportunity cost and you would rather spend weekends with family than on a ladder, the $3,000 to $8,000 in labor savings may not justify the time investment. A typical DIY exterior paint project consumes 80 to 160 hours of work spread over multiple weekends.

Choosing Exterior Paint Colors

Color selection affects more than aesthetics. It influences heat absorption, fade resistance, perceived home size, and resale value. Making a thoughtful color choice before buying paint saves the expense and frustration of repainting because you are unhappy with the result.

Testing Colors Before Committing

Paint colors look dramatically different on a small swatch card than they do on a 2,000 square foot wall surface. Large areas of color appear lighter and more saturated than the same color on a small sample. I always recommend buying quart-sized samples and painting 4-foot by 4-foot test patches on each side of the house (north, south, east, west). Observe the patches at different times of day because sunlight angle changes how the color appears. Morning light is warm and golden, midday light is neutral, and afternoon light is cooler. A color that looks perfect at noon may look washed out in morning light or harsh in afternoon sun.

Color and Heat Absorption

Darker exterior colors absorb significantly more solar heat than lighter colors. A dark navy or black house absorbs up to 90% of solar radiation, while a white or light gray house absorbs only 20% to 30%. In hot climates (Southwest, Southeast), lighter colors reduce cooling costs and place less thermal stress on siding materials. In cold climates (Northeast, Midwest), darker colors can marginally reduce heating costs by absorbing winter sun, though the effect is small compared to insulation quality.

Fade Resistance

Red, blue, and green pigments fade faster than earth tones, whites, and grays. Premium paints with UV-resistant binders slow fading, but no exterior paint is completely immune to UV degradation. South-facing and west-facing walls receive the most direct sun exposure and will show fading first. If you choose a vibrant color, expect to repaint sun-exposed surfaces 2 to 3 years sooner than shaded surfaces. Earth tones (beige, brown, olive, tan) show the least visible fading over time.

Impact on Resale Value

Neutral exterior colors (white, gray, greige, soft blue, sage green) appeal to the broadest range of buyers and consistently produce the highest resale values. A Zillow analysis found that homes with specific exterior colors sold for $5,000 to $7,000 more than comparable homes with unusual colors. If you plan to sell within 5 years, choosing a neutral palette is a financial decision as much as an aesthetic one.

Multi-Color Schemes

Most exteriors use a three-color scheme: a body color (the main siding color), a trim color (window frames, door frames, fascia), and an accent color (front door, shutters). A classic approach is to choose the body color first, then select a trim color that is lighter or darker in the same color family, and finally choose an accent color that provides contrast. Limiting the palette to three colors keeps the appearance cohesive. More than three colors can look busy and disjointed, especially on smaller homes.

Finishing Tips for a Professional-Quality Paint Job

Whether you hire a professional or tackle the project yourself, understanding what separates a mediocre paint job from a professional one helps you evaluate work quality and get better results.

Brush and Roller Technique

For siding, use a high-quality 9-inch roller with a 3/8-inch to 1/2-inch nap for smooth surfaces or 3/4-inch nap for textured surfaces like stucco. Back-brush roller applications by following with a brush to work paint into crevices, nail holes, and wood grain. This produces better adhesion and a more uniform appearance than rolling alone.

For trim, use a 2.5-inch to 3-inch angled sash brush with synthetic (nylon/polyester) bristles for latex paint. Load the brush to about one-third of the bristle length, tap off excess (do not wipe across the can rim), and apply in long smooth strokes following the direction of the wood grain. Feather the edges by lifting the brush at the end of each stroke rather than stopping abruptly.

Cutting In and Masking

Professional painters "cut in" by painting a straight line freehand along edges where different surfaces or colors meet. This skill takes practice but produces cleaner lines than tape in most situations. For DIY painters, blue painter's tape (FrogTape for the sharpest lines) provides dependable edge protection. Press the tape firmly to prevent paint from bleeding underneath, and remove it while the paint is still slightly tacky to avoid peeling.

Maintaining a Wet Edge

The most common visible defect in amateur paint jobs is lap marks, the visible lines where wet paint overlaps dried paint. To prevent lap marks, maintain a "wet edge" by painting in manageable sections and overlapping into the wet area before it dries. Work in the shade side of the house when possible, and avoid painting in direct sun or temperatures above 85 degrees, both of which accelerate drying and increase the risk of lap marks.

Spray Application

Airless sprayers apply paint faster than brush and roller methods and provide a more uniform coat on large flat surfaces. Spraying is particularly effective for siding, large walls, and textured surfaces like stucco. However, spraying requires extensive masking (every surface not being painted must be covered), produces overspray that can drift to neighboring properties, and applies a thinner coat per pass than rolling. Professional painters often spray the main body coat and back-brush for adhesion, then brush all trim work by hand for precision.

Expected Lifespan by Surface Type

The longevity of an exterior paint job varies significantly by surface material. Wood clapboard siding retains paint for 7 to 10 years with quality paint and proper preparation. Cedar shingles may need repainting every 5 to 7 years due to their natural oils that can interfere with adhesion. Stucco surfaces hold paint well (8 to 12 years) but may develop hairline cracks that allow moisture behind the paint film. Aluminum siding holds paint for 15 to 20 years when properly primed. Fiber cement siding (Hardie board) provides the most durable paint surface, often lasting 15+ years because the substrate does not expand, contract, or absorb moisture like wood.

Warranty Considerations

Premium paint brands offer limited warranties on their products, typically guaranteeing against peeling, blistering, and excessive fading for 15 to 25 years. These warranties require proof of proper surface preparation and application, which is another argument for using professional painters who can document their process. Professional painting companies may offer their own labor warranty (typically 2 to 5 years) covering workmanship defects. Always get warranty terms in writing before work begins.

Getting and Comparing Professional Estimates

If you decide to hire a professional painter, getting precise and comparable estimates is important for making an informed decision. The estimate process reveals both the price and the quality of communication, professionalism, and attention to detail you can expect from the contractor.

How Many Estimates to Get

I recommend getting at least three estimates from different painting contractors. This gives you a range of prices and approaches. If all three estimates cluster within 10% to 15% of each other, you have a solid sense of the market rate. If one estimate is dramatically lower or higher than the others, that warrants investigation. A very low estimate may indicate corners will be cut on preparation or materials. A very high estimate may indicate the contractor is busy and pricing to discourage the job, or it may include premium services the others do not.

What a Good Estimate Includes

A professional estimate should be written, detailed, and include the following: a complete scope of work (what will be painted and what will not), surface preparation details (pressure washing, scraping, sanding, caulking, priming), number of coats, paint brand and product name, paint color plan, estimated start and completion dates, payment schedule, warranty terms, and license and insurance documentation. Verbal estimates and single-line-item quotes do not provide enough detail for meaningful comparison.

Questions to Ask Every Painter

  • What brand and line of paint will you use? (Verify it matches the quality level you expect.)
  • How many coats will be applied? (Two is standard; one is insufficient for most situations.)
  • How will you prepare the surface? (The answer should include at minimum pressure washing, scraping, and caulking.)
  • Who does the actual painting? (Some companies subcontract labor, which can affect quality and accountability.)
  • What is your warranty on labor? (2 to 5 years is standard for reputable painters.)
  • Do you carry liability insurance and workers' compensation? (Request certificates.)
  • Can you provide references from recent exterior projects? (Contact at least two.)
  • What happens if weather delays the project? (Clarify the rescheduling process.)

Verifying Contractor Credentials

Check that the painter is licensed in your state (requirements vary by state), carries general liability insurance (at least $500,000 coverage), and carries workers' compensation insurance for their employees. Request certificates of insurance and verify them directly with the insurance company. Check online reviews on Google, Yelp, and the Better Business Bureau. Ask for and contact at least two references from projects completed within the last year.

Common Pricing Traps

Lowball estimates that seem too good to be true usually are. Common cost-cutting tactics include using cheaper paint than specified, applying only one coat instead of two, skipping or minimizing surface preparation, using inexperienced or unlicensed labor, and leaving cleanup for the homeowner. Protect yourself by specifying the paint brand and product in the contract, requiring two coats in writing, and conducting periodic inspections during the project.

Upfront payment demands are another warning sign. Reputable painters may request 10% to 30% of the total as a deposit, with the balance due upon completion. Painters who demand 50% or more upfront, or full payment before starting, may not complete the work satisfactorily because they have already been paid. Payment upon completion gives you the strongest position for addressing any quality issues.

Complete DIY Exterior Painting Checklist

If you decide to tackle the project yourself, this complete checklist ensures you do not miss any critical steps. I recommend printing it and checking off each item as you complete it.

Planning Phase (1 to 2 Weeks Before)

  • Calculate paintable square footage using the calculator above
  • Purchase paint, primer, brushes, rollers, tape, drop cloths, and caulk
  • Rent or borrow a pressure washer, ladders, and scaffolding if needed
  • Test paint colors with quart-size samples on each wall orientation
  • Check the weather forecast and choose a multi-day window with no rain and temperatures between 50 and 85 degrees
  • Notify neighbors if overspray is possible

Preparation Phase (2 to 4 Days)

  • Pressure wash the entire exterior and allow 24 to 48 hours to dry
  • Scrape all loose and peeling paint using a paint scraper and 5-in-1 tool
  • Sand scraped areas to feather edges with 80 to 100 grit sandpaper
  • Fill holes, cracks, and nail holes with exterior wood filler
  • Replace any rotted wood sections
  • Caulk all gaps around windows, doors, and trim joints with exterior acrylic latex caulk
  • Mask windows, doors, light fixtures, and any surfaces not being painted
  • Cover landscaping, walkways, and driveways with drop cloths

Priming Phase (1 Day)

  • Apply primer to all bare wood, repaired areas, and stained surfaces
  • Use oil-based primer on bare wood and stains, latex primer on previously painted surfaces
  • Allow primer to dry according to the manufacturer's instructions (usually 4 to 8 hours)
  • Inspect for missed spots and touch up before proceeding to finish coats

Painting Phase (2 to 4 Days)

  • Start at the top of the house and work down
  • Paint the siding first, then trim, then the front door and accent areas
  • Apply first coat working in the shade when possible to maintain a wet edge
  • Allow first coat to dry completely (4 to 8 hours minimum, overnight is better)
  • Apply second coat, inspecting for thin spots, holidays (missed areas), and lap marks
  • Cut in edges and corners with a brush before rolling large surfaces
  • Paint trim with a high-quality angled sash brush for clean lines

Cleanup Phase (Half Day)

  • Remove all masking tape while paint is still slightly tacky for the cleanest edges
  • Remove drop cloths carefully to avoid spreading paint drips
  • Clean brushes and rollers immediately (soap and water for latex, mineral spirits for oil-based)
  • Store leftover paint properly sealed for future touch-ups
  • Inspect the entire house from ground level and from 20 feet away to identify any missed spots
  • Touch up as needed

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does it cost to paint the exterior of a house?
The average cost ranges from $3,000 to $7,000 for a single-story home and $5,000 to $12,000 for a two-story home when hiring professionals. DIY projects typically cost $500 to $2,000 in materials. The total depends on home size, paint quality, surface condition, and local labor rates.
How many gallons of paint do I need for my house exterior?
One gallon covers approximately 350 square feet per coat on smooth surfaces. Calculate your paintable area (perimeter times height, minus windows and doors), divide by 350, and multiply by the number of coats. A typical 2,000 square foot paintable area with two coats needs about 12 gallons of paint plus 6 gallons of primer.
Do I need primer for exterior painting?
Primer is recommended when painting bare wood, covering dark colors with lighter ones, painting over stains, switching from oil-based to latex, or painting chalky surfaces. Repainting the same color over a surface in good condition may allow you to skip primer, though a primer coat always improves adhesion and coverage.
How long does exterior paint last?
Quality exterior paint lasts 7 to 10 years on wood siding, 5 to 7 years on stucco, and 15 to 20 years on properly painted aluminum or vinyl siding. Premium paints with higher resin content tend to last longer. Surface preparation quality and sun exposure are the biggest factors in paint longevity.
What is the best time of year to paint a house exterior?
Late spring through early fall provides the best conditions. Ideal temperatures range from 50 to 85 degrees Fahrenheit with low humidity and no rain forecast for 24 to 48 hours. Avoid painting in direct afternoon sun, which causes the paint to dry too quickly and can lead to blistering and poor adhesion.
Should I paint my house myself or hire a professional?
DIY saves 60% to 70% of total cost by eliminating labor. However, professionals deliver faster completion, better prep, proper equipment for multi-story homes, and warranty coverage. Hire pros for homes over two stories, homes with lead paint, or when significant repairs are needed before painting.
How much does a professional painter charge per square foot?
Professional rates typically range from $1.50 to $4.00 per square foot of paintable area, with the national average around $2.50. This includes labor, standard prep, and two coats. Rates vary by region, with coastal and urban areas at the higher end.
What type of paint is best for house exteriors?
100% acrylic latex paint is the best choice for most exterior surfaces. It offers excellent adhesion, flexibility to expand and contract with temperature changes, UV resistance, mildew resistance, and easy cleanup. Use semi-gloss or gloss finishes for trim areas that need extra durability and moisture resistance.
How do I calculate the paintable square footage of my house?
Measure the perimeter and multiply by wall height per story times the number of stories. Subtract approximately 15 square feet per window and 20 square feet per door. For a 50x30 foot house with 9-foot walls, two stories, 12 windows, and 3 doors: (160 x 9 x 2) - (12 x 15) - (3 x 20) = 2,880 - 180 - 60 = 2,640 square feet.
What is included in a professional painting estimate?
A thorough estimate includes pressure washing, scraping loose paint, sanding, caulking gaps, priming bare spots, two coats of finish paint, masking non-painted surfaces, and cleanup. Some estimates include minor wood repair. Always get itemized estimates from at least three painters to compare scope and pricing.
Does the color of paint affect the cost?
Deep and vivid colors cost $5 to $15 more per gallon because they require more pigment. Changing from a dark to a light color may require an extra primer coat and paint coat, increasing both material and labor costs. Staying within the same color family minimizes additional expense.
How much does surface preparation cost?
Surface prep accounts for 30% to 50% of professional labor time. Pressure washing runs $200 to $500. Scraping and sanding add $0.50 to $1.50 per square foot for badly deteriorated surfaces. Minor wood repairs average $50 to $200 per area. Proper prep is the biggest factor in how long the paint job lasts.

Safety Considerations for Exterior Painting

Exterior painting involves working at heights, handling chemical products, and operating power equipment. Taking proper safety precautions prevents injuries that range from minor inconveniences to life-threatening falls.

Ladder Safety

Falls from ladders are the leading cause of injury in home improvement projects. Use a ladder rated for your weight plus the weight of your materials (typically 250 to 300 pound capacity for residential users). Place the ladder on firm, level ground. The base of an extension ladder should be one foot away from the wall for every four feet of height. Never stand on the top two rungs. If you need to reach higher than your ladder safely allows, invest in scaffolding rental ($100 to $200 per day) rather than overextending on a ladder.

Chemical Safety

Some paint products, particularly oil-based primers and paint strippers, contain volatile organic compounds that can cause respiratory irritation, dizziness, and long-term health effects with extended exposure. Wear a respirator mask (not a dust mask) rated for organic vapors when using oil-based products. Latex paint is safer but can still irritate skin and eyes. Wear safety glasses when painting overhead, and use gloves when handling caustic products like paint strippers and chemical removers.

Heat and Sun Exposure

Exterior painting often occurs during the warmest months. Working in direct sun for extended periods causes heat exhaustion and dehydration. Take breaks every 30 to 45 minutes in hot weather. Drink water continuously rather than waiting until you feel thirsty. Wear a hat with a brim to shade your face and neck. Schedule the most physically demanding work (pressure washing, scraping) for early morning or late afternoon when temperatures are lower.

Real-World Cost Examples

To make the cost ranges more concrete, here are three common exterior painting scenarios with itemized cost breakdowns.

Small Ranch Home (1,200 sq ft, 1 story)

A single-story ranch with approximate dimensions of 50 feet by 24 feet, 8 windows, 2 doors, and 8-foot walls has roughly 1,068 square feet of paintable area. Using standard paint ($42/gallon) with 2 coats requires 7 gallons of paint and 4 gallons of primer. DIY cost: approximately $520 (paint, primer, and supplies). Professional cost with labor at $2.50/sqft: approximately $3,190. This is the most affordable exterior painting project because single-story homes require no ladders or scaffolding above standard extension ladders.

Two-Story Colonial (2,400 sq ft)

A two-story colonial measuring 40 feet by 30 feet with 9-foot walls per story, 16 windows, and 3 doors has approximately 2,180 square feet of paintable area. Using standard paint with 2 coats requires 13 gallons of paint and 7 gallons of primer. DIY cost: approximately $1,050 (including scaffold rental at $200). Professional cost with labor at $2.75/sqft (higher rate for two stories): approximately $7,045. The second story adds cost because of scaffold setup time, slower work pace at height, and safety requirements.

Large Victorian (3,500+ sq ft, complex trim)

A large Victorian with wrap-around porch, ornate trim, and 3 colors has approximately 3,200 square feet of paintable siding plus 600 square feet of detailed trim work. Using premium paint ($60/gallon) with 2 coats for siding and 2 coats for trim requires 19 gallons of body paint, 8 gallons of trim paint, and 10 gallons of primer. Professional cost with labor at $3.50/sqft (premium rate for detail work): approximately $15,800. Victorian homes are the most expensive to paint because every linear foot of ornate trim must be brushed by hand, and multi-color schemes require careful masking and cutting in at every color transition.

Estimated Savings From DIY

Across all three examples, DIY painting saves 60% to 70% of the total project cost by eliminating labor charges. The small ranch saves approximately $2,670, the colonial saves approximately $5,995, and the Victorian saves approximately $10,400 (though a Victorian is not recommended as a DIY project due to the complexity and height). For a typical two-story home, the labor savings of $6,000 must be weighed against the 80 to 120 hours of personal time the project requires, which works out to $50 to $75 per hour in effective labor savings.

What is the best time of year to paint a house exterior?

Late spring through early fall is ideal, when temperatures are between 50-85F with low humidity and no rain expected for 24-48 hours after application. Avoid painting in direct sunlight or when temperatures drop below 50F overnight. Paint applied in cold or damp conditions will not adhere properly and will peel prematurely.
