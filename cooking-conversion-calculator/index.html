Common Baking Substitutions

When you're missing an ingredient, these tested substitutions can save the recipe.

Buttermilk (1 cup)

1 cup milk + 1 tablespoon white vinegar or lemon juice. Let sit 5 minutes before using.

Self-Rising Flour (1 cup)

1 cup all-purpose flour + 1.5 tsp baking powder + 0.25 tsp salt. Whisk together thoroughly.

Cake Flour (1 cup)

1 cup all-purpose flour minus 2 tablespoons, plus 2 tablespoons cornstarch. Sift twice.

1 Egg

0.25 cup unsweetened applesauce, or 1 mashed ripe banana, or 3 tbsp aquafaba (chickpea water).

Baking Powder (1 tsp)

0.25 tsp baking soda + 0.5 tsp cream of tartar. Use immediately after mixing.

Brown Sugar (1 cup)

1 cup granulated sugar + 1 tablespoon molasses. Mix well until evenly combined.

Heavy Cream (1 cup)

0.75 cup milk + 0.33 cup melted butter. Works for baking, not for whipping.

Honey (1 cup)

1.25 cups sugar + 0.33 cup water. Or use equal amount maple syrup.

Sour Cream (1 cup)

1 cup plain Greek yogurt. Works in baking and as a topping.

Cornstarch (1 tbsp)

2 tbsp all-purpose flour, or 1 tbsp arrowroot powder for clear sauces.

Unsweetened Chocolate (1 oz)

3 tbsp cocoa powder + 1 tbsp butter or oil. Mix before adding to recipe.

Vanilla Extract (1 tsp)

Seeds from half a vanilla bean, or 1 tsp maple extract, or 1 tbsp vanilla-flavored liqueur.

Why Cooking Conversions Matter

Cooking measurement conversions are one of those things that seem simple until you try to do them accurately. The fundamental problem is that recipes from different countries use different measurement systems. American recipes use cups and tablespoons (volume-based), while European and Australian recipes typically use grams and milliliters (weight-based). This creates confusion because the weight of a cup of an ingredient depends entirely on what that ingredient is.

A cup of all-purpose flour weighs about 125 grams, but a cup of granulated sugar weighs 200 grams. A cup of honey weighs 340 grams. A cup of rolled oats weighs only 90 grams. These differences mean that a simple "1 cup = X grams" conversion is meaningless without knowing the ingredient. This calculator accounts for these density differences across more than 30 common cooking ingredients.

Volume vs Weight Measurement

Professional bakers almost universally prefer weight measurements over volume measurements. The reason is consistency. When you measure a cup of flour by scooping directly from the bag, you can easily pack in 150 grams or more. When you spoon it into the cup and level it off, you get closer to 125 grams. That 20% difference can ruin a recipe.

Weight measurements (grams, ounces) eliminate this variability entirely. 125 grams of flour is 125 grams of flour regardless of how you transfer it to the bowl. If you're serious about consistent baking results, investing in a kitchen scale is the single most impactful upgrade you can make.

That said, volume measurements are perfectly fine for most savory cooking where precision matters less. A tablespoon more or less of olive oil in a pasta sauce rarely causes problems. But for baking, where ingredient ratios directly control chemical reactions (gluten development, leavening, moisture balance), weight precision matters significantly.

US Customary vs Metric System

The United States is one of only three countries (along with Myanmar and Liberia) that has not officially adopted the metric system for everyday use. In cooking, this means American recipes use cups, tablespoons, teaspoons, fluid ounces, pints, quarts, and gallons for volume, plus ounces and pounds for weight.

The rest of the world primarily uses milliliters and liters for volume, and grams and kilograms for weight. Australia, the UK, and New Zealand add another layer of complexity: they use metric measurements but often reference "cups" with a slightly different definition. The Australian cup is 250 mL, while the US cup is 236.59 mL (often rounded to 237 mL). This 5% difference can matter in precise baking.

The UK also has different pint and gallon sizes. A UK pint is 568 mL versus the US pint at 473 mL. A UK gallon is 4.546 liters versus the US gallon at 3.785 liters. This converter uses US measurements as the default, which is the standard for most online recipes.

Ingredient Density and Why It Matters

Density is mass per unit volume. In cooking terms, it's how much a given volume of an ingredient weighs. Water has a density of 1 g/mL, which is why 1 cup of water (237 mL) weighs 237 grams. But most cooking ingredients are not water.

Flour is less dense than water because of the air trapped between particles. Sugar is denser because the crystals pack more tightly. Butter is dense because it's a solid fat. Honey is very dense because it has very little air and is a thick liquid. Understanding these differences is the key to accurate conversions.

This calculator's ingredient density database includes values sourced from the USDA Food Data Central, King Arthur Baking, and verified through kitchen scale measurements. The values assume standard measurement technique (spoon-and-level for dry ingredients, eye-level reading for liquids).

Tips for Scaling Recipes

Scaling a recipe is not always as simple as multiplying everything by the same factor. Here are some important considerations:

  • Spices and salt don't scale linearly. When doubling, start with 1.5x the spice amount and adjust to taste.
  • Leavening agents (baking powder, baking soda, yeast) also don't scale linearly. For doubling, use about 1.5x. For tripling, use about 2.5x.
  • Cooking time may need adjustment. A doubled casserole in a larger pan needs more time. Two pans at original size may need the same time but different rack positions.
  • Eggs can be tricky when scaling by odd fractions. If a recipe calls for 1 egg and you're making 1.5x, beat the egg and use half.
  • Pan size matters. Doubling a recipe doesn't mean doubling pan dimensions. Area scales with the square of the dimension, so a 9" round has 77% more area than an 8" round, not just 12.5% more.

Gas Mark Explained

Gas Mark is a temperature scale used in the United Kingdom, Ireland, and some Commonwealth countries for gas ovens. It ranges from 1 (275°F / 140°C) to 10 (500°F / 260°C). The scale is not linear in Gas Mark 1 is 275°F, and each subsequent mark adds 25°F. So Gas Mark 4 is 350°F, Gas Mark 6 is 400°F, and Gas Mark 9 is 475°F.

The formula to convert Gas °F = (Gas Mark x 25) + 250. For Celsius: °C = (Gas Mark x 14) + 121. These are approximations since gas ovens vary, but they're accurate enough for practical cooking.

The Science Behind Altitude Baking Adjustments

At higher altitudes, atmospheric pressure is lower. This affects baking in three main ways: gases expand more easily (so leavened goods rise faster and may collapse), liquids evaporate faster (leading to drier baked goods), and water boils at a lower temperature (so things cook differently).

At 5,000 feet, water boils at about 203°F (95°C) instead of 212°F (100°C). This means moisture evaporates from batter before the structure sets, which is why high-altitude recipes need more liquid, less sugar (which weakens structure when too much is present), less leavening (to slow the rise), and higher oven temperatures (to set the structure before it collapses).

Cities like Denver (5,280 ft), Bogota (8,660 ft), and Mexico City (7,350 ft) all require altitude adjustments for reliable baking results. The adjustments in this tool are based on guidelines from Colorado State University Extension, which has research on high-altitude baking.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q - How many grams are in a cup of flour?

One cup of all-purpose flour weighs approximately 125 grams (4.4 oz). This can vary slightly depending on whether the flour is sifted, spooned, or scooped directly from the bag. Bread flour is slightly heavier at 130g per cup, and cake flour is lighter at 114g per cup.

Q - How do I convert cups to grams for different ingredients?

Each ingredient has a different density, so a cup of sugar (200g) weighs differently than a cup of flour (125g) or butter (227g). Use the Ingredient Converter tab to select your specific ingredient and get an accurate conversion.

Q - What is the difference between fluid ounces and ounces?

Fluid ounces measure volume (liquid capacity), while ounces measure weight. One fluid ounce of water weighs approximately one ounce, but this equivalence does not hold for other liquids or solids. Honey, for example, is about 1.4 ounces per fluid ounce.

Q - How do I convert Fahrenheit to Celsius?

Subtract 32 from the Fahrenheit value, then multiply by 5/9. For example, 350°F = (350-32) x 5/9 = 176.7°C. Gas Mark 4 is approximately 350°F / 180°C. Use the Temperature tab for instant conversions.

Q - How do I scale a recipe for more or fewer servings?

Divide the desired number of servings by the original number, then multiply each ingredient quantity by that factor. The Recipe Scaler tab does this automatically. Note that spices, salt, and leavening agents don't always scale linearly.

Q - What are common baking substitutions?

Common substitutions include: 1 cup buttermilk = 1 cup milk + 1 tablespoon vinegar; 1 cup self-rising flour = 1 cup flour + 1.5 tsp baking powder + 0.25 tsp salt; 1 egg = 0.25 cup applesauce or 1 mashed banana. See the Substitutions tab for more.

Q - Does altitude affect baking?

Yes. At high altitudes (above 3,500 feet / 1,067 meters), lower air pressure causes baked goods to rise faster and lose moisture more quickly. Common adjustments include reducing sugar and leavening, increasing liquid and oven temperature. The Substitutions tab includes a detailed altitude adjustment table.

Q - How many tablespoons are in a cup?

There are 16 tablespoons in 1 US cup. Other useful equivalents: 1 tablespoon = 3 teaspoons, 1 cup = 8 fluid ounces, and 1 cup = approximately 237 milliliters.
