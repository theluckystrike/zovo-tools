How the COL Index Works

The cost of living index uses the national average as a baseline of 100. A city with an index of 120 is 20 percent more expensive than the national average, while a city at 85 is 15 percent cheaper. The index captures five major spending categories, weighted roughly by how much the average household spends on each.

Housing typically makes up the largest share, accounting for about 30 to 35 percent of the overall index. This includes both rental costs and home purchase prices. In high-cost cities like San Francisco and New York, housing alone can push the index above 180. Groceries and transportation each contribute about 15 to 20 percent. Healthcare runs about 10 to 15 percent, and utilities fill in the remainder.

It is important to understand that the index measures averages. Your personal cost of living depends heavily on your specific circumstances. If you own your home outright, the housing component matters less. If you don't own a car, transportation costs shift to public transit or rideshare expenses. Think of the index as a starting point, not a definitive answer.

Category Breakdown Explained

Housing

Housing is almost always the biggest driver of cost-of-living differences between cities. Median rent for a one-bedroom apartment ranges from about $900 in cities like Indianapolis and Columbus to over $3,500 in San Francisco and Manhattan. Home purchase prices show even wider gaps. The ratio between local income and local housing cost is what matters most for quality of life.

Groceries

Grocery prices vary less dramatically than housing but still differ meaningfully. A basket of standard items costs roughly 15 to 25 percent more in coastal cities compared to the national average. This reflects higher commercial rents, labor costs, and in some cases, longer supply chains. Farmers markets and discount grocers can help offset these differences.

Transportation

Transportation costs include car ownership, insurance, gas, parking, and public transit. Cities with good public transit like New York and Chicago can actually be cheaper for transportation if you don't need a car, even though their overall COL is high. In car-dependent cities, insurance rates and gas prices become the key variables.

Healthcare

Healthcare costs vary by region due to differences in provider networks, state regulations, and local market competition. The same procedure can cost 2 to 3 times more in one city versus another. This category is harder to predict because it depends heavily on your insurance plan and health needs.

Utilities

Utility costs depend on climate, energy sources, and local rates. Cities with extreme summers or winters tend to have higher utility bills. Texas cities often have lower electricity rates but higher summer cooling costs. Northeast cities tend to have higher heating costs in winter.

Salary Equivalent Math

The salary equivalent calculation is straightforward: if you earn $75,000 in Houston (COL index 91) and want to maintain the same purchasing power in San Francisco (COL index 179), you divide your salary by the origin index, then multiply by the destination index. In this case: ($75,000 / 91) * 179 = approximately $147,500.

That doesn't mean you need to negotiate exactly that salary. It means your purchasing power at $147,500 in San Francisco roughly matches $75,000 in Houston. You might accept a lower salary if other factors matter to you, like career opportunities, lifestyle, climate, or proximity to family. But the number gives you a concrete baseline for negotiations.

Keep in mind that taxes aren't fully reflected in the COL index. States like Texas, Florida, and Tennessee have no state income tax, which effectively boosts your take-home pay compared to states like California (up to 13.3%) or New York (up to 10.9%). I'd recommend using a separate tax calculator alongside this tool for a complete picture.

Cost of Living Trends in 2025 and 2026

The cost of living field has shifted noticeably since 2020. Remote work has flattened some of the traditional gaps as higher-income workers relocated from expensive metros to mid-tier cities. This pushed up housing costs in places like Boise, Austin, Nashville, and Raleigh while slightly cooling some of the most expensive markets.

Housing remains the wildcard. Mortgage rates have stabilized in the mid-6 percent range as of early 2026, which keeps homeownership expensive but more predictable than the volatile period from 2022 to 2024. Rental markets have softened in cities that built significant new apartment supply, particularly in the Sun Belt.

Grocery inflation has largely returned to pre-pandemic norms (2 to 3 percent annually), though some categories like eggs and beef continue to see above-average increases. Gas prices remain regionally variable, with West Coast prices typically 30 to 50 percent above the national average due to refinery capacity and state taxes.

Relocation Tips

If you're considering a move, here is what I've learned from analyzing cost of living data across hundreds of cities:

Visit before you commit. Spend at least a week in the destination city, ideally during the worst weather season. A city that feels perfect in October might feel very different in August or February. Check grocery store prices, drive the commute, and look at actual rental listings rather than relying on averages.

Factor in your specific lifestyle. If you work remotely and cook at home, the transportation and dining cost differences matter less. If you have kids, school quality and childcare costs become critical variables that most COL indexes don't fully capture.

Negotiate with data. When discussing salary adjustments for a relocation, come prepared with specific COL numbers. Employers expect this conversation and many have formal relocation adjustment policies. A well-researched ask is much more likely to succeed than a vague request for "more money."

Don't forget the hidden costs of moving. The move itself can cost $3,000 to $10,000 depending on distance. Security deposits, utility connection fees, new furniture, and the inevitable first few months of figuring out where to shop all add up. Budget an extra $5,000 to $15,000 for transition costs.

Remote Work and Cost of Living Arbitrage

The rise of remote work has created an entirely new dimension to cost of living planning. If you can earn a San Francisco salary while living in Nashville, you're effectively getting a 40 to 50 percent purchasing power boost without changing jobs. This strategy, sometimes called geographic arbitrage, has become a major driver of migration patterns since 2020.

But it isn't without complications. Many companies have adopted location-based pay adjustments that reduce salaries when employees relocate to lower-cost areas. The size of these adjustments varies widely. Some companies cut pay by the full COL differential, some split the difference, and a growing number have adopted location-agnostic pay that is the same regardless of where you live. Before making a move, it is critical to understand your employer's policy.

Tax implications add another layer of complexity. If you move from a high-tax state to a low-tax state, you generally benefit immediately from the lower tax burden. But some states have specific rules about remote workers, and if your company has offices in your former state, you might owe taxes there on days you work at that office. States like New York have been particularly aggressive about claiming tax revenue from remote workers who serve New York-based clients.

The social dimension matters too. Cost of living numbers don't capture the value of being near friends, family, cultural institutions, or professional networking opportunities. I've seen people save thousands per year by relocating to a cheaper city only to feel isolated and end up moving back within a year or two. The cheapest city isn't necessarily the best city for your specific circumstances.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does a COL index of 100 mean?
A cost of living index of 100 represents the national average. If a city has an index of 120, it is 20 percent more expensive than the average US city. If a city has an index of 85, it is 15 percent cheaper. The index is calculated from a weighted basket of goods and services across housing, groceries, transportation, healthcare, and utilities.
How often does the COL data get updated?
The underlying data sources (BLS, C2ER, Census) update quarterly or annually depending on the category. Housing data moves the fastest, with rental indexes updating monthly. This calculator uses a blend of the most recent available data as of March 2026. I review and update the figures periodically to keep them current.
Does this include taxes?
The COL index partially reflects taxes through their effect on prices (sales tax affects groceries and goods), but it doesn't directly include income tax or property tax differences. State income tax is a major factor: moving from California (up to 13.3%) to Texas (0%) can effectively give you a 10%+ raise even if the COL indexes are similar. Use a separate tax calculator for a complete picture.
Why does housing vary so much between cities?
Housing costs are driven by supply and demand in local markets. Cities with geographic constraints (San Francisco's peninsula, Manhattan's island), strict zoning laws, limited buildable land, and strong job markets tend to have the highest housing costs. Cities that can expand outward and have more permissive building regulations (Houston, Phoenix) tend to keep housing costs lower through abundant supply.
How should I use this for salary negotiations?
Calculate the equivalent salary first to establish your baseline. Then research the market rate for your role in the destination city using sites like Glassdoor or Levels.fyi. Your target should be the higher of the two numbers. Present the COL data as part of your negotiation, framing it as maintaining purchasing power parity rather than asking for a raise. Most companies find this approach reasonable and data-driven.
Is it cheaper to live in the suburbs?
Usually, yes, for housing. But suburban living often comes with higher transportation costs (car payments, insurance, gas, maintenance) that offset some of the housing savings. A study by the Center for Neighborhood Technology found that when you combine housing and transportation costs, some inner-city neighborhoods are actually cheaper than their suburban counterparts. It depends heavily on the specific metro area.
Can I use this for international comparisons?
This calculator is designed for US cities only. International comparisons involve additional complexities like currency exchange rates, purchasing power parity, different tax structures, and vastly different consumption baskets. For international comparisons, I'd recommend the Economist's Big Mac Index or Numbeo's international cost of living rankings as starting points.
How precise are COL calculators in general?
Cost of living calculators provide reasonable estimates for typical households, but individual results can vary significantly based on lifestyle choices. The housing component is usually the most precise because rental and home price data is abundant and well-tracked. Categories like groceries and transportation have more variability because personal choices (shopping at Whole Foods versus Aldi, owning a car versus taking the bus) create wide ranges within a single city. Use calculator results as a starting point for your own research rather than a definitive answer.
What about cost of living for retirees?
Retirees have a different spending pattern than working-age adults, which changes how COL comparisons work. Healthcare typically becomes a larger percentage of spending, while commuting costs drop to near zero. Housing costs depend heavily on whether you own your home outright. States with no income tax (Florida, Texas, Tennessee) are popular retirement destinations partly for this reason. Property taxes become more significant for homeowners since they represent an ongoing cost even after the mortgage is paid off. Some states offer property tax exemptions or freezes for seniors.

Hidden Costs Most Calculators Miss

Standard cost of living indexes capture the major expense categories, but several meaningful costs fly under the radar. I've identified these through original research and conversations with people who have relocated between cities.

Childcare costs vary enormously and aren't well captured in most COL indexes. In cities like San Francisco, Boston, and Washington DC, full-time daycare for an infant can exceed $25,000 per year. In lower-cost cities, the same quality of care might run $10,000 to $15,000. For families with young children, this single expense can dwarf differences in other categories.

Auto insurance rates reflect local risk factors including accident rates, theft rates, litigation culture, and state regulations. Moving from a low-insurance state like Maine ($900/year average) to Michigan ($2,600/year average) can add $1,700 annually to your transportation costs. This isn't captured well in broad transportation indexes.

Home maintenance costs scale with both the age of the housing stock and the local climate. Homes in humid southern climates face HVAC, pest control, and mold issues that northern homes generally avoid. Conversely, northern homes deal with snow removal, ice damage, and heating system maintenance. These ongoing costs add $2,000 to $5,000 annually depending on the home and location, but they rarely appear in COL comparisons.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Having reviewed many cost of living calculator calculations, I have identified the most common errors that lead to incorrect results. Avoiding these mistakes will improve the accuracy of your work significantly.

The most frequent error is using inconsistent units. Mixing metric and imperial measurements, or confusing different unit scales, accounts for a large percentage of calculation mistakes. This calculator handles unit conversions internally, but if you are performing manual calculations or using the results in subsequent work, always verify that your units are consistent throughout the entire calculation chain.

Another common mistake is applying formulas outside their valid range. Many formulas have assumptions and limitations that restrict their applicability. Using a formula designed for one scenario in a different context can produce results that look reasonable but are actually significantly wrong.

Rounding errors can accumulate in multi-step calculations. This calculator maintains full precision throughout the calculation chain and only rounds the displayed result, which is the recommended practice.
