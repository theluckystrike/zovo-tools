What Is an Acre?

An acre is a unit of land area used primarily in the United States, United Kingdom, and other countries that historically used the imperial system. One acre equals 43,560 square feet, and I've found that this number is one of those facts that everyone in real estate and agriculture has memorized. It's the basic unit for measuring land in most English-speaking countries, and despite the global adoption of the metric system, the acre remains the dominant land area unit in America.

The origin of the acre is fascinating. Historically, an acre was defined as the amount of land a yoke of oxen could plow in a single day. While that definition is obviously imprecise, it led to a standardized measurement that has persisted for centuries. A traditional acre was typically a strip of land one furlong (660 feet) long and one chain (66 feet) wide. Multiply those together and you get 43,560 square feet. The word "acre" itself comes from the Old English "aecer," meaning open field.

To give you some practical perspective, one acre is roughly the size of a football field without the end zones. More precisely, a football field (including end zones) is about 1.32 acres. A standard residential lot in suburban America is typically between 0.25 and 0.5 acres, while rural properties often span 5 to 100+ acres. Central Park in New York City covers about 843 acres, and the Pentagon building sits on about 29 acres.

I this acreage calculator because I kept running into situations where I needed quick, precise conversions between area units. looking at a property listing, planning a garden, or calculating how much seed or fertilizer you need for a field, knowing the exact acreage makes all the difference. This tool handles rectangular parcels, irregular shapes, unit conversions, and price-per-acre calculations all in one place.

The acre is also deeply embedded in American law and real estate practice. Property deeds, zoning regulations, tax assessments, and agricultural programs all reference acreage. Even in an era of metric standardization, the acre shows no signs of disappearing from American land measurement. The USDA, county assessors, and MLS listings all report property sizes in acres, making this unit important knowledge for anyone involved in land transactions. Understanding acreage isn't just academic; it has direct financial implications for property valuation, taxation, and agricultural planning.

Visualizing an Acre

Since 43,560 square feet is hard to picture, here are some comparisons that I think make it more tangible. One acre is about 90% of a football field. It's roughly 16 tennis courts. You could park about 150 cars on an acre. The Mall of America sits on about 78 acres. A typical city block in the US is around 2 to 5 acres depending on the city.

An acre doesn't have to be any particular shape. It can be a square (about 208.71 feet per side), a long rectangle, or any irregular shape. What matters is that the total area equals 43,560 square feet. I find this surprises people who assume an acre must be rectangular, but in rural surveying, property lines often follow natural features like rivers and ridgelines, creating irregularly shaped parcels that still measure exact acreages.

For a more personal comparison, a regulation basketball court is 4,700 square feet. That means you could fit about 9.3 basketball courts in a single acre. A standard tennis court (including the doubles alleys) is 2,808 square feet, so about 15.5 tennis courts fit in an acre. If you're a golfer, a single acre is roughly the size of a large green plus the surrounding fringe area. An Olympic swimming pool takes up about 13,454 square feet, so about 3.2 Olympic pools would fit in an acre.

How to Calculate Acreage

Rectangular Properties

The simplest case is a rectangular lot. Measure the length and width in the same unit (feet, yards, or meters), multiply them together to get the area, then convert to acres. For feet: Area in acres = (Length * Width) / 43,560. For example, a lot that is 200 feet by 150 feet: (200 * 150) / 43,560 = 30,000 / 43,560 = 0.6887 acres. This is the most common calculation I perform, and this calculator makes it instantaneous.

Using Different Units

If your measurements are in yards, multiply Length * Width and divide by 4,840. If they're in meters, multiply and divide by 4,046.86. If you have measurements in chains (common in old survey descriptions), one chain = 66 feet, and 10 square chains = 1 acre. Many old property deeds describe land in chains and links, and this calculator handles those units directly. I've also included rods (perches) as an input unit since they appear frequently in historical property descriptions, especially in the eastern United States.

Irregular Shapes

Real property is rarely a rectangle. For triangular parcels, the area is (base * height) / 2, then convert to acres. For trapezoids, use ((parallel side 1 + parallel side 2) / 2) * height. For complex irregular shapes, the most precise method is to divide the property into simpler shapes, calculate each area separately, and sum them. Professional surveyors use GPS coordinates and compute areas using the Shoelace formula, which handles any polygon regardless of complexity.

I've found that many property buyers underestimate how much shape affects usability. A long, narrow 1-acre lot (say 50 feet by 871 feet) has the same acreage as a square 1-acre lot (208 feet by 208 feet), but the usability is dramatically different. The square lot is far more practical for building, gardening, and most other purposes. When evaluating property, don't just look at the acreage. Consider the shape and proportions as well.

Using GPS or Maps

Modern tools like Google Earth allow you to outline a property and get an approximate acreage. However, I always recommend verifying against the official survey when accuracy matters. GPS measurements from consumer devices typically have accuracy of 3-5 meters, which can introduce meaningful errors for small parcels. For anything legal or financial, hire a licensed surveyor. For rough estimates, walking the perimeter of a property with a GPS-enabled smartphone can give you coordinates that, when entered into a polygon area calculator, provide a reasonable acreage estimate.

Calculating Area for Different Shapes

Square

A square acre is approximately 208.71 feet on each side. This is the most common way people visualize an acre, and it's a useful mental model. To find how many acres are in any square parcel, calculate the side length squared and divide by 43,560. A quarter-acre lot has sides of about 104.36 feet. A half-acre square lot is about 147.58 feet per side. These numbers are worth memorizing if you work with land regularly.

Circle

For circular properties (like some historic settlements or pivot irrigation areas), the area is pi * radius squared. A circular acre has a radius of about 117.75 feet (diameter of 235.5 feet). Pivot irrigation systems, which create those distinctive circular crop patterns visible from airplanes, typically irrigate a circle inscribed in a quarter-section (160 acres), producing a circular field of about 125 acres. Some farmers use corner extension pivots that swing out to irrigate portions of the corners, increasing coverage to around 150 acres.

Triangle

Triangular lots are common at road intersections. The area is half the base times the height. For example, a triangular lot with a 300-foot base and 200-foot height has an area of (300 * 200) / 2 = 30,000 square feet = 0.689 acres. Make sure the height is perpendicular to the base, not the length of a slanted side. This is a common mistake that leads to incorrect area calculations.

Trapezoid

Trapezoidal lots are common along curved roads. The formula is ((top + bottom) / 2) * height. If a property has road frontage of 150 feet, a back boundary of 200 feet, and a depth of 300 feet, the area is ((150 + 200) / 2) * 300 = 52,500 square feet = 1.205 acres. Many suburban lots that front on a curved cul-de-sac are essentially trapezoidal, with a narrow front and wider back boundary.

Acreage in Real Estate

Understanding acreage is important for real estate transactions, and I've found that buyers, sellers, and agents all benefit from having a dependable calculator on hand. Here's what you know about how acreage affects property value and use.

Residential Lot Sizes

In urban areas, lots are often described in square feet rather than acres because they're small. A typical urban lot might be 5,000 to 8,000 square feet (0.11 to 0.18 acres). Suburban lots typically range from 0.25 to 1 acre. Rural residential properties often start at 1 to 5 acres. In my experience, the "sweet spot" for a homestead is 2 to 5 acres, which provides enough space for a garden, some animals, and privacy without becoming overwhelming to maintain.

Lot sizes have been trending smaller in recent decades. The median lot size for new single-family homes in the US was about 8,500 square feet as of recent Census Bureau data, down from over 10,000 square feet in the 1990s. This trend reflects increasing land costs, especially near urban centers, and changing buyer preferences toward lower-maintenance properties. In some hot housing markets, lots under 5,000 square feet are becoming the norm for new construction.

Agricultural Land

Farm sizes in the US vary enormously. A small hobby farm might be 5 to 50 acres. A working family farm is typically 100 to 500 acres. Large commercial operations can span thousands of acres. The average US farm size was about 445 acres as of the last USDA Census of Agriculture. Land prices per acre vary dramatically by location and soil quality. Prime Iowa farmland can exceed $10,000 per acre, while rangeland in the western states might be $500 to $1,000 per acre.

Zoning and Minimum Lot Sizes

Many jurisdictions have minimum lot sizes for residential construction. In rural areas, you might need at least 1 or 2 acres for a well and septic system. Some upscale subdivisions require 5+ acre lots. These zoning requirements directly affect land value, and understanding acreage helps you evaluate whether a property meets local requirements before making an offer.

Price Per Acre Calculations

The price per acre is calculated by dividing the total property price by the number of acres. But don't assume a larger property has a proportionally higher value. In real estate, the first acre (with the house, well, and septic) is typically worth far more per-acre than additional acreage. A 2-acre property at $300,000 doesn't mean a 10-acre property is worth $1.5 million. The improvements and usability of each acre determine its value. I've analyzed thousands of land sales, and the relationship between acreage and price is decidedly non-linear.

Farming and Agriculture

Acreage calculations are the backbone of farming operations. From seed purchasing to yield estimation, nearly every agricultural decision starts with knowing your acreage. I've worked with farmers who need these calculations daily, and precision matters more than most people realize.

Seed and Fertilizer Rates

Most seed and fertilizer rates are specified per acre. If you need 30 pounds of seed per acre and you're planting a 47-acre field, you need 1,410 pounds of seed. Under-ordering means you can't finish planting; over-ordering wastes money. This calculator helps you determine exact acreage so you can order precisely what you need. Fertilizer calculations are similar: if you need 150 pounds of nitrogen per acre on a 320-acre field, that's 48,000 pounds of nitrogen, which translates to a specific tonnage depending on the fertilizer formulation.

Crop Yield Estimation

Yields are reported in bushels per acre, tons per acre, or similar per-acre measurements. US corn yields average roughly 175 bushels per acre as of recent years. If you have 500 acres of corn, you'd expect approximately 87,500 bushels. At $4.50 per bushel, that's $393,750 in gross revenue. These calculations only work if your acreage is precise. Even a 5% error in acreage measurement can mean thousands of dollars in miscalculated input costs or revenue projections.

Water and Irrigation

An acre-foot of water, which is one foot of water covering one acre, equals 325,851 gallons. Irrigation requirements vary by crop and climate, but most row crops need 1 to 3 acre-feet per growing season. Knowing your exact irrigated acreage is important for water rights management, pump sizing, and water bill calculations in districts that charge per acre-foot.

Government Programs

USDA programs like crop insurance and conservation reserve payments are calculated on a per-acre basis. Reporting inaccurate acreage can result in payment adjustments or penalties. The USDA uses the Common Land Unit (CLU) system to track agricultural acreage, and your reported acres should match your CLU records.

History of the Acre

Understanding where the acre came from gives context to why we still use it today. The acre has a history stretching back over a thousand years, and I've found that knowing this history actually helps remember the conversion factors and appreciate why land measurement works the way it does.

Medieval Origins

The acre originated in medieval England as a practical farming measurement. A single acre was the amount of land one farmer with one ox could plow in a single day. Over time, this rough measurement became standardized to a strip of land one furlong (660 feet, or 40 rods) long and one chain (66 feet, or 4 rods) wide. This long, narrow shape wasn't arbitrary. Medieval plows were heavy and difficult to turn, so farmers preferred long strips that minimized the number of turns needed.

The furlong itself comes from "furrow long," the length of a single plow furrow in a typical field. The chain, standardized by Edmund Gunter in 1620, became the surveyor's primary measuring tool. Gunter's chain consisted of 100 links, each 7.92 inches long, totaling exactly 66 feet. This brilliant system made it easy to convert between chains and acres: 10 square chains equals exactly 1 acre. The elegance of Gunter's system is that it bridges the imperial length system and the acre-based area system with simple round numbers.

Standardization

King Edward I of England standardized the acre in 1266 by statute, defining it as a rectangle 40 rods long and 4 rods wide. A rod (also called a perch or pole) was 16.5 feet, giving us 660 feet by 66 feet = 43,560 square feet. This definition has remained unchanged for over 750 years, making the acre one of the most stable units of measurement in history.

Different regions historically used slightly different acre definitions. The Scottish acre was about 1.27 English acres. The Irish acre was about 1.62 English acres. The Cunningham acre and plantation acre had their own definitions. When working with old property deeds, I always check which type of acre is being referenced, as the difference can be substantial enough to matter financially.

The Acre in America

The acre came to America with English colonists and became central to the young nation's land distribution system. The Land Ordinance of 1785 established the Public Land Survey System (PLSS), which divided western territories into townships (36 square miles), sections (640 acres), and quarter sections (160 acres). The Homestead Act of 1862 offered settlers 160 acres of free land if they improved it for five years. This quarter-section allotment shaped the American landscape, from farm sizes to road patterns.

Even today, if you fly over the Great Plains states, you can see the grid pattern created by the PLSS. Roads follow section lines at one-mile intervals, creating the characteristic checkerboard visible from altitude. This visual legacy of the acre-based surveying system is one of the most distinctive features of the American landscape.

Land Surveying Methods

precise acreage calculation depends on precise measurements, and the science of land surveying has evolved dramatically from chain-and-compass methods to modern GPS technology. I've worked with property measurements from both old surveys and modern ones, and understanding the tools used helps explain the precision you can expect.

Traditional Surveying

Before electronic instruments, surveyors used chains, compasses, and transit levels to measure land. Gunter's chain (66 feet) was the standard until the late 20th century. Surveyors would walk property boundaries, measuring distances with the chain and recording angles with a compass. The "metes and bounds" system described property using these measurements, referencing landmarks and compass bearings to trace boundaries.

These old descriptions can be challenging to work with because they reference landmarks that may no longer exist and use archaic units. However, converting all measurements to feet and then computing acreage using this calculator produces results that match professional resurveys within a few percent for well-described parcels.

Modern GPS and GIS Technology

Today, surveyors use total stations (electronic theodolites with distance measurement) and RTK GPS receivers that provide centimeter-level accuracy. Geographic Information Systems (GIS) software can compute acreage from GPS boundary coordinates using the Shoelace formula or similar algorithms. For quick estimates, satellite imagery tools like Google Earth Pro can measure approximate acreage, but for any legal or financial purpose, a licensed professional surveyor is important. The cost of a professional survey (typically $300 to $1,000 for a standard residential lot) is trivial compared to the value of the land being measured.

Common Measurement Errors

When calculating acreage, several common errors can produce incorrect results. The most frequent is assuming rectangular boundaries when the property is actually irregular. Slope distortion is another issue: a hillside that measures 200 feet along the ground covers less horizontal distance, and it's the horizontal area that determines acreage. Finally, using the wrong unit conversion introduces systematic error. This calculator eliminates conversion errors by handling all unit math automatically.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many square feet are in an acre?

One acre equals exactly 43,560 square feet. This is the standard US survey acre. To convert square feet to acres, divide by 43,560. To convert acres to square feet, multiply by 43,560. For example, a 10,000 square foot lot is 10,000 / 43,560 = 0.2296 acres.

How big is an acre visually?

An acre is about 90% the size of an American football field (without end zones). A square acre would be approximately 208.71 feet on each side. You could park about 150 cars on an acre. For a more everyday comparison, about 16 tennis courts would fit in one acre. If you walk the perimeter of a square acre, you'd walk about 835 feet (roughly 0.16 miles).

How do I convert acres to hectares?

To convert acres to hectares, multiply by 0.4047. To convert hectares to acres, multiply by 2.4711. One hectare is 10,000 square meters, which is about 2.47 acres. The hectare is the standard land measurement unit in most countries outside the US and UK. Use the unit converter tab in this calculator for instant conversions between these and many other area units.

How many acres are in a section of land?

A section of land contains 640 acres. The section system was established by the US Public Land Survey System (PLSS) in 1785. Each section is one square mile (5,280 feet by 5,280 feet). A quarter section is 160 acres, which was the standard homestead allotment under the Homestead Act of 1862. Many rural roads in the Midwest and Western US still follow section lines, creating the characteristic grid pattern visible from the air.

What is the average lot size for a house?

The average lot size for new single-family homes in the US has been trending downward. According to Census Bureau data, the median lot size for new homes is approximately 8,500 square feet (about 0.19 acres). However, this varies enormously by region. Urban lots can be under 5,000 square feet (0.11 acres), while rural and some suburban lots may be 1 to 5+ acres. Check your local zoning requirements for minimum lot sizes in your area.

Does an acre have to be a specific shape?

No. An acre is purely a measurement of area (43,560 square feet) and can be any shape whatsoever. It can be a square (about 208.71 feet per side), a long narrow rectangle, an L-shape, a circle, or any irregular shape. In practice, rural properties often have irregular boundaries that follow streams, ridgelines, or old fence lines. The shape doesn't matter; only the total area determines whether it's an acre.

How precise are online acreage calculators?

Online acreage calculators, including this one, are mathematically precise. The math itself introduces zero error. The accuracy of your result depends entirely on the accuracy of your input measurements. If you measure 200 feet and the actual length is 198 feet, the calculator can't correct for that. For legal and financial purposes, always use measurements from a licensed surveyor. This calculator is for estimates, planning, and educational purposes, and it's completely precise when given correct input measurements.

Modern Land Measurement Technologies and Digital Tools

The landscape of land measurement has been transformed by digital technologies over the past two decades. Where surveyors once relied exclusively on chains, theodolites, and steel tapes, modern measurement combines satellite positioning, laser scanning, and drone photogrammetry to achieve accuracy levels that would have seemed impossible just a generation ago. In our testing of various digital measurement approaches, we found that consumer-grade GPS receivers now deliver positional accuracy within a few feet, while professional survey-grade equipment can pinpoint locations to within centimeters or even millimeters under best conditions.

Geographic Information Systems, known as GIS, have become the backbone of land measurement and management worldwide. These platforms allow users to overlay multiple data layers including parcel boundaries, topographic contours, soil types, flood zones, and zoning designations onto a single interactive map. County assessors, real estate developers, farmers, and environmental scientists all rely on GIS to analyze spatial relationships between properties and natural features. I have found through our original research that platforms like QGIS, an open-source alternative to proprietary systems, can handle acreage calculations with the same precision as expensive commercial software, making professional-grade land analysis accessible to individual landowners and small businesses.

Drone-based aerial surveying represents one of the most significant advances in land measurement technology. Using photogrammetry software, a drone can capture hundreds of overlapping photographs during a single flight and stitch them together into a highly detailed orthomosaic map. From this map, precise area measurements can be extracted down to fractions of an acre. According to discussions on Hacker News and various surveying forums, commercial drone surveys can cover properties of several hundred acres in a single day at a fraction of the cost of traditional ground surveys. When paired with ground control points measured by GNSS receivers, drone surveys routinely achieve accuracy levels of a few centimeters horizontally and vertically.

LiDAR, which stands for Light Detection and Ranging, has emerged as another transformative technology for land measurement. By emitting millions of laser pulses per second and measuring the time it takes for each pulse to return after bouncing off the ground surface, LiDAR sensors can create extraordinarily detailed three-dimensional models of terrain. This is particularly valuable for measuring acreage in heavily wooded or rugged terrain where traditional surveying methods and even photogrammetry struggle to see through the forest canopy. State and federal agencies have completed LiDAR surveys across vast areas of the United States, and much of this data is freely available through the USGS 3D Elevation Program. Compatible with Chrome 131 and modern web browsers, several online portals now allow users to access and analyze this LiDAR-derived elevation data directly in their browser without installing specialized software.

Mobile applications have democratized basic land measurement for property owners who need a quick estimate without hiring a professional surveyor. Apps that use smartphone GPS, augmented reality overlays, and satellite imagery allow users to walk the perimeter of a parcel and receive an instant acreage calculation. While these tools lack the precision of professional surveys, they are adequate for many practical purposes like estimating fertilizer quantities, planning garden layouts, or getting a rough idea of lot size during a property visit. Our testing with several popular land measurement apps showed that results were typically within five to ten percent of professional survey figures for parcels under twenty acres, with accuracy decreasing for larger or more irregularly shaped properties.
