How the Dilution Equation Works

The dilution equation C1V1 = C2V2 is one of the most frequently used formulas in chemistry and biology laboratories. It states that the product of the initial concentration and the initial volume equals the product of the final concentration and the final volume. This relationship holds because dilution does not add or remove solute from a solution. You are simply adding more solvent (usually water or buffer) to spread the same amount of solute across a larger volume.

From Wikipedia

A serial dilution is the stepwise dilution of a substance in solution, either by a constant or varying dilution factor. It is used to accurately create highly diluted solutions, as well as solutions for experiments requiring a concentration curve with an exponential or logarithmic scale.

Read more on Wikipedia

The equation works with any concentration unit as long as both sides use the same unit. Whether you are working with molar concentrations, percent solutions, parts per million, or milligrams per milliliter, the math is identical. Volume units do not match between V1 and V2 because the calculator handles the conversion internally, but the concentration units for C1 and C2 must be the same.

When you solve for a specific variable, you simply rearrange the equation. To find V1 (how much stock solution you need), divide both sides by C1 to get V1 = (C2 x V2) / C1. To find C2 (what your final concentration will be), divide both sides by V2 to get C2 = (C1 x V1) / V2. The same algebraic manipulation gives you V2 = (C1 x V1) / C2 and C1 = (C2 x V2) / V1.

Understanding Concentration Units

Choosing the right concentration unit depends on the field you are working in and the precision you need. Molarity (M) is the standard unit in analytical chemistry, representing moles of solute per liter of solution. For biological research, millimolar (mM), micromolar (uM), and nanomolar (nM) are common because many biological molecules are active at very low concentrations. A 1 M solution contains 1 mole of solute in 1 liter. A 1 mM solution is 1000 times more dilute, containing 0.001 moles per liter.

Percent solutions come in three varieties. Weight per volume (w/v) is the most common in life sciences and means grams of solute per 100 mL of solution. Volume per volume (v/v) is used for liquid-liquid mixtures, such as ethanol solutions. Weight per weight (w/w) is used less often in laboratory settings but is standard in some industrial applications.

Parts per million (ppm) is widely used in environmental science, water treatment, and food safety. In aqueous solutions at standard conditions, 1 ppm is approximately equal to 1 mg/L. The mg/mL unit is common in pharmaceutical and clinical laboratory settings, especially for protein solutions and drug preparations.

Serial Dilutions Explained

A serial dilution is a series of sequential dilutions, each performed on the previous diluted sample rather than the original stock. This technique is indispensable in microbiology, pharmacology, and any field where you need a wide range of concentrations spanning several orders of magnitude.

The most common serial dilution is a 1:10 (ten-fold) series. Starting with a 1 M solution and performing six ten-fold dilutions gives you concentrations of 0.1 M, 0.01 M, 0.001 M, 0.0001 M, 0.00001 M, and 0.000001 M. Two-fold serial dilutions are standard in immunology for antibody titrations. The dilution factor tells you how much each step reduces the concentration.

To perform a serial dilution physically, you transfer a calculated volume from tube to tube. For a 1:10 dilution with 1 mL final volume, you take 0.1 mL (100 uL) from the previous tube and add 0.9 mL of diluent. For a 1:2 dilution with 1 mL final volume, you take 0.5 mL from the previous tube and add 0.5 mL of diluent. The transfer volume equals the final volume divided by the dilution factor.

Step-by-Step Dilution Procedure

Preparing a dilution in the laboratory follows a consistent protocol regardless of what solution you are working with. First, calculate the volume of stock solution needed using the C1V1 = C2V2 formula. Second, measure that volume of stock solution using a pipette or graduated cylinder appropriate for the volume. Third, transfer the stock solution to a volumetric flask or container. Fourth, add solvent (water, buffer, or other diluent) until you reach the final desired volume. Mix thoroughly.

Accuracy depends heavily on using the right measuring equipment. For volumes under 1 mL, use a micropipette. For volumes between 1 and 25 mL, a serological pipette works well. For volumes of 25 mL and above, graduated cylinders and volumetric flasks provide adequate precision. Volumetric flasks are the gold standard for accuracy because they are calibrated to contain a precise volume at a specific temperature.

Common mistakes include measuring final volume incorrectly. The final volume V2 is the total volume of the solution after dilution, not the volume of solvent added. If you need 500 mL of final solution and your stock volume is 50 mL, you add solvent until the total reaches 500 mL, which means adding 450 mL of solvent.

Five Real-World Dilution Examples

1. Diluting Stock NaCl for Cell Culture

You have a 5 M NaCl stock and need 200 mL of 0.15 M NaCl (physiological saline). V1 = (0.15 M x 200 mL) / 5 M = 6 mL. Pipette 6 mL of 5 M NaCl into a graduated cylinder, then add deionized water to the 200 mL mark.

2. Preparing a 1X Buffer from 10X Stock

You have a 10X PBS stock and need 500 mL of 1X PBS for washing cells. V1 = (1X x 500 mL) / 10X = 50 mL. Measure 50 mL of 10X PBS and bring the total volume to 500 mL with distilled water. This 1:10 dilution is one of the most common laboratory preparations.

3. Diluting 70% Ethanol to 10% for Cleaning

You need 1 L of 10% ethanol from a 70% ethanol stock. V1 = (10% x 1000 mL) / 70% = 142.9 mL. Measure 142.9 mL of 70% ethanol and add water to reach 1000 mL total.

4. Antibiotic Stock Dilution for Agar Plates

Your ampicillin stock is 100 mg/mL and you need a working concentration of 0.1 mg/mL in 250 mL of agar. V1 = (0.1 mg/mL x 250 mL) / 100 mg/mL = 0.25 mL (250 uL). Add 250 uL of stock ampicillin to 250 mL of cooled agar medium.

5. Environmental Water Sample Dilution

A water sample tests at 500 ppm total dissolved solids and you dilute it to 50 ppm for instrument calibration in 100 mL. V1 = (50 ppm x 100 mL) / 500 ppm = 10 mL. Take 10 mL of the sample and dilute with pure water to 100 mL total.

Common Dilution Ratios and Their Uses

Certain dilution ratios appear frequently across scientific disciplines. A 1:2 dilution (two-fold) is standard in antibody titrations and dose-response curves. You mix equal parts sample and diluent. A 1:10 dilution (ten-fold) is the default in microbiology for colony counting and in chemistry for preparing dilution series. A 1:100 dilution is common when working with concentrated reagents or when samples need significant reduction for detection methods.

In clinical laboratories, specific dilutions are required for certain assays. Blood samples are often diluted 1:20 for manual cell counts using a hemocytometer. Urine samples may require 1:10 or 1:100 dilutions depending on the analyte being measured. In food microbiology, serial ten-fold dilutions from 1:10 to 1:1,000,000 are standard for determining colony-forming units per gram of food.

The dilution ratio notation can be confusing. A "1:10 dilution" means 1 part sample plus 9 parts diluent, giving 10 parts total. Some fields use "1 in 10" or "1/10" to express the same thing. A "dilution factor of 10" is equivalent. Be careful with the notation "1+9" which explicitly states the volumes: 1 part sample combined with 9 parts diluent.

Troubleshooting Common Dilution Errors

The most frequent error in dilution calculations is confusing the final volume (V2) with the volume of diluent added. If V2 is 100 mL and V1 is 10 mL, you add 90 mL of diluent, not 100 mL. Adding 100 mL of diluent would give you 110 mL total, making your actual final concentration lower than intended.

Unit mismatches cause problems when the concentration units for C1 and C2 differ. If your stock is labeled 1 M but you enter 1 mM for C1, your calculated volumes will be off by a factor of 1000. Always double-check that C1 and C2 are expressed in the same unit before plugging numbers into the equation.

Pipetting errors compound in serial dilutions. If you transfer slightly more or less than intended at each step, the error multiplies across all subsequent tubes. Using calibrated pipettes and proper pipetting technique (pre-wetting tips, touching off on the vessel wall, pipetting at the correct angle) reduces systematic errors.

Temperature affects solution volumes. Water and most solvents expand when heated and contract when cooled. For high-precision work, prepare dilutions at the temperature specified in the protocol and use volumetric glassware calibrated for that temperature (usually 20 degrees Celsius).

Dilutions in Specific Fields

In pharmacology, dilution calculations are critical for drug preparation. Intravenous medications often require dilution from concentrated vials to safe infusion concentrations. A vial containing 100 mg of drug in 5 mL must be diluted to 1 mg/mL for slow infusion, requiring a total volume of 100 mL. Errors in these calculations have direct patient safety implications.

Environmental scientists use dilutions when analyzing water and soil samples. Samples from contaminated sites may contain pollutants at concentrations too high for direct instrument analysis. Diluting by known factors brings analyte concentrations into the linear range of analytical methods like atomic absorption spectroscopy or ion chromatography.

In molecular biology, dilution calculations govern PCR setup, primer preparation, and nucleic acid quantification. A 100 uM primer stock is typically diluted to a 10 uM working stock, then further diluted in the reaction mix to a final concentration of 0.2 to 0.5 uM. DNA samples are diluted to normalized concentrations before library preparation for sequencing.

Food and beverage industries rely on dilutions for quality control. Sugar content in beverages is measured after dilution to bring samples within refractometer range. Microbial testing of food products requires serial dilutions to produce countable colonies on agar plates, typically between 30 and 300 colonies per plate for statistically valid results.

Advanced Dilution Concepts

When mixing solutions of different concentrations (not just diluting with pure solvent), you need the mixing equation: C1V1 + C2V2 = C3V3, where C3 and V3 are the concentration and volume of the final mixture. This three-component equation has an extra degree of freedom, so you typically fix one variable (like V3) and solve for the two volumes needed.

Dilution factors in multi-step dilutions multiply. If you perform a 1:10 dilution followed by a 1:5 dilution, the total dilution factor is 10 x 5 = 50. The final concentration is the original concentration divided by 50. This principle underlies the logic of serial dilutions and allows you to calculate the overall dilution from any number of sequential steps.

For gravimetric dilutions, where you work with masses instead of volumes, the equation is C1m1 = C2m2, where m represents mass. This approach is more accurate than volumetric dilution because mass can be measured more precisely than volume. Analytical chemistry laboratories performing trace-level analysis often prepare standards gravimetrically.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the dilution formula C1V1 = C2V2?
C1V1 = C2V2 is the dilution equation where C1 is the initial concentration, V1 is the initial volume (or volume of stock solution needed), C2 is the final desired concentration, and V2 is the final total volume. It works because the amount of solute stays constant during dilution. You are only adding solvent, so the total moles or mass of solute in the initial and final solutions are equal.
How do I calculate the volume of stock solution needed?
Rearrange the formula to V1 = (C2 x V2) / C1. Enter your stock concentration as C1, the concentration you want as C2, and the total volume you need as V2. The calculator gives you V1, which is the amount of stock to measure out. Then add diluent to reach V2 total volume.
What units can I use in the dilution calculator?
For concentration, this calculator supports M (molar), mM (millimolar), uM (micromolar), nM (nanomolar), % (percent), ppm (parts per million), and mg/mL. For volume, it supports L (liters), mL (milliliters), and uL (microliters). The concentration units for C1 and C2 must match. Volume units can differ because the calculator converts them automatically.
What is a serial dilution?
A serial dilution is a stepwise dilution where each step uses the previous diluted solution as the source. Each transfer reduces the concentration by a fixed factor. For example, a 1:10 serial dilution starting at 1 M produces concentrations of 0.1 M, 0.01 M, 0.001 M, and so on. Serial dilutions are essential for creating standard curves and working with wide concentration ranges.
How do I prepare a 1:10 dilution?
Take 1 part of your solution and combine it with 9 parts of diluent for a total of 10 parts. For example, pipette 100 uL of your sample into 900 uL of water. The resulting concentration is one-tenth of the original. The "1:10" means 1 part sample in 10 parts total, not 1 part sample plus 10 parts diluent.
Can I mix different concentration units?
C1 and C2 must use the same concentration unit for the equation to work. You cannot use M for C1 and mM for C2 without converting first. Volume units (V1 and V2) can differ because the calculator converts them internally. If your stock is in mg/mL and your target is in molar, you will convert one to match the other using the molecular weight.
Why does dilution not change the amount of solute?
Dilution only adds solvent to a solution. No solute is added or removed during the process. The same number of molecules (or same mass) of solute exists before and after dilution. The concentration decreases because those molecules are now dispersed in a larger volume. This conservation principle is what makes C1V1 = C2V2 work.
How do I dilute a percentage solution?
Use C1V1 = C2V2 with the percentage values directly. To make 500 mL of 10% from a 70% stock: V1 = (10 x 500) / 70 = 71.4 mL. Measure 71.4 mL of the 70% stock and add water to 500 mL total. This works the same way regardless of whether the percentage is w/v, v/v, or w/w, as long as both sides use the same type.
What is the difference between dilution and dilution factor?
A dilution ratio like 1:10 describes the proportion of sample to total volume. The dilution factor is the number by which you divide the original concentration to get the new concentration. For a 1:10 dilution, the dilution factor is 10. If you start with 100 mM and apply a dilution factor of 10, you get 10 mM.
How many serial dilution steps do I need?
Divide your starting concentration by your target concentration and take the logarithm base equal to your dilution factor. For example, going from 1 M to 0.000001 M with ten-fold dilutions: log10(1/0.000001) = log10(1,000,000) = 6 steps. The serial dilution calculator above handles this math automatically. You can also just experiment with different numbers of steps to see which series covers your desired range.
How accurate are dilution calculations?
The math is exact, but real-world accuracy depends on your measuring equipment and technique. Volumetric flasks are accurate to within 0.05% to 0.1%. Micropipettes are typically accurate to 0.5% to 1.5%. Graduated cylinders are less precise, typically 1% to 3%. For best results, use calibrated equipment, work at the recommended temperature, and practice proper pipetting technique.
