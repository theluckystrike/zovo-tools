Work-Energy Theorem Calculator

The work-energy theorem is one of the most useful relationships in classical mechanics. It directly connects force, displacement, and kinetic energy change without needing to know time. I use this constantly when analyzing collisions, braking scenarios, and projectile motion. Stopping Distance Estimator

This is the section I get the most questions about, and we've all been in the situation of wondering how far our car actually needs to stop. Stopping distance comes directly from the work-energy theorem: the friction force times the stopping distance must equal the kinetic energy. What surprises most people is that doubling your speed quadruples the stopping distance because KE scales with v^2. I've included presets for dry road, wet road, and ice because I found these are the most common scenarios people need.

Kinetic Energy, Potential Energy, and Total Mechanical Energy

I can't discuss kinetic energy without covering its relationship to potential energy. In any conservative system (where no energy is lost to friction or heat), the total mechanical energy is constant. This is the conservation of energy principle, and it's one of the most fundamental laws in physics. I've a visual diagram below that shows how KE and PE trade off as an object moves through different heights.

Etotal = KE + PE = ½mv² + mgh = constant

How Energy Transforms

Consider a ball dropped from a height. At the top, it has maximum potential energy (PE = mgh) and zero kinetic energy. As it falls, PE converts to KE. At the bottom, all PE has become KE and the velocity is maximum. This is why a ball dropped from 10 meters hits the ground at about 14 m/s regardless of its mass. v = sqrt(2gh).

Energy Distribution at Different Heights (ball dropped from 100m)

At 100m (top): 100% PE, 0% KE
At 75m: 75% PE, 25% KE
At 50m: 50% PE, 50% KE
At 25m: 25% PE, 75% KE
At 0m (ground): 0% PE, 100% KE

Real-World Implications

This KE-PE relationship is everywhere. A roller coaster converts PE at the top of the hill to KE at the bottom. A pendulum oscillates between KE and PE. A hydroelectric dam converts the PE of water at height to KE of falling water, then to electrical energy. Understanding this relationship is the key to solving most mechanics problems without needing to analyze forces at every point.

When Energy Isn't Conserved

In real systems, friction and air resistance convert mechanical energy to thermal energy (heat). A sliding block loses KE to friction. A falling object with air drag reaches terminal velocity when the drag force equals gravity. The total energy is still conserved (first law of thermodynamics), but the mechanical energy decreases. This is why real-world stopping distances are calculable. The braking force (friction) converts all KE to heat in the brake pads and tires.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does doubling velocity quadruple kinetic energy?
Because KE is proportional to v squared. If you double v, then v^2 becomes (2v)^2 = 4v^2. This is why highway speed crashes are so much more destructive than city speed crashes. A car going 120 km/h has four times the kinetic energy of the same car going 60 km/h, even though it's only going twice as fast. This relationship comes from the calculus of work and force. To accelerate a moving object, you apply force over a longer distance, so each increment of speed requires more energy than the last.
When do I use relativistic kinetic energy instead of classical?
As a rule of thumb, use relativistic KE when the velocity exceeds about 10% the speed of light (0.1c or about 30,000 km/s). At 0.1c, the classical formula is off by about 0.75%. At 0.5c, the error is about 19%. At 0.9c, classical KE underestimates by about 71%. For everyday objects like cars, planes, and even spacecraft, classical KE is perfectly fine. You only need relativistic corrections for subatomic particles, particle accelerator calculations, and astrophysical scenarios.
What is the moment of inertia and why does it matter for rotational KE?
Moment of inertia (I) is the rotational equivalent of mass. It measures how hard it is to change an object's rotational speed. Unlike mass, which is just a number, moment of inertia depends on both the mass AND how that mass is distributed relative to the axis of rotation. A hollow cylinder has a larger I than a solid cylinder of the same mass and radius because more mass is farther from the axis. This is why figure skaters spin faster when they pull their arms in. They're reducing their moment of inertia.
Can kinetic energy be negative?
No. Kinetic energy is always zero or positive. The formula KE = 1/2 mv^2 squares the velocity, so even if you define a "negative" direction, the KE is positive. This is a fundamental property. An object at rest has zero KE, and any moving object has positive KE. Changes in kinetic energy (delta KE) can be negative, which means the object slowed down, but KE itself is never negative.
How accurate is the stopping distance calculator?
The formula d = v^2/(2*mu*g) gives the theoretical minimum stopping distance assuming constant braking force, flat road, and no reaction time. Real-world stopping distances are longer because of driver reaction time (typically 0.7-1.5 seconds), brake system delay, varying friction during braking, road grade, and vehicle weight distribution. Use this as a minimum estimate. Actual stopping distances in normal conditions are typically 1.5 to 2.5 times the theoretical minimum.
What's the difference between kinetic energy and momentum?
Momentum (p = mv) is a vector quantity that scales linearly with velocity. Kinetic energy (KE = 1/2 mv^2) is a scalar that scales with the square of velocity. Both are conserved in elastic collisions, but only momentum is conserved in inelastic collisions. A heavy slow object and a light fast object can have the same momentum but very different kinetic energies. Momentum determines how hard something is to stop. KE determines how much damage it does on impact.
