Understanding Virginia's Four Tax Brackets

Virginia uses a progressive income tax system with four brackets that haven't changed significantly in years. Here's what makes Virginia's system unique: the brackets are relatively compressed compared to many states. The 5.75% top rate kicks in at just $17,001 of taxable income - meaning the vast majority of Virginia workers are effectively paying 5.75% on most of their income.

Let's walk through a concrete example. Say you earn $75,000 annually and you're filing single with the standard deduction of $8,000 and one personal exemption of $930. Your Virginia taxable income would be $66,070. Here's how the brackets apply:

  • First $3,000 at 2%: $60
  • Next $2,000 at 3%: $60
  • Next $12,000 at 5%: $600
  • Remaining $49,070 at 5.75%: $2,821.53
  • $3,541.53

This works out to an effective state tax rate of about 4.72% on your gross income. That's fairly competitive for the region - and it doesn't include any pre-tax deductions you might be making for retirement or health insurance.

Virginia Standard Deduction and Personal Exemptions

For tax year 2025, Virginia offers a standard deduction of $8,000 for single filers and $16,000 for married filing jointly. Each personal exemption is worth $930. If you're age 65 or older, you receive an additional $800 exemption - and if your adjusted gross income is below $75,000 (single) or $100,000 (married), you may qualify for the Virginia age deduction of up to $12,000.

Don't overlook the age 65+ deduction. I've found that many retirees moving to Virginia from high-tax northeastern states aren't aware of this benefit. Combined with the fact that Virginia fully exempts Social Security benefits from state income tax, it can represent significant savings for retirees.

How Pre-Tax Deductions Impact Your Virginia Take-Home Pay

One of the most ways to reduce both your federal and Virginia state tax burden is through pre-tax deductions. Traditional 401(k) contributions - up to $23,500 in 2025 ($31,000 if you're age 50+) - come directly off your taxable income before either federal or state taxes are calculated.

Health insurance premiums paid through your employer's cafeteria plan (Section 125) work the same way. If you're paying $3,600 per year in health insurance premiums pre-tax, that reduces your taxable income by $3,600 for both federal and Virginia state tax purposes. At a combined marginal rate of about 28% (22% federal + 5.75% state), that's roughly $1,008 in annual tax savings - money that would otherwise vanish from your paycheck.

This is why I the pre-tax deduction fields directly into this calculator. It won't give you an accurate picture if it doesn't account for the deductions that most working Virginians actually use.

FICA The Tax That Doesn't Care About Your State

Federal Insurance Contributions Act (FICA) taxes are the same whether you live in Virginia, California, or Alaska. In 2025, you'll pay 6.2% for Social Security on the first $168,600 of earned income, plus 1.45% for Medicare on all earned income. If you earn more than $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax kicks in.

For someone earning $75,000, FICA totals $5,737.50 - that's $4,650 for Social Security and $1,087.50 for Medicare. This is often the second-largest deduction from your paycheck after federal income tax, and it's non-negotiable. Pre-tax 401(k) contributions don't reduce FICA - only your income tax.

Virginia vs. The District of Columbia has a much steeper progressive structure, with rates climbing to 10.75% on income over $1,000,000. For middle-income earners, DC's rates range from 6% to 8.5%, generally resulting in $500, $2,000 more in state-level tax than Virginia for typical salaries.

Virginia vs. West Virginia's top rate is 6.5% on income over $60,000, making it slightly more expensive than Virginia for most earners. West Virginia also has fewer deduction options at the state level.

Virginia vs. North Carolina switched to a flat tax and currently charges 4.5% on all taxable income. For most earners above $40,000, North Carolina is modestly cheaper than Virginia - though the difference narrows when you factor in North Carolina's higher property taxes in many areas.

Virginia vs. Tennessee has no state income tax at all. If you're comparing these states for a potential move, you'll keep roughly $3,500 more per year on a $75,000 salary by living in Tennessee., Tennessee's sales tax rate of 7% (plus local additions up to 9.75%) is among the highest in the nation.

Virginia vs. Kentucky imposes a flat 4% individual income tax rate, making it one of the simpler and cheaper neighboring options. On a $75,000 income, you'd save approximately $1,200 annually compared to Virginia.

Virginia-Specific Tax Situations to Watch For

There are several Virginia-specific tax scenarios that catch people off guard. I've compiled these based on our testing methodology and research into Virginia Department of Taxation guidance:

Active-duty military stationed in Virginia but domiciled elsewhere don't owe Virginia income tax on their military pay., non-military income earned in Virginia is taxable. Virginia also allows a $15,000 military basic pay subtraction for service members stationed in a combat zone.

If you live in Virginia but work remotely for an out-of-state employer, you generally owe Virginia income tax on all your income. Virginia doesn't have a convenience-of-the-employer rule like New York, so your tax situation is relatively straightforward.

Part-year residents: If you moved to or from Virginia during the year, you'll file as a part-year resident and prorate your Virginia income. Our calculator assumes full-year residency - adjust manually if you moved mid-year.

How This Calculator Works Under the Hood

Common Mistakes When Estimating Virginia Take-Home Pay

After building and testing dozens of salary calculators, I've identified the most common mistakes people make:

  • Confusing marginal vs. effective rate: Virginia's top bracket is 5.75%, but your effective rate is lower because the first $17,000 is taxed at lower rates.
  • Many people only think about income tax, but FICA takes 7.65% right off the top. On $75,000 that's $5,737.
  • Not accounting for pre-tax deductions: If you contribute to a 401(k) or pay health insurance pre-tax, your taxable income is lower than your gross salary.
  • Using the wrong year's brackets: Tax brackets adjust annually for inflation. Make sure you're using 2025 figures.
  • Ignoring pay frequency: Your per-paycheck amount varies dramatically between weekly (52) and monthly (12) schedules.

Virginia Salary Ranges by Industry and Region

Understanding where your salary falls relative to Virginia averages can help you contextualize your tax burden. Northern Virginia - encompassing Fairfax County, Arlington, Loudoun, and Prince William - commands the highest salaries in the state, driven by federal government contractors, technology companies, and consulting firms. The average household income in Fairfax County exceeds $130,000, making it one of the wealthiest counties in the entire United States. In contrast, rural areas of Southwest Virginia and the Shenandoah Valley see average household incomes closer to $45,000, $60,000.

For technology workers in the Northern Virginia corridor - particularly those in cybersecurity, cloud computing, and defense contracting - starting salaries commonly range from $80,000 to $120,000, with senior positions reaching $150,000, $200,000+. At these income levels, Virginia's 5.75% top rate is noticeably cheaper than what the same worker would pay across the river in Maryland (5.75% state plus 3.2% Montgomery County) or in DC (8.5% on income in that range).

Healthcare professionals in the Hampton Roads area (Norfolk, Virginia Beach, Newport News) typically earn $60,000, $120,000 depending on specialization. Military families stationed at the large bases in this region - Naval Station Norfolk, Joint Base Langley-Eustis, Fort Barfoot - benefit from Virginia's military pay exclusions and the absence of local income taxes.

For state government employees in Richmond, salaries generally range from $40,000 to $90,000. These workers can take advantage of the Virginia Retirement System (VRS), which provides both a traditional pension and optional hybrid retirement plan with employer-matched contributions. VRS pension income is taxable by Virginia in retirement, but the age 65+ deduction can offset a significant portion.

The Impact of Virginia's No Local Income Tax Policy

One of Virginia's most underappreciated tax advantages is the complete absence of local income taxes. While neighboring Maryland levies county income taxes ranging from 2.25% in Worcester County to 3.2% in most Washington suburban counties, Virginia localities rely instead on property taxes, personal property taxes (particularly on vehicles), and sales tax revenue. This structural difference means that a $100,000 earner in Fairfax County, Virginia pays roughly $2,400, $3,200 less in total income taxes than an equivalent earner in adjacent Montgomery County, Maryland.

Virginia's personal property tax on vehicles - often called the "car tax" - is the most visible local tax that catches transplants off guard. Annual assessments are based on the vehicle's market value, and rates vary by locality but typically run 1%, 5% of the car's assessed value. A $35,000 vehicle in Fairfax County might generate a $1,200 annual personal property tax bill. The state provides partial relief through the Personal Property Tax Relief Act (PPTRA), but the remaining bill still surprises many new Virginia residents. Despite this, the total tax burden - income plus vehicle property tax - remains lower than Maryland's combined income tax plus local income tax for most families.

Virginia Tax Filing Tips and Deadlines

Virginia individual income tax returns are due May 1, which gives residents an extra two weeks beyond the federal April 15 deadline. This is an unusual grace period that most states don't provide, and it can be useful if you're waiting on federal refund information before completing your state return.

Virginia offers free electronic filing through its iFile system at tax.virginia.gov, supporting both resident and part-year resident returns. The state also participates in the Free File Alliance for taxpayers with AGI below $79,000. Estimated tax payments are due quarterly (May 1, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year) if you expect to owe more than $150 in state tax after withholding.

For workers with side income, freelance earnings, or investment income not subject to withholding, the estimated payment schedule is critical. Virginia charges a penalty for underpayment of estimated taxes - so if your calculator results show a significant gap between your withholding and actual liability, it's worth making quarterly payments to avoid that penalty. Many people I've talked to don't realize this until they get hit with the underpayment charge on their first Virginia return.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the Virginia state income tax brackets for 2025?
Virginia has four progressive tax brackets: 2% on the first $3,000, 3% on $3,001, $5,000, 5% on $5,001, $17,000, and 5.75% on income over $17,000. These rates apply to taxable income after deductions and exemptions.
How much is the Virginia standard deduction?
The Virginia standard deduction is $8,000 for single filers and $16,000 for married filing jointly. Taxpayers age 65 or older receive an additional $800 personal exemption on top of the standard $930 personal exemption.
Does Virginia tax retirement income?
Virginia taxes most retirement income including 401(k) distributions, IRA withdrawals, and pension income., taxpayers age 65+ may qualify for an age deduction of up to $12,000. Social Security benefits are fully exempt from Virginia state income tax.
How does Virginia's tax rate compare to neighboring states?
Virginia's top rate of 5.75% is moderate compared to neighbors. Maryland's top rate is 5.75% plus local taxes up to 3.2%. DC ranges up to 10.75%. West Virginia goes up to 6.5%. North Carolina has a flat 4.5%. Tennessee has no state income tax. Kentucky has a flat 4% rate.
What is the FICA tax rate in Virginia?
FICA taxes are federal and the same in all states: 6.2% for Social Security (on income up to $168,600 in 2025) and 1.45% for Medicare. An additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies to income over $200,000 for single filers.
Can I reduce my Virginia taxable income with 401(k) contributions?
Yes. Traditional 401(k) contributions reduce both your federal and Virginia state taxable income. The 2025 contribution limit is $23,500 ($31,000 if age 50+). Health insurance premiums paid pre-tax also reduce your taxable income.
Does Virginia have local income taxes?
No, Virginia does not impose local or city income taxes. Unlike Maryland, which has county-level income taxes ranging from 2.25% to 3.2%, Virginia residents only pay the state income tax., Virginia does have local personal property taxes on vehicles.

Understanding Virginia Salary and Tax Structure

Virginia uses a progressive state income tax system with four brackets. The rates are 2 percent on the first $3,000 of taxable income, 3 percent on income from $3,001 to $5,000, 5 percent on income from $5,001 to $17,000, and 5.75 percent on all taxable income above $17,000. Because the top bracket begins at just $17,000, the vast majority of working Virginians pay an effective state rate that approaches 5.75 percent on most of their income. Virginia's standard deduction is $8,000 for single filers and $16,000 for married couples filing jointly, and each personal exemption reduces taxable income by $930 per person claimed.

Virginia employees face the standard set of federal payroll deductions including federal income tax withholding, Social Security tax at 6.2 percent on wages up to the annual base, and Medicare tax at 1.45 percent (with the additional 0.9 percent surtax on earnings above $200,000 for single filers). Virginia does not impose local income taxes at the city or county level for most workers, although some localities levy taxes on specific types of business income. The state's overall tax structure is considered moderate compared to other East Coast states, with the combination of state income tax, property taxes, and sales taxes (the general rate is 5.3 percent, with an additional 0.7 percent in certain regions) creating a middle-of-the-road tax burden.

Virginia's proximity to Washington, D.C. creates unique salary and tax considerations for the many residents who commute across state lines for work. Virginia has a reciprocity agreement with the District of Columbia, Maryland, Kentucky, Pennsylvania, and West Virginia, which means that Virginia residents working in these jurisdictions are taxed only by Virginia on their earned income. This reciprocity simplifies tax filing and withholding for the large population of Northern Virginia residents who work in D.C. or Maryland. However, residents should ensure their employer withholds Virginia taxes rather than the state where they work, as correcting withholding errors across multiple state returns can be complex and time-consuming.

Practical Applications for Virginia Salary Planning

Virginia's economy is diverse and driven by several major sectors including the federal government and defense contracting, technology, health care, agriculture, and tourism. The Northern Virginia region, encompassing Fairfax County, Arlington County, and Loudoun County, is one of the wealthiest areas in the nation and home to major employers such as Amazon (HQ2), Boeing's defense division, Northrop Grumman, Capital One, and numerous government agencies and military installations. Salaries in Northern Virginia tend to be significantly higher than in other parts of the state, reflecting the region's high cost of living and intense competition for skilled workers in technology and defense.

For a worker earning $90,000 in Virginia, the approximate annual deduction breakdown includes roughly $12,000 to $15,000 in federal income tax, about $4,500 in Virginia state income tax (after accounting for the standard deduction and personal exemptions), $5,580 in Social Security tax, and $1,305 in Medicare tax. The resulting take-home pay of approximately $54,000 to $57,000 translates to roughly $2,250 to $2,375 per biweekly paycheck before voluntary deductions. These figures can vary significantly based on filing status, number of dependents, pre-tax contributions, and other individual circumstances.

Virginia residents should pay particular attention to the interaction between state and federal deductions. Virginia conforms to many federal tax provisions, but there are important differences. For example, Virginia allows a deduction for contributions to Virginia529 education savings plans, which can reduce state taxable income by up to $4,000 per account per year (with unlimited deductions for taxpayers aged 70 and older). The state also offers a deduction for long-term care insurance premiums. These Virginia-specific deductions can meaningfully reduce your state tax liability and should be factored into any comprehensive salary and tax planning analysis.
