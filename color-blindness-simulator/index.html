Understanding Color Vision Deficiency

Color vision deficiency (CVD), commonly called color blindness, is a condition where a person's ability to distinguish certain colors is reduced. It affects approximately 8% of males and 0.5% of females of Northern European descent, meaning roughly 300 million people worldwide experience some form of altered color perception. For designers, developers, and content creators, understanding CVD is essential for creating accessible digital experiences.

Human color vision relies on three types of cone cells in the retina, each sensitive to different wavelengths of light: short (blue), medium (green), and long (red). Color vision deficiency occurs when one or more types of cone cells are absent, reduced in number, or shifted in their spectral sensitivity. This tool simulates how images and colors appear to people with each major type of CVD, helping you design with inclusivity in mind.

Red-Green Color Blindness

The most common forms of CVD affect the ability to distinguish red and green hues. Protanopia (absence of red cones) and deuteranopia (absence of green cones) are the complete forms, where the affected cone type is entirely missing. People with protanopia see reds as dark and may confuse red with black. Those with deuteranopia have difficulty distinguishing green from red and brown. The anomalous forms, protanomaly and deuteranomaly, involve shifted rather than absent cones, resulting in reduced but not eliminated color discrimination.

Deuteranomaly is by far the most common form, affecting about 6% of males. When designing interfaces, avoid relying solely on red-green color differences to convey information. For example, a form validation system that only uses red and green to indicate errors and success will be invisible to users with red-green CVD. Adding icons, text labels, or patterns alongside color ensures the information reaches everyone.

Blue-Yellow Color Blindness

Tritanopia (absence of blue cones) and tritanomaly (reduced blue cone sensitivity) are much rarer, affecting fewer than 1 in 10,000 people. People with tritanopia have difficulty distinguishing blue from green and yellow from violet. Unlike red-green CVD, tritanopia affects males and females equally because the gene responsible is located on chromosome 7, not the X chromosome.

Complete Color Blindness

Achromatopsia is an extremely rare condition (approximately 1 in 33,000) where all cone cells are absent or nonfunctional. People with achromatopsia see only in shades of gray, experience extreme light sensitivity, and typically have reduced visual acuity. Achromatomaly is a milder form where cone function is severely reduced but not entirely absent, resulting in very limited color perception with a heavy gray overlay.

Color Transformation Matrices

This simulator uses mathematically derived color transformation matrices based on research by Brettel, Vienot, and Machado. Each matrix maps RGB color values from normal vision to the simulated CVD type. For the complete deficiency types (protanopia, deuteranopia, tritanopia, achromatopsia), the transformations are well-established and scientifically validated. The anomalous types (protanomaly, deuteranomaly, tritanomaly, achromatomaly) use interpolation between the normal vision matrix and the corresponding complete deficiency matrix at a severity factor of approximately 0.6.

WCAG and Color Accessibility

The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines (WCAG) 2.1 establish minimum contrast ratios for text legibility. Level AA compliance requires a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text (under 18pt or 14pt bold) and 3:1 for large text. Level AAA, the highest standard, requires 7:1 for normal text and 4.5:1 for large text. These ratios ensure text remains readable for users with low vision, color vision deficiency, and those viewing screens in bright environments.

The contrast ratio is calculated using the relative luminance of two colors, defined as L = 0.2126 * R + 0.7152 * G + 0.0722 * B where R, G, and B are linearized sRGB values. The ratio is (L1 + 0.05) / (L2 + 0.05) where L1 is the lighter color and L2 is the darker. This tool computes this ratio and evaluates it against all four WCAG thresholds, giving you immediate feedback on whether your color combination meets accessibility standards.

Frequently Asked Questions

What types of color blindness can this simulate?▾

Eight types are supported: Protanopia (no red cones), Deuteranopia (no green cones), Tritanopia (no blue cones), Protanomaly (weak red perception), Deuteranomaly (weak green perception), Tritanomaly (weak blue perception), Achromatopsia (total color blindness with no functioning cones), and Achromatomaly (severely reduced color perception across all cones).

How does the simulation work?▾

The tool reads each pixel of your image via the HTML5 Canvas API and applies a scientifically-derived color transformation matrix. These 3x3 matrices model how each type of color vision deficiency remaps RGB values, based on research by Brettel, Vienot, and Machado. The processing happens entirely in your browser with no server involvement.

Can I test specific colors without uploading an image?▾

Yes. Switch to the "Color Check" tab to enter two colors using a color picker or hex code input. The tool will show you how that color pair appears under all eight types of color vision deficiency, making it easy to check if your chosen colors remain distinguishable for all users.

What is WCAG color contrast?▾

WCAG (Web Content Accessibility Guidelines) defines minimum contrast ratios between foreground and background colors. Level AA requires 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Level AAA requires 7:1 for normal text and 4.5:1 for large text. Meeting these thresholds ensures your text is readable for people with low vision and color vision deficiency.

Is my image uploaded to a server?▾

No. All image processing happens entirely within your browser using the Canvas API and JavaScript. Your images are never transmitted to any server. You can verify this by disconnecting from the internet after the page loads and confirming the tool continues to work.

What image formats are supported?▾

All formats your browser supports natively, including PNG, JPEG, GIF, WebP, SVG, and BMP. There is no file size limit imposed by the tool, though very large images (over 4000 pixels wide) may take a moment to process as each pixel is individually transformed.

How common is color blindness?▾

Approximately 8% of males and 0.5% of females of Northern European descent have some form of color vision deficiency. Deuteranomaly (weak green perception) is the most prevalent, affecting about 6% of males. Globally, that translates to roughly 300 million people who experience altered color perception in their daily lives.

How can I make my designs more accessible?▾

Use sufficient contrast ratios (at least WCAG AA). Never rely on color alone to convey meaning - add icons, patterns, or text labels alongside color indicators. Test your designs with this simulator across all major CVD types. Use colorblind-safe palettes when possible, and consider tools like pattern fills for charts and graphs.

Color blindness, also known as color vision deficiency (CVD), is the decreased ability to see color or differences in color. The most common cause of color blindness is an inherited problem or variation in the functionality of one or more of the three classes of cone cells in the retina, which mediate color vision. Males are more likely to be color blind than females, as the genes responsible for the most common forms of color blindness are on the X chromosome.
