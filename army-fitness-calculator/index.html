Mental Preparation for the ACFT

Physical readiness only accounts for part of your ACFT performance. Mental preparation plays an equally significant role in achieving your target scores. I have seen soldiers who were physically capable of scoring 500+ fall short because they did not approach test day with the right mindset.

Start your mental preparation several weeks before the test. Visualize yourself completing each event successfully. Run through the sequence in your mind: the three-repetition maximum deadlift, standing power throw, hand-release push-ups, sprint-drag-carry, plank or leg tuck, and the two-mile run. Picture your form, your breathing, and your pacing for each one.

Sleep quality in the 72 hours before the test matters more than most soldiers realize. Aim for seven to nine hours each night during that window. Avoid alcohol for at least 48 hours before the test. Hydrate consistently in the days leading up, not just the morning of. Your muscles need adequate glycogen stores, so eat balanced meals with complex carbohydrates, lean protein, and healthy fats.

On test morning, arrive early enough to complete a thorough warm-up. adaptable stretching, light jogging, and event-specific activation movements prepare your body and mind. Rushing through the warm-up or skipping it entirely is one of the most common mistakes I see on test day.

Building an Effective Unit PT Program Around ACFT Standards

Unit physical training programs need to address all six ACFT events systematically. The old approach of running three days a week with occasional push-up sessions does not prepare soldiers for the diverse demands of the ACFT. A well-structured program cycles through strength, power, endurance, and agility work across the training week.

Monday sessions might focus on deadlift progressions and upper body pushing movements. Wednesday could address the sprint-drag-carry through interval training and functional circuits. Friday might combine distance running with plank holds and power throw practice. This periodization ensures soldiers develop across all six domains rather than excelling in two events while struggling in others.

Track individual soldier scores over time using this calculator after each diagnostic ACFT. Identifying weak events early allows for targeted supplemental training. A soldier who consistently scores below 70 on the sprint-drag-carry needs different programming than one who struggles with the two-mile run. Personalized attention based on data from diagnostic tests produces faster improvements than generic group workouts.

Recovery protocols deserve as much attention as the training itself. Incorporate mobility work, foam rolling, and active recovery sessions into the weekly schedule. Overtraining leads to injuries that set soldiers back weeks or months. The goal is progressive overload with adequate recovery, building fitness gradually rather than attempting rapid gains that increase injury risk.
