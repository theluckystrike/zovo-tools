# Washington's No State Income Tax Advantage

I've analyzed salary calculations across all 50 states, and Washington consistently comes out among the top for take-home pay. The reason is straightforward: Washington state doesn't levy a personal income tax. This puts it in an exclusive group of nine states (along with Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, and Wyoming) that let workers keep more of their earnings.

For a worker earning $100,000, the difference is substantial. In Washington, your state income tax bill is $0. In neighboring Oregon, you'd pay roughly $7,800 in state income tax on the same salary. In California, you'd lose about $5,500 to state taxes. Over a 30-year career, choosing Washington over a high-tax state can mean hundreds of thousands of dollars in savings. I've run these numbers repeatedly, and the compounding effect of keeping that extra income is significant.

The no-income-tax status is enshrined in Washington's constitution and has been upheld by the state Supreme Court multiple times. In 2023, the court upheld a capital gains tax (7% on gains over $250,000 from stocks and bonds), but this was classified as an excise tax, not an income tax. For the vast majority of wage earners, Washington remains a zero-state-income-tax state.

Washington funds its government primarily through sales tax (6.5% state rate, plus local additions that can bring it to 10.25% in Seattle), property taxes, and the Business and Occupation (B&O) tax. This means you don't escape taxes entirely by living in Washington. You just pay them differently. The sales tax is regressive, meaning it takes a larger percentage of income from lower earners. Various policy proposals have attempted to introduce an income tax, but voters have consistently rejected them.

For tech workers considering a move from San Francisco to Seattle, the salary math often works out favorably even with Seattle's higher cost of living compared to other WA cities. I tested the numbers, and a $150,000 salary in Seattle gives you roughly the same take-home as a $170,000 salary in San Francisco when you account for California's state income tax. That's a meaningful difference that doesn't require a raise to achieve.

# 2025 Federal Tax Brackets

Since Washington has no state income tax, federal taxes are your primary deduction. Understanding the 2025 federal tax brackets helps you see exactly where your money goes. I've found that many people misunderstand how progressive taxation works, so let me clarify: you don't pay the top rate on all your income. Each bracket applies only to the income within that range.

The standard deduction for 2025 is $15,000 for single filers and $30,000 for married filing jointly. This amount is subtracted from your gross income before tax brackets are applied. So if you earn $85,000 as a single filer, your taxable income is $70,000. The first $11,925 is taxed at 10%, the next chunk up to $48,475 at 12%, and the remainder at 22%. Your effective tax rate ends up being much lower than 22%.

For Washington residents, the standard deduction matters even more because you can't deduct state income taxes (there aren't any). However, you can still deduct property taxes up to $10,000, which is relevant given Washington's above-average property tax rates. The choice between standard and itemized deductions depends on your specific situation, but the higher standard deductions have made itemizing less beneficial for most filers.

# FICA and Medicare Explained

FICA (Federal Insurance Contributions Act) taxes are the second major deduction from your Washington paycheck. Everyone pays these regardless of which state they live in. FICA consists of two parts: Social Security tax at 6.2% of wages up to $176,100 (the 2025 wage base) and Medicare tax at 1.45% on all wages with no cap.

For high earners, there's an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% on wages exceeding $200,000 for single filers ($250,000 for married filing jointly). This means if you're a single filer earning $300,000 in Washington, you'd pay 1.45% Medicare on the first $200,000 and 2.35% on the remaining $100,000. Combined with federal income tax, your total effective rate can climb into the 30-35% range even without state income tax.

Social Security tax has a wage base cap that adjusts annually for inflation. In 2025, you stop paying Social Security tax once your wages reach $176,100. Every dollar above that threshold is only subject to Medicare tax (and the additional Medicare tax if applicable). This cap is why very high earners often have a lower FICA effective rate than middle-income workers.

Your employer matches your FICA contribution dollar for dollar. So while you see 7.65% (6.2% + 1.45%) deducted from your paycheck, your total FICA contribution including the employer match is 15.3%. Self-employed workers in Washington pay both halves through self-employment tax, which is why freelancers often experience sticker shock when they realize they owe 15.3% on net self-employment income before even getting to income tax.

I should mention that Washington has a unique state program called WA Cares, which is a long-term care insurance program funded by a payroll premium of 0.58% on employee wages. This isn't technically a tax, but it does reduce your take-home pay slightly. The calculator above includes this as an optional deduction. Workers who had qualifying private long-term care insurance before November 2021 could opt out, but the window for new exemptions has closed.

# How Washington Compares to Other States

I've calculated take-home pay across all 50 states, and Washington consistently ranks in the top tier. The advantage of no state income tax is most apparent at higher salary levels where progressive state tax rates would bite hardest. Here's how Washington stacks up against its neighbors and other major states based on our testing.

Washington vs Oregon is the comparison that matters most for Pacific Northwest residents. Oregon has no sales tax but imposes a state income tax up to 9.9%, one of the highest in the nation. For a $100,000 salary, you'd take home roughly $5,000 more in Washington than Oregon. However, Oregon's lack of sales tax partially offsets this for high spenders. I've found that the breakeven point depends heavily on your spending habits. If you spend most of your income (rather than saving it), Oregon's no-sales-tax benefit narrows the gap.

Washington vs California is a dramatic comparison. California's top marginal rate is 13.3%, the highest in the country. Even at moderate income levels, California's state tax takes a significant bite. A tech worker earning $200,000 would save over $15,000 annually in state taxes by choosing Washington over California. This math has driven significant migration of tech companies from the Bay Area to the Seattle area over the past decade.

Washington vs Texas is interesting because both have no state income tax. The difference comes down to cost of living and other taxes. Texas has no state income tax and lower property costs in most areas, but Washington has higher median wages (especially in the Seattle tech corridor). Texas also has higher property tax rates, which can offset the income tax advantage for homeowners.

Among neighboring states, Idaho (1% to 5.8% state income tax) and Montana (1% to 6.75%) also cost more in state taxes compared to Washington. This creates a notable adaptable where many workers live in Washington's border cities and commute to neighboring states, or vice versa. If you work in Washington but live in Oregon or Idaho, your state tax situation depends on where you work, not where you live.

# Cost of Living in Major Washington Cities

Your take-home pay is only half the equation. The other half is how far that money goes in your city. Washington has one of the most dramatic cost-of-living ranges of any state, from expensive Seattle to much more affordable cities in the eastern part of the state. I've researched the latest data to give you a realistic picture.

Seattle is the most expensive metro in Washington, with a cost of living index of roughly 149 (where 100 is the national average). Housing drives most of this. Median home prices in Seattle hover around $850,000, and average rent for a one-bedroom apartment is approximately $2,100. However, Seattle salaries are also significantly higher than the national average, particularly in tech, healthcare, and finance. The city's minimum wage of $20.76 per hour (as of 2025) is among the highest in the nation.

Tacoma, located about 30 miles south of Seattle, offers a meaningful discount. The cost of living index is approximately 115, with median home prices around $480,000 and average one-bedroom rent around $1,500. Many workers commute from Tacoma to Seattle, saving on housing while accepting a longer commute. The Sound Transit light rail expansion is expected to improve this corridor significantly by 2030.

Spokane, on the eastern side of the state, is the most affordable major city in Washington. The cost of living index is approximately 95, actually below the national average. Median home prices are around $375,000, and one-bedroom rent averages $1,100. Spokane has grown significantly in recent years as remote workers discovered they could earn Seattle or Bay Area salaries while living in a much more affordable city. The catch is fewer local job opportunities in high-paying industries.

Other notable cities include Olympia (the state capital, COL index around 108), Bellevue (extremely expensive at around 170, driven by tech headquarters), Bellingham (growing college town, COL index around 118), and the Tri-Cities area of Kennewick, Richland, and Pasco (very affordable at around 92, with a strong economy driven by the Hanford site and agriculture).

# Washington B&O Tax for Businesses

If you're a business owner or self-employed in Washington, there's an important tax you won't find in most other states: the Business and Occupation (B&O) tax. This is worth mentioning because it affects your actual take-home as a business owner and can influence your decision about where to incorporate.

The B&O tax is a gross receipts tax, meaning it's calculated on total revenue, not profit. This is unusual and can be burdensome for businesses with thin margins. The rates vary by business type: retailing is 0.471%, service and other is 1.5%, and manufacturing is 0.484%. For a consulting business with $500,000 in revenue, the B&O tax would be $7,500 (1.5%) regardless of whether the business made a profit.

Small businesses get a B&O tax credit that eliminates the tax for businesses with less than $125,000 in annual gross income (as of 2025). This helps sole proprietors and small freelancers, but the credit phases out quickly. If you're earning above the threshold, you'll feel the B&O tax. I've talked to many business owners who didn't factor this into their "no income tax" calculations and were surprised at the cost.

The B&O tax doesn't directly affect employees. If you work for someone else, your employer pays it, and it doesn't show up on your paycheck. It's relevant for business owners, independent contractors, and anyone thinking about incorporating in Washington versus another state. Some businesses structure operations to reduce B&O tax exposure, such as locating their wholesale operations in Washington while handling retail in other states.

Washington also imposes a 7% capital gains tax on the sale of stocks, bonds, and other long-term capital assets when gains exceed $250,000 per year. This tax, upheld by the state Supreme Court in 2023 as an excise tax, primarily affects high-net-worth individuals and won't apply to most salary earners. However, if you're exercising stock options from a tech company, this is something you plan for.

# Frequently Asked Questions

Does Washington state really have no income tax?
Correct. Washington has no personal income tax on wages, salaries, or retirement income. The state constitution has been interpreted to prohibit a graduated income tax. Washington does have a 7% capital gains tax on gains exceeding $250,000 from stocks and bonds, but this was classified as an excise tax by the state Supreme Court, not an income tax.
What taxes do Washington residents pay instead?
Washington relies on sales tax (6.5% state, plus local rates up to 10.25%), property taxes (averaging about 0.84% of assessed value), and the Business and Occupation (B&O) tax on business gross receipts. There's also a real estate excise tax on property sales. The overall tax structure is often criticized as regressive because sales tax impacts lower-income residents more heavily as a percentage of their income.
I live in Oregon but work in Washington. Which state taxes me?
You'd pay Oregon state income tax on your wages because Oregon taxes its residents on all income regardless of where it's earned. However, Washington won't tax your income at all. The reverse scenario (living in Washington, working in Oregon) means you'd owe Oregon income tax on your Oregon-sourced wages. This border adaptable is why many people choose to live in Washington and commute to Oregon for work.
How does Seattle's minimum wage affect salary calculations?
Seattle has one of the highest minimum wages in the US at $20.76/hour as of 2025 (for large employers). This means the minimum annual full-time salary in Seattle is approximately $43,180. Washington state's minimum wage is $16.66/hour, also among the highest nationally. Both are significantly above the federal minimum of $7.25/hour.
What is Washington's WA Cares payroll premium?
WA Cares is a state-run long-term care insurance program funded by a 0.58% payroll premium on employee wages (no employer match). It provides up to $36,500 in lifetime long-term care benefits. Workers who had qualifying private long-term care insurance could opt out during the initial exemption window, but that window is now closed for most workers. Self-employed individuals can opt in voluntarily.
Is it cheaper to live in Spokane or Seattle?
Spokane is significantly cheaper. Housing costs in Spokane are roughly 55-60% of Seattle's, and the overall cost of living in Spokane is below the national average while Seattle's is well above it. However, Seattle offers much higher salaries, especially in tech. A $120,000 tech salary in Seattle might translate to $85,000-$95,000 for a comparable role in Spokane, though remote work is changing this equation.
How does the 401(k) contribution affect my taxes?
Traditional 401(k) contributions are pre-tax, meaning they reduce your taxable income. If you earn $100,000 and contribute 10% ($10,000) to your 401(k), you're taxed on $90,000 instead. In Washington, this only reduces federal tax since there's no state income tax. Roth 401(k) contributions are post-tax and don't reduce your current taxable income but grow tax-free.
