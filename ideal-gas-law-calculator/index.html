STP and SATP Reference

Standard conditions are used as reference points in gas calculations. There are two commonly used standards.

STP (Standard Temperature and Pressure)

Temperature: 0 °C (273.15 K)
Pressure: 1 atm (101.325 kPa)
Molar volume: 22.414 L/mol
Used in most general chemistry courses.

SATP (Standard Ambient Temperature and Pressure)

Temperature: 25 °C (298.15 K)
Pressure: 1 bar (100 kPa)
Molar volume: 24.790 L/mol
IUPAC recommended standard since 1982.

At STP, one mole of any ideal gas occupies exactly 22.414 liters. This is a useful shortcut for stoichiometric calculations involving gases at standard conditions. At SATP, the molar volume increases to 24.790 liters because of the higher temperature.

Worked Examples

Working through example problems is the best way to build confidence with gas law calculations. Here are several common problem types with full solutions.

Example 1: Finding Pressure

Problem: A 2.50 L flask contains 0.100 mol of helium at 25.0 degrees C. What is the pressure?

Step 1: Convert temperature to Kelvin: T = 25.0 + 273.15 = 298.15 K
Step 2: Identify known values: V = 2.50 L, n = 0.100 mol, T = 298.15 K
Step 3: Rearrange PV = nRT for P: P = nRT / V
Step 4: Substitute: P = (0.100)(0.08206)(298.15) / (2.50)
Step 5: Calculate: P = 2.447 / 2.50 = 0.979 atm

Example 2: Finding Volume at STP

Problem: What volume does 2.00 mol of an ideal gas occupy at STP?

Step 1: At STP: T = 273.15 K, P = 1.000 atm
Step 2: Rearrange PV = nRT for V: V = nRT / P
Step 3: Substitute: V = (2.00)(0.08206)(273.15) / (1.000)
Step 4: Calculate: V = 44.83 L
Check: This is 2 times the molar volume at STP (22.414 L), which makes sense for 2 moles.

Example 3: Combined Gas Law

Problem: A gas occupies 3.00 L at 1.00 atm and 300 K. If the gas is compressed to 1.50 L and heated to 450 K, what is the new pressure?

Step 1: Identify: P1 = 1.00 atm, V1 = 3.00 L, T1 = 300 K, V2 = 1.50 L, T2 = 450 K
Step 2: Use P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2, solve for P2: P2 = P1V1T2 / (T1V2)
Step 3: Substitute: P2 = (1.00)(3.00)(450) / ((300)(1.50))
Step 4: Calculate: P2 = 1350 / 450 = 3.00 atm
Check: Volume halved (doubles P) and temperature increased 50% (increases P by 1.5x). 1.00 * 2 * 1.5 = 3.00 atm.

Example 4: Van der Waals vs Ideal

Problem: Calculate the pressure of 1.00 mol CO2 in a 0.500 L container at 300 K using both the ideal gas law and the Van der Waals equation.

Ideal gas: P = nRT/V = (1.00)(0.08206)(300) / (0.500) = 49.24 atm

Van der Waals: For CO2: a = 3.592 L2 atm/mol2, b = 0.04267 L/mol
P = nRT/(V - nb) - a(n/V)2
P = (1.00)(0.08206)(300) / (0.500 - 1.00 * 0.04267) - 3.592(1.00/0.500)2
P = 24.618 / 0.45733 - 3.592(4.000)
P = 53.83 - 14.37 = 39.46 atm

Difference: The ideal gas law overestimates pressure by 9.78 atm (24.8%). At this high density, intermolecular attractions significantly reduce the actual pressure. This shows why the Van der Waals equation matters for real gas behavior.

Individual Gas Laws

The ideal gas law combines several earlier gas laws discovered independently. Understanding each one helps clarify which variable relationships are at play in different situations.

Boyle's Law (1662)

P1V1 = P2V2 (at constant T and n). At constant temperature, pressure and volume are inversely proportional. Doubling the pressure halves the volume. Named after Robert Boyle.

Charles's Law (1787)

V1/T1 = V2/T2 (at constant P and n). At constant pressure, volume is directly proportional to absolute temperature. Named after Jacques Charles, who studied gas behavior during balloon flights.

Gay-Lussac's Law (1809)

P1/T1 = P2/T2 (at constant V and n). At constant volume, pressure is directly proportional to absolute temperature. This explains why tire pressure increases in hot weather.

Avogadro's Law (1811)

V1/n1 = V2/n2 (at constant P and T). At constant pressure and temperature, volume is directly proportional to the number of moles. Equal volumes of gas at the same T and P contain equal numbers of molecules.

How the Laws Combine

Each individual law holds one or two variables constant. The ideal gas law (PV = nRT) unifies all four into a single expression that relates pressure, volume, temperature, and amount of substance simultaneously. The gas constant R acts as the proportionality constant that makes the units work out correctly.

Common Unit Conversion Pitfalls

The most frequent errors in gas law calculations come from unit mismatches. Here are the conversions you need to remember.

  • Temperature must always be in Kelvin for gas law calculations. Add 273.15 to Celsius. Using Celsius or Fahrenheit directly will give wrong answers.
  • Pressure units must match the R constant you are using. If R is 0.08206, pressure must be in atm. If R is 8.314, pressure must be in kPa (or Pa with volume in cubic meters).
  • Volume must be in liters when using R = 0.08206 L atm/(mol K). One milliliter equals 0.001 liters. One cubic meter equals 1000 liters.
  • Amount of substance is in moles. If given mass in grams, divide by the molar mass to get moles before using the gas law.
  • Standard conditions vary by context. STP (0 C, 1 atm) is traditional. SATP (25 C, 1 bar) is the IUPAC recommendation. Always check which standard your problem uses.

Assumptions and Limitations

The ideal gas law is a powerful approximation, but understanding its assumptions helps you know when to trust the results and when to use more advanced models.

Key Assumptions of an Ideal Gas

  • Gas molecules have negligible volume compared to the container. This fails at high pressures where molecules are packed tightly.
  • There are no intermolecular attractive or repulsive forces between molecules. Real gases have Van der Waals forces, dipole interactions, and hydrogen bonds.
  • Collisions between molecules and container walls are perfectly elastic, meaning no energy is lost during collisions.
  • Molecules are in constant random motion and obey Newton's laws of motion.
  • The average kinetic energy of molecules is proportional to the absolute temperature.

When Does the Ideal Gas Law Fail?

ConditionWhy It FailsBetter Model
High pressure (above 10 atm)Molecular volume becomes significant relative to container volumeVan der Waals equation
Low temperature (near boiling point)Intermolecular attractions become strong enough to cause condensationVan der Waals or Redlich-Kwong
Polar molecules (H2O, NH3)Strong dipole-dipole forces violate the no-interaction assumptionVan der Waals with gas-specific constants
Very high densityBoth molecular volume and intermolecular forces matter simultaneouslyPeng-Robinson or virial equation
Near critical pointGas and liquid phases become indistinguishableCubic equations of state

As a rule of thumb, the ideal gas law works well when the gas is at temperatures well above its boiling point and pressures below 5-10 atmospheres. Monatomic noble gases (He, Ne, Ar) behave most ideally because their spherical symmetry minimizes intermolecular forces. Polar polyatomic molecules (H2O, NH3, SO2) deviate most from ideal behavior.

The Compressibility Factor (Z)

The compressibility factor Z measures how much a real gas deviates from ideal behavior: Z = PV/(nRT). For an ideal gas, Z = 1 at all conditions. Real gases have Z values that vary with pressure and temperature.

Z ValueMeaningDominant Effect
Z = 1.00Gas behaves ideallyAttractive and repulsive effects cancel
Z < 1.00Gas is more compressible than idealIntermolecular attractions dominate (moderate P)
Z > 1.00Gas is less compressible than idealMolecular volume effects dominate (very high P)

At very high pressures (hundreds of atmospheres), Z for all gases eventually exceeds 1.0 because the finite volume of the molecules themselves becomes the dominant factor. At moderate pressures, gases with strong intermolecular attractions (CO2, NH3, H2O) show Z values well below 1.0, meaning they occupy less volume than the ideal gas law predicts. Helium and hydrogen, with their weak intermolecular forces, have Z values closest to 1.0 across the widest range of conditions.

Comparison of Gas Equations

Beyond the ideal gas law and Van der Waals equation, several other equations of state are used in engineering and research. Each offers different tradeoffs between accuracy and complexity.

For most educational and general-purpose calculations, the ideal gas law and Van der Waals equation (both available in the calculators above) are sufficient. The Peng-Robinson equation is the industry standard for process engineering, particularly in oil and gas applications.

Frequently Asked Questions

The ideal gas law is PV = nRT, where P is pressure, V is volume, n is the number of moles, R is the universal gas constant (0.08206 L atm/(mol K)), and T is temperature in Kelvin. It describes the behavior of an ideal gas where molecules have negligible volume and no intermolecular forces.
The universal gas constant R has different values depending on the units used: 0.08206 L atm/(mol K), 8.314 J/(mol K), 8.314 kPa L/(mol K), 62.364 L mmHg/(mol K), or 1.987 cal/(mol K). The value 8.314 J/(mol K) is the SI standard.
The ideal gas law becomes inaccurate at high pressures (above roughly 10 atm), low temperatures (near a gas's boiling point), or for gases with strong intermolecular forces (like water vapor or ammonia). In these cases, the Van der Waals equation or other real gas models give better results.
STP stands for Standard Temperature and Pressure: 0 degrees C (273.15 K) and 1 atm (101.325 kPa). At STP, one mole of an ideal gas occupies 22.414 liters. IUPAC updated the standard to SATP: 25 degrees C (298.15 K) and 1 bar (100 kPa), where one mole occupies 24.790 liters.
The combined gas law is P1V1/T1 = P2V2/T2. It relates pressure, volume, and temperature of a fixed amount of gas between two states. It combines Boyle's law (pressure-volume at constant T), Charles's law (volume-temperature at constant P), and Gay-Lussac's law (pressure-temperature at constant V).
Dalton's law states that the total pressure of a gas mixture equals the sum of the partial pressures of each individual gas. Each partial pressure is the pressure that gas would exert if it alone occupied the entire volume at the same temperature. This is useful for analyzing gas mixtures and atmospheric composition.
