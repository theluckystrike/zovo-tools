How the Calculator Works

When you enter your salary, the calculator runs through several steps. First, it subtracts any pre-tax deductions like 401(k) contributions. Then it applies the 2025 federal income tax brackets, which range from 10% to 37%. After that, it calculates Missouri state tax using the progressive brackets listed above, applying the appropriate standard deduction. Finally, it adds FICA taxes, which include 6.2% for Social Security (capped at $176,100 in 2025) and 1.45% for Medicare.

The result is your estimated net take-home pay, broken down by pay period. You can toggle between weekly, bi-weekly, semi-monthly, and monthly views. I built this tool to handle edge cases like high earners who exceed the Social Security wage base, and it factors in the additional 0.9% Medicare surtax for income above $200,000.

All calculations happen client-side in JavaScript. I tested this against payroll software outputs from ADP and Gusto to make sure the numbers are within a few dollars of what you would actually see on your paycheck. The small differences come from rounding methods that vary between payroll providers.

Understanding Your Missouri Paycheck

When you look at your Missouri paycheck, you will see several deductions. Here is what each line means and why it is there.

Federal Income Tax Withholding

This is calculated based on your W-4 form selections. The amount withheld from each paycheck is approximate your annual federal tax liability spread across your pay periods. If too much is withheld, you get a refund when you file. If too little is withheld, you may owe at tax time. The W-4 form was redesigned in 2020 and no longer uses allowances. Instead, it asks about additional income, deductions, and dependents.

Missouri State Income Tax Withholding

Similar to federal withholding, this amount is based on your Missouri W-4 form (Form MO W-4). Missouri uses its own withholding tables that correspond to the progressive bracket rates I covered earlier. The withholding is match your expected annual state tax liability. One thing I've noticed is that Missouri withholding tends to be slightly higher than the actual liability for many workers, resulting in small state refunds.

Social Security Tax (OASDI)

This is a flat 6.2% on your gross earnings up to the Social Security wage base, which is $176,100 for 2025. Your employer also pays 6.2%, for a total of 12.4% going into the Social Security system. If you earn more than $176,100, you stop paying Social Security tax on the excess. Self-employed individuals pay the full 12.4% through self-employment tax.

Medicare Tax

Medicare is a flat 1.45% on all earnings with no cap. There is an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% on earnings above $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). Unlike Social Security, Medicare has no upper limit. Your employer matches the 1.45% but not the additional 0.9%.

Kansas City and St. Louis Earnings Tax

If you work within Kansas City or St. Louis city limits, you will see a 1% earnings tax deduction. This applies to all income earned in those cities, regardless of where you live. If you live in one of these cities but work outside it, you still owe the tax on any income earned within city limits. The Kansas City earnings tax is subject to periodic voter approval, which has been a contentious local political issue.

Retirement Planning in Missouri

Missouri offers several tax-advantaged retirement options that can reduce your current tax liability. Contributing to a traditional 401(k) reduces both your federal and Missouri state taxable income. For 2025, you can contribute up to $23,500 per year ($31,000 if you are 50 or older). Each dollar you contribute saves you both federal tax (at your marginal rate) and Missouri state tax (up to 4.95%).

Missouri also conforms to the federal treatment of IRA contributions. Traditional IRA contributions (up to $7,000, or $8,000 if 50+) are deductible if you meet income requirements. Roth contributions are not deductible but grow tax-free. Missouri fully exempts Social Security benefits from state income tax, which is a significant advantage for retirees. Public pension income may also qualify for a partial deduction.

If you max out a 401(k) at $23,500 and earn $85,000, you reduce your taxable income to $61,500 for both federal and state purposes. That drops your Missouri tax by about $1,163 and your federal tax by a similar amount. Over a 30-year career, those annual tax savings compound significantly when invested in your retirement account.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is Missouri's top income tax rate for 2025?

Missouri's top marginal income tax rate for 2025 is 4.95%, which applies to taxable income over $8,449. This is a reduction from the previous top rate, thanks to the state's ongoing tax reform that began in 2023. The rate is scheduled to potentially decrease further in future years if revenue triggers are met.

Does Missouri have local income taxes?

Yes. Both Kansas City and St. Louis impose a 1% earnings tax on income earned within city limits. This calculator focuses on state-level taxes, so if you work in either city, your actual take-home pay will be slightly lower. The Kansas City earnings tax has been subject to voter renewal and was most recently affirmed.

Can I deduct my federal taxes on my Missouri return?

Yes. Missouri is one of the few states that allows you to deduct a portion of your federal income tax liability from your state taxable income. This can result in meaningful tax savings, especially for higher earners. The deduction is limited to $5,000 for single filers and $10,000 for married filing jointly.

How accurate is this calculator compared to my actual paycheck?

This calculator typically comes within $5-15 per paycheck of the actual amount. Small differences arise from rounding conventions (some payroll systems round per-period while others round annually), employer-specific deductions, and local taxes. For the most precise figure, compare against your most recent pay stub.

Is Missouri a good state for taxes compared to neighboring states?

Missouri is reasonably competitive. Its top rate of 4.95% is lower than Illinois (4.95% flat on all income) and comparable to Kansas. It can't match the zero-income-tax states like Tennessee, Texas, or Florida, but the cost of living in Missouri is generally lower, which can offset the tax difference. The ability to deduct federal taxes is a unique advantage that few other states provide.

What is the Missouri standard deduction for 2025?

For 2025, Missouri uses $14,600 for single filers, $29,200 for married filing jointly, and $21,900 for head of household. These amounts mirror the federal standard deduction and are applied before the progressive tax brackets kick in.

Missouri Tax Credits and Deductions You Might Be Missing

Beyond the standard deduction, Missouri offers several tax credits that can reduce your tax liability. These are often overlooked by taxpayers who use basic filing software without reviewing available credits.

Property Tax Credit (Circuit Breaker)

Missouri's property tax credit is available to homeowners 65 and older, or those receiving Social Security disability. It provides a credit for a portion of property taxes paid, with the amount depending on income. For renters, 20% of rent paid is treated as property taxes for this credit. Maximum credit amounts range from $750 to $1,100 depending on filing status and number of qualified occupants.

Missouri Working Family Tax Credit

Missouri offers a non-refundable tax credit equal to 10% of the federal Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC). If you qualify for the federal EITC, you automatically qualify for this state credit. For a single parent with two children earning $40,000, this could mean an additional $600+ in state tax savings.

Health Insurance Premium Deduction

If you are self-employed and pay your own health insurance premiums, Missouri allows a deduction for those premiums on your state return. This mirrors the federal self-employed health insurance deduction and can save several hundred dollars in state taxes for freelancers and small business owners paying $500+ per month in premiums.

529 Education Savings Plan Deduction

Missouri allows a deduction of up to $8,000 per year ($16,000 for married couples) for contributions to a Missouri 529 plan (MOST plan). This is a dollar-for-dollar reduction in state taxable income, which at the 4.95% rate saves up to $396 per year for a single filer contributing the maximum. Any state's 529 plan qualifies for the federal tax advantages, but only contributions to Missouri's MOST plan qualify for the state deduction.

Guide for Freelancers and Self-Employed Workers in Missouri

If you are self-employed or freelancing in Missouri, your tax situation is more complex than a traditional W-2 employee. I've outlined the key differences and considerations.

Self-Employment Tax

Self-employed workers pay the full 15.3% FICA tax (both the employer and employee portions). On a net self-employment income of $75,000, that is $10,597 in self-employment tax alone, compared to $5,738 for a W-2 employee earning the same amount. You do get to deduct half of the self-employment tax from your federal adjusted gross income, which also reduces your Missouri taxable income.

Quarterly Estimated Payments

Missouri requires quarterly estimated tax payments if you expect to owe more than $100 in state income tax. The due dates align with federal quarterly dates: April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15. Failing to make estimated payments can result in underpayment penalties, even if you pay in full when you file your annual return.

Business Deductions That Reduce Missouri Taxes

Since Missouri taxable income starts from your federal adjusted gross income, any legitimate business deduction that reduces your federal income also reduces your Missouri state tax. Common deductions include home office expenses, vehicle mileage (67 cents per mile in 2024), professional development, software and equipment, and business insurance. Keeping thorough records is essential because Missouri can audit state returns independently of the IRS.

Military Pay and Missouri Taxes

Missouri is home to several major military installations, including Fort Leonard Wood, Whiteman Air Force Base, and the headquarters for U.S. Transportation Command at Scott Air Force Base (just across the border in Illinois). If you are military personnel stationed in Missouri, here is how taxes work for you.

Active-duty military members are taxed based on their state of legal residence, not their duty station. If Missouri is your home of record, your military pay is subject to Missouri income tax. However, Missouri provides a 100% deduction for military retirement pay, making it one of the better states for military retirees. Active-duty pay does not qualify for this deduction, but the state's relatively low rates keep the tax burden manageable.

Housing allowances (BAH) and subsistence allowances (BAS) are not taxed at either the federal or state level. For a service member earning a base pay of $50,000 with $18,000 in BAH, only the $50,000 base pay is subject to Missouri income tax. The BAH provides tax-free income that effectively increases your total compensation well beyond what the salary figure suggests.

Salary Negotiation Tips for Missouri Workers

Understanding Missouri's tax structure can give you an edge during salary negotiations. Here are some approaches I recommend when negotiating compensation in Missouri.

Use the Tax Advantage in Your Pitch

If you are a remote worker being recruited by a company headquartered in a high-tax state, your Missouri residency is a genuine cost advantage for the employer. In states like California or New York, employers pay higher payroll taxes, state disability insurance, and unemployment insurance. While your individual income tax is your concern, the employer's cost side is lower in Missouri. Some companies adjust salaries by location, but the best negotiating position is to push for the full salary while highlighting the mutual tax advantages.

Know the Market Rates

Missouri salaries for the same role can vary 15% to 25% between Kansas City, St. Louis, and outstate areas. If you are applying for a role in Kansas City, don't accept a Springfield-level salary. Research the specific metro area's compensation data through the Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Employment Statistics, which breaks down wages by metropolitan statistical area.

Total Compensation Matters More Than Base Salary

A $70,000 salary with no benefits costs more in total than a $65,000 salary with employer-paid health insurance and a 4% 401(k) match. The health insurance alone could be worth $7,000 to $10,000 annually, and the 401(k) match adds another $2,600. In Missouri, 401(k) contributions also reduce both your federal and state tax liability, creating a triple benefit: immediate savings, tax reduction, and compound growth.

Missouri Tax Filing Tips

Filing your Missouri state tax return is straightforward, but a few tips can save you time and possibly money.

File Electronically

Missouri accepts electronic filing through its MyTax Missouri portal and through most commercial tax software. E-filing is faster, reduces errors, and typically results in faster refunds. The state processes e-filed returns in 2 to 4 weeks compared to 8 to 12 weeks for paper returns.

Check for Pass-Through Entity Election

If you own a business organized as an S-corp, partnership, or LLC, Missouri allows a pass-through entity tax election. This lets the business pay state income tax at the entity level, which may allow the tax to be deducted on your federal return as a business expense rather than being limited by the $10,000 SALT deduction cap. This strategy can save thousands for business owners with high pass-through income.

Missouri Extension Rules

If you file a federal extension (Form 4868), Missouri automatically grants you a 6-month state extension. You do not need to file a separate state extension form. However, the extension is for filing only, not for payment. If you owe Missouri taxes, you should estimate and pay by April 15 to avoid interest and penalties.

Amended Returns

If you discover an error on a previously filed Missouri return, you can file an amended return using Form MO-1040. You have three years from the original filing date or two years from when the tax was paid, whichever is later. Common reasons to amend include discovering you missed a deduction, receiving a corrected W-2, or changing your filing status.

Working Across State Lines

Missouri shares borders with eight states, and cross-border employment is common, especially in the Kansas City and St. Louis metropolitan areas. Understanding how taxes work when you live in one state and work in another is essential for accurate planning.

Missouri-Kansas Reciprocity

Missouri and Kansas have a partial reciprocity agreement. If you live in Kansas and work in Missouri (or vice versa), you generally file a return in both states and receive a credit for taxes paid to the non-resident state. The credit prevents double taxation, but the mechanics of the credit mean you effectively pay the higher of the two state tax rates. Since Missouri's top rate (4.95%) is slightly lower than Kansas's top rate (5.7%), Kansas residents working in Missouri typically owe a small additional amount to Kansas.

Missouri-Illinois Cross-Border

The St. Louis metro area straddles the Missouri-Illinois border. Missouri and Illinois do not have a reciprocity agreement, so cross-border workers must file in both states and claim a credit. Illinois has a flat 4.95% tax rate, which is the same as Missouri's top rate. The practical difference depends on your income level, because Missouri's progressive structure means lower earners pay less in Missouri than they would under Illinois's flat tax.

Cross-border workers should also be aware of the St. Louis earnings tax (1%) if they work within city limits. This tax applies regardless of where you live and is not offset by credits in your home state. It is an additional cost on top of regular state income taxes.
