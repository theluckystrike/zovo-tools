How 1RM Formulas Work

Every one-rep max formula takes the same two inputs, weight and reps, and uses a mathematical relationship between load and repetition count to extrapolate the maximum load for a single rep. Here is how each of the seven formulas included in this calculator works.

Epley (1985)

One of the most widely cited formulas in strength training literature. The equation is 1RM = w × (1 + r / 30). It is linear, which means predictions scale up at a steady rate as reps increase. It tends to produce slightly higher estimates at rep counts above 10.

Brzycki (1993)

Matt Brzycki published this formula as 1RM = w × 36 / (37 - r). It closely matches Epley for 1 to 6 reps and is the basis for many percentage charts you see posted on gym walls. It becomes less reliable above 12 reps because the denominator approaches zero.

Lander

Lander's formula uses 1RM = 100 × w / (101.3 - 2.67123 × r). It produces moderate estimates that typically fall between Epley and Lombardi, making it a reasonable middle-ground choice.

Lombardi

Lombardi uses a power-law model: 1RM = w × r^0.10. Because the exponent is small, results grow slowly with rep count. This formula tends to give the most conservative estimates of the seven, particularly for sets of 8 or more reps.

Mayhew et al.

Mayhew's team developed an exponential regression model from bench press data: 1RM = 100 × w / (52.2 + 41.9 × e^(-0.055 × r)). It tends to produce mid-range estimates and is well-validated for upper-body pressing movements.

O'Conner et al.

A simple linear formula: 1RM = w × (1 + 0.025 × r). The 0.025 coefficient is smaller than Epley's 1/30, so O'Conner estimates come out lower. If you prefer a conservative target, this formula is a solid choice.

Wathen

Wathen's formula uses exponential decay: 1RM = 100 × w / (48.8 + 53.8 × e^(-0.075 × r)). The decay rate is steeper than Mayhew's, which means Wathen typically returns slightly higher values in the 3-to-8 rep range.

No single formula is universally best. Factors such as training background, muscle fiber composition, fatigue state, and which exercise you are testing all influence accuracy. Averaging all seven gives you a balanced number that smooths out any single formula's bias.

Tips for Accurate Estimates

Getting a reliable 1RM estimate depends on the quality of the data you feed into the calculator. Here are practical guidelines for the best results.

Use low rep counts

Sets of 3 to 5 reps produce the tightest agreement across all seven formulas. A 3-rep set is close enough to a true max that the math does not need to extrapolate far. A 5-rep set gives a solid data point while still keeping accuracy high.

Go to true failure (or very close)

The formulas assume you could not have performed one more rep with good technique. If you stopped two reps short of failure, the calculator will underestimate your max. Push to the point where one more rep would break form, not to the point of injury.

Warm up properly

A well-structured warm-up, such as 2 to 3 progressively heavier sets before your test set, ensures your muscles and nervous system are primed. Cold test sets lead to artificially low numbers and increase injury risk.

Test compound lifts

1RM formulas were developed and validated primarily on compound barbell movements like the bench press, squat, and deadlift. They are less accurate for isolation exercises such as bicep curls or lateral raises, where fatigue patterns differ.

Stay consistent with rest and conditions

If you plan to re-test periodically to track progress, try to replicate the same conditions each time: similar time of day, similar warm-up routine, and similar rest period before the test set. Consistency makes the comparison meaningful.

A 1 rep max is the maximum weight you can lift for a single repetition with proper form. It is the standard measure of absolute strength for a given exercise. Coaches and athletes use it to set training loads, track progress over time, and compare strength across different individuals or time periods.
For sets of 10 reps or fewer, most formulas predict within 5% of a tested 1RM for trained lifters. Accuracy drops for higher rep counts because muscular endurance starts to influence the result more than pure maximal strength. Using the average of multiple formulas, as this calculator does, further improves reliability.
Epley and Brzycki are the most widely validated in research. However, the best single formula varies by individual. Using the average of all seven formulas smooths out the quirks of any single model and gives you a well-rounded estimate to base your training on.
Testing a true 1RM carries real injury risk, especially for beginners and intermediate lifters. Estimating from a 3-to-5 rep set is safer and still gives you a useful working number. If you do test a true max, warm up thoroughly, use a competent spotter, and stop if your form breaks down.
