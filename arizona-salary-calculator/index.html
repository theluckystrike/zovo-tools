Arizona Flat State Income Tax

Arizona imposes a flat income tax rate of 2.5% on all taxable income. This is one of the lowest state income tax rates in the nation and applies equally to every income level. Whether you earn $30,000 or $300,000, the same 2.5% rate applies to your Arizona taxable income.

Arizona 2024 Tax at a Glance

  • State income tax rate: 2.5% flat
  • Local income taxes: None
  • Standard deduction (single): $14,600
  • Standard deduction (married joint): $29,200
  • Personal exemption: None (eliminated with flat tax)

The flat tax structure makes Arizona particularly attractive for high earners. In a state like California, where the top rate reaches 13.3%, a person earning $500,000 would pay over $50,000 in state income tax. In Arizona, that same person would pay about $11,765 — a difference of roughly $38,000 per year. This tax advantage is a significant factor in Arizona's population growth over the past several years.

No Local Income Taxes

Unlike states such as Maryland, Ohio, or Pennsylvania where cities and counties can add their own income taxes, Arizona prohibits local income taxes entirely. This means your Arizona tax calculation only involves two layers: federal and state. No city tax, no county tax, no school district income tax. This simplicity makes Arizona one of the easiest states for payroll processing.

History of Arizona Tax Reform

Arizona did not always have a flat tax. Understanding the history helps explain why the current system works the way it does.

Before 2023: Graduated Brackets

Prior to 2023, Arizona used a graduated income tax system with rates ranging from 2.59% to 4.50%. Higher earners paid proportionally more, similar to how most states with income taxes operate. The top bracket applied to income over $159,000 for single filers.

The Shift to a Flat Tax

In 2021, Arizona passed SB 1828, which established a roadmap to a flat income tax. The law set revenue triggers that would reduce the tax rate once state revenues hit certain thresholds. The rate dropped from the graduated system to 2.5% beginning in the 2023 tax year, ahead of the original schedule, because Arizona's revenues exceeded the trigger amounts.

Proposition 208 and Its Reversal

In 2020, Arizona voters passed Proposition 208, which would have added a 3.5% surcharge on high earners (income over $250,000 single / $500,000 joint) to fund education. However, the Arizona Supreme Court ruled key provisions of Prop 208 unconstitutional in 2023, and the flat tax legislation effectively superseded it. The education surcharge never took full effect.

The current 2.5% flat rate is expected to remain stable for the foreseeable future. Arizona's tax policy has become a selling point for attracting businesses and remote workers from higher-tax states.

FICA Taxes: Social Security and Medicare

FICA (Federal Insurance Contributions Act) taxes apply to all W-2 employees in every state. They fund Social Security and Medicare programs and are split between employee and employer.

Social Security Tax

The employee Social Security tax rate is 6.2% on wages up to the 2024 wage base of $168,600. Earnings above this cap are not subject to Social Security tax. Your employer pays a matching 6.2%. On a $70,000 salary, your Social Security tax is $4,340 per year.

Medicare Tax

The employee Medicare tax rate is 1.45% on all wages with no cap. Your employer matches this rate. If your wages exceed $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married joint), you pay an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax on the excess. The employer does not match this additional tax.

Combined FICA for Arizona Workers

For most Arizona workers, total employee FICA is 7.65% of gross wages. On a $70,000 annual salary, that amounts to $5,355 per year or about $205.96 per biweekly paycheck. FICA is one of the most predictable payroll deductions because it does not change based on filing status, deductions, or allowances.

Arizona-Specific Deductions

Arizona allows several deductions that are not available in all states:

  • Charitable contributions to school tuition organizations (STOs): Arizona provides a dollar-for-dollar tax credit (not just a deduction) for contributions to qualifying STOs. The credit limit is $1,459 for single filers and $2,435 for joint filers in 2024.
  • Contributions to public schools: A separate credit of up to $400 (single) or $800 (joint) for donations to Arizona public schools for extracurricular activities.
  • Qualifying charitable organizations: An additional credit of up to $470 (single) or $938 (joint) for donations to qualifying charities that serve the working poor.

These credits directly reduce your tax bill, not just your taxable income, making them particularly valuable. A married couple could reduce their Arizona tax by up to $4,173 through these credits alone.

Paycheck Calculation Example

Let us work through a complete example for a single filer in Arizona earning $70,000 per year, paid biweekly, with no 401(k) contributions.

Step 1: Federal Income Tax

Gross income: $70,000. Federal standard deduction: $14,600. Federal taxable income: $55,400.

  • 10% on first $11,600 = $1,160
  • 12% on $11,601 to $47,150 = $4,265.88
  • 22% on $47,151 to $55,400 = $1,814.78

Total federal tax: $7,240.66 per year, or $278.49 per biweekly paycheck.

Step 2: Arizona State Income Tax

Arizona taxable income: $70,000 - $14,600 standard deduction = $55,400. State tax: $55,400 x 2.5% = $1,385.00 per year, or $53.27 per biweekly paycheck.

Step 3: FICA Taxes

Social Security: 6.2% of $70,000 = $4,340. Medicare: 1.45% of $70,000 = $1,015. Total FICA: $5,355 per year, or $205.96 per biweekly paycheck.

Step 4: Net Pay

Annual gross: $70,000. Total deductions: $13,980.66. Annual net pay: $56,019.34. Biweekly net paycheck: $2,154.59. Effective total tax rate: 19.97%.

The effective tax rate under 20% is notably lower than what workers in high-tax states experience. A comparable earner in California would face an effective rate closer to 25-27%, primarily due to the much higher state income tax.

Why People Move to Arizona for Tax Savings

The combination of a low flat tax, no local income taxes, and a relatively affordable cost of living makes Arizona a top destination for workers relocating from California, Illinois, and New York. Remote workers in particular benefit — they can earn a California-level salary while paying Arizona tax rates. Arizona's population grew by over 750,000 between 2020 and 2024, and tax savings are consistently cited as a primary motivator.

Arizona Cost of Living Considerations

Take-home pay is only part of the picture. Your real spending power depends on what things cost where you live. Arizona's cost of living varies significantly by city.

Phoenix Metro Area

The Phoenix metro area (including Scottsdale, Tempe, Mesa, Chandler, and Gilbert) has seen significant cost increases since 2020, especially in housing. Median home prices in Phoenix reached approximately $420,000 in 2024. However, compared to Los Angeles ($860,000+) or San Francisco ($1.3 million+), Phoenix remains substantially more affordable. Rent for a one-bedroom apartment in Phoenix averages around $1,400 per month.

Tucson

Tucson is notably more affordable than Phoenix. Median home prices hover around $310,000, and one-bedroom apartment rent averages about $1,000. For workers who can be remote or work in Tucson's growing tech and defense sectors, the combination of low taxes and low cost of living makes it one of the best value propositions in the Southwest.

Sales Tax Consideration

While Arizona has no local income tax, it does have a state transaction privilege tax (TPT) of 5.6%, plus additional local TPT rates that vary by city. In Phoenix, the combined rate can reach 8.6%. In Scottsdale, it is about 8.05%. This is something to factor into your overall budget, especially for large purchases.

Arizona Payroll Laws and Regulations

Arizona has specific labor laws that affect how employees are paid. Here are the key rules every Arizona worker and employer should know.

Minimum Wage

Arizona's minimum wage for 2024 is $14.35 per hour. This is set by the state and adjusted annually for inflation based on the Consumer Price Index. Tipped employees must receive at least $11.35 per hour in direct wages, with tips making up the difference to the full minimum wage. Some cities have higher minimums — Flagstaff's minimum wage reached $17.40 per hour in 2024.

Pay Frequency

Arizona law (A.R.S. section 23-351) requires employers to pay employees at least twice per month (semi-monthly), no more than 16 days apart. Employers must establish regular paydays and notify employees of any changes. Employees in certain occupations, such as seasonal agricultural workers, may have different rules.

Overtime

Arizona follows federal FLSA rules for overtime. Non-exempt employees must be paid 1.5 times their regular rate for hours worked over 40 in a workweek. Arizona does not have its own overtime statute, so the federal thresholds and exemptions apply directly.

Final Paycheck

When an employee is terminated in Arizona, the employer must pay all wages due within 7 working days or by the next regular payday, whichever is sooner. When an employee resigns, the employer must pay by the next regular payday. Arizona law also requires that unused, earned vacation pay be paid out if the employer has a policy or practice of paying it.

Maximizing Your Take-Home Pay in Arizona

Arizona already gives you a tax advantage with its low flat rate, but there are additional ways to keep more of your earnings.

  1. Max out your 401(k) or 403(b). The 2024 contribution limit is $23,000 ($30,500 if you are 50+). Every dollar contributed reduces your federal taxable income by up to 22-37 cents (depending on your bracket) and your Arizona taxable income by 2.5 cents.
  2. Use an HSA if you qualify. Health Savings Accounts offer triple tax advantages: contributions are pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. The 2024 limit is $4,150 (individual) or $8,300 (family).
  3. Claim Arizona tax credits. The school tuition organization credit, public school credit, and qualifying charity credit can reduce your Arizona tax bill by thousands of dollars. These are actual credits, not deductions, so they provide a dollar-for-dollar reduction.
  4. Adjust your W-4. If you consistently receive large refunds, you are giving the government an interest-free loan. Reduce your withholding to increase each paycheck. The IRS withholding estimator can help you dial in the right amount.
  5. Contribute to a Flexible Spending Account. Dependent care FSAs allow up to $5,000 in pre-tax contributions. Healthcare FSAs let you set aside pre-tax money for medical expenses.
  6. Consider a Roth IRA. While this does not reduce your current tax bill, Arizona's low state tax rate means you pay less tax on Roth contributions now, and all future growth and withdrawals are tax-free in retirement.

Arizona Retirement Income and Taxes

Arizona is a popular retirement destination for good reason. The tax treatment of retirement income is favorable, and the climate attracts retirees from across the country.

Social Security

Arizona does not tax Social Security benefits at the state level. Regardless of your total income, your Social Security payments are completely exempt from Arizona income tax. At the federal level, up to 85% of Social Security benefits may be taxable depending on your combined income.

Pension and 401(k) Income

Distributions from 401(k)s, traditional IRAs, and most pensions are subject to Arizona's flat 2.5% tax. Arizona provides a $2,500 exemption for income received from certain government pensions (U.S. military retirement, Arizona state/local government pensions). This exemption is relatively small but does provide some additional savings for qualifying retirees.

Why Retirees Choose Arizona

The combination of no Social Security tax, a flat 2.5% rate on other retirement income, no estate or inheritance tax, and a warm climate makes Arizona one of the top retirement states. For a retiree receiving $50,000 in combined pension and 401(k) distributions plus $25,000 in Social Security, the Arizona state tax bill would be approximately $885 — far less than what they would pay in states like Minnesota, Vermont, or Connecticut.

Arizona Self-Employment Taxes

If you are self-employed in Arizona — as a freelancer, contractor, or business owner — your tax situation has some important differences from W-2 employees.

Self-Employment Tax (SE Tax)

Self-employed individuals pay the full 15.3% FICA rate (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare) instead of the employee-only 7.65%. This is because there is no employer to cover the other half. However, you can deduct the employer-equivalent portion (7.65%) of your SE tax when calculating your adjusted gross income.

Quarterly Estimated Payments

Self-employed Arizona residents must make quarterly estimated tax payments to both the IRS (Form 1040-ES) and the Arizona Department of Revenue (Form 140ES). These payments are due on April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year. Underpayment can result in penalties and interest.

Arizona Transaction Privilege Tax

Depending on your business type, you may also owe Arizona's transaction privilege tax (TPT) on your business activities. This is Arizona's version of a sales tax, and it applies to many types of business revenue, not just retail sales. You will need a TPT license from the Arizona Department of Revenue if your business is subject to TPT.

Frequently Asked Questions

Did Arizona really go to a 2.5% flat tax?

Yes. Starting with the 2023 tax year, Arizona's individual income tax rate is a flat 2.5%. This replaced the previous graduated system that had rates up to 4.50%. The change was enacted through SB 1828 and accelerated when state revenue targets were met ahead of schedule.

Do I need to file an Arizona state return if I earned under the standard deduction?

Generally, if your Arizona gross income is below the standard deduction amount ($14,600 single / $29,200 married joint for 2024), you may not owe Arizona tax. However, you should still file a return to claim any credits or to receive a refund of withholding.

What form do I use to file Arizona state taxes?

Most Arizona residents file Form 140 (Resident Personal Income Tax Return). Part-year residents use Form 140PY, and non-residents use Form 140NR. Arizona also offers a simplified Form 140EZ for filers with simple tax situations.

Does my employer automatically withhold Arizona state tax?

Yes, if you work for an Arizona employer. Arizona uses a percentage-based withholding system where you choose a withholding percentage on Form A-4 (Arizona Employee's Withholding Election). You can choose from several percentages ranging from 0.8% to 5.1% of your gross taxable wages. The default is 2.0% if you do not submit a form.

How does Arizona compare to no-income-tax states like Texas or Florida?

Arizona's 2.5% rate means you will pay somewhat more in state income tax than in zero-income-tax states. On a $70,000 salary, that is roughly $1,385 per year. However, Texas has higher property taxes, and both Texas and Florida may have higher sales taxes or other fees. The total tax burden, including property and sales taxes, is often comparable.
