Gear Geometry Fundamentals

Every gear is defined by a set of standard dimensions that determine how teeth mesh and transmit power. Understanding these dimensions is necessary for designing gears that work correctly together.

  • Pitch Circle: The imaginary circle where two mating gears effectively contact. This is the reference circle for all other dimensions.
  • Addendum: The height of the tooth above the pitch circle. For standard gears, addendum = 1 module (or 1/DP).
  • Dedendum: The depth of the tooth below the pitch circle. Standard dedendum = 1.25 × module (includes clearance).
  • Whole Depth: Addendum + Dedendum = 2.25 × module (the total height of the tooth).
  • Working Depth: The depth of tooth engagement = 2 × addendum = 2 × module.
  • Clearance: The gap between the top of one tooth and the bottom of the mating tooth space = 0.25 × module.
  • Circular Pitch: The distance from one tooth to the next, measured along the pitch circle = π × module.
  • Tooth Thickness: The width of the tooth at the pitch circle = circular pitch / 2 = π × module / 2.
  • Base Circle: The circle from which the involute tooth profile is generated = PD × cos(pressure angle).

These formulas assume standard full-depth teeth with no profile shift. Profile-shifted gears have modified addendum and dedendum values to avoid undercutting or to adjust center distance.

Module vs Diametral Pitch

Module and diametral pitch are two ways of expressing the same thing: tooth size. They are inversely related:

Module (mm) = 25.4 / Diametral Pitch (teeth/inch)
Diametral Pitch = 25.4 / Module

Module is the metric standard (ISO) and is used worldwide. Diametral pitch is the Imperial standard (AGMA/ANSI) and is common in the United States and United Kingdom.

Two gears must have the same module (or DP) and the same pressure angle to mesh correctly. Gears with different modules cannot work together.

Pressure Angle and Its Effects

The pressure angle is the angle between the line of action (the direction of force between meshing teeth) and the tangent to the pitch circle. It fundamentally affects tooth shape, strength, and operation:

Property14.5°20°25°
Tooth ShapeTall, slenderMediumShort, stubby
Tooth StrengthLowerGoodHighest
Contact RatioHigher (smoother)GoodLower
Bearing LoadLower radial loadModerateHigher radial load
Noise LevelQuieterModerateLouder
Min Teeth (no undercut)321712

The 20° pressure angle has become the de facto standard because it provides a good balance between strength and smooth engagement. The 14.5° angle is found primarily in older machinery and replacement gears. The 25° angle is used where maximum load capacity is needed, such as in heavy-duty speed reducers.

The Involute Tooth Profile

Virtually all modern gears use the involute curve for the tooth profile. The involute is generated by unwinding a taut string from the base circle. The key property that makes it so useful: the contact between two involute teeth always occurs along a straight line (the line of action), producing a constant angular velocity ratio.

This means that even if the center distance between two gears is slightly off due to manufacturing tolerances or thermal expansion, the speed ratio remains exactly correct. Only the pressure angle changes slightly. This tolerance for center distance variation is the primary reason involute gears replaced cycloidal gears in almost all applications.

The involute function (inv φ) is defined as:

inv(φ) = tan(φ) - φ (where φ is in radians)

This function is used in tooth thickness calculations at different radii and in profile shift calculations. The involute starts at the base circle and extends outward. Below the base circle, the tooth flank is typically a straight radial line (or a trochoid cut by the generating tool).

Gear Types Comparison

Different gear configurations suit different speed, load, and space requirements:

Gear TypeAxis OrientationEfficiencySpeed RangeNoiseCost
SpurParallel98-99%Low-MediumModerateLow

Double HelicalParallel96-99%HighVery LowHigh
Bevel (straight)Intersecting (90°)97-99%Low-MediumModerateMedium
Spiral BevelIntersecting (90°)97-99%Medium-HighLowHigh
WormNon-intersecting (90°)40-90%High ratiosLowMedium
Rack and PinionLinear97-99%AnyModerateLow
InternalParallel98-99%Low-MediumLowHigh

Spur gears are the simplest and cheapest to manufacture. They work well at moderate speeds but become noisy at high RPM because teeth engage along their entire width simultaneously (line contact).

Helical gears have teeth cut at an angle (helix angle, typically 15-30°). The gradual tooth engagement reduces noise and vibration. The downside is axial thrust, which requires thrust bearings.

Bevel gears connect shafts at an angle, most commonly 90°. Straight bevel gears are the simplest; spiral bevel gears are quieter and stronger.

Worm gears provide very high reduction ratios (5:1 to 100:1) in a compact package. However, they are less efficient due to sliding contact and often cannot be back-driven (the output cannot drive the input), which can be a safety feature or a limitation.

Backlash and Tolerances

Backlash is the gap between the non-driving face of a tooth and the mating tooth when the driving faces are in contact. Some backlash is necessary for:

  • Thermal expansion: Gears heat up during operation and grow. Without backlash, they would bind.
  • Lubrication: Oil needs space to form a film between teeth.
  • Manufacturing tolerances: No gear is perfect. Backlash accommodates small errors in tooth profile and spacing.

Recommended backlash values (measured at the pitch circle):

Backlash can be controlled by adjusting center distance (moving gears closer reduces backlash), by thinning teeth, or by using anti-backlash mechanisms (spring-loaded split gears, preloaded duplex gears). For servo systems and CNC machines, minimal backlash is critical for positioning accuracy.

Standard Module and DP Sizes

Using standard module or DP values ensures that off-the-shelf cutting tools (hobs, shaper cutters) are available. Custom tooth sizes require custom tooling, which is expensive.

ISO Standard Modules (mm): 0.3, 0.4, 0.5, 0.6, 0.8, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0, 12.0, 16.0, 20.0, 25.0

Standard Diametral Pitches: 2.5, 3, 4, 5, 6, 8, 10, 12, 14, 16, 18, 20, 24, 32, 48, 64, 72, 80, 96, 120

When designing a gear train, select the smallest standard module that provides adequate tooth strength and fits the available space. Larger modules mean larger, heavier gears but with stronger teeth that can handle more torque.

Undercutting and Profile Shift

Undercutting occurs when the generating tool (hob or shaper cutter) removes material from the base of the tooth during manufacturing. This weakens the tooth root and reduces the effective contact length.

Undercutting happens when the number of teeth is below a critical minimum:

N_min = 2 / sin²(φ)

For standard pressure angles: N_min = 32 at 14.5°, 17 at 20°, and 12 at 25°.

If you need fewer teeth than the minimum, profile shift (also called addendum modification or x-shift) is used. A positive profile shift moves the cutting tool away from the gear center, producing thicker teeth at the root and thinner teeth at the tip:

Profile shift coefficient: x = (N_min - N) / N_min (approximate starting point)

Profile shift also affects center distance and backlash. When one gear has a positive shift, the mating gear typically has a negative shift so that the center distance remains standard. Consult gear design software or AGMA standards for precise profile shift calculations.

AGMA Quality Grades

AGMA (American Gear Manufacturers Association) defines quality grades that specify allowable tolerances for tooth-to-tooth and total composite errors. Higher grades mean tighter tolerances and higher manufacturing cost:

AGMA GradeISO GradeTolerance LevelTypical Application
3 - 510 - 8LowNon-critical, hand-operated mechanisms
6 - 87 - 5MediumGeneral industrial, commercial vehicles
9 - 114 - 2HighMachine tools, turbines, precision drives
12 - 141 - 0Ultra-highInstruments, master gears, aerospace

Note that AGMA and ISO numbering runs in opposite directions. AGMA grade 12 is the highest precision (tightest tolerance), while ISO grade 0 is the highest. Most industrial gears are AGMA 8-10 (ISO 5-3).

Manufacturing method affects achievable quality: hobbing produces AGMA 8-10, shaving achieves AGMA 10-11, and grinding can reach AGMA 12-14. Each additional quality grade approximately doubles the manufacturing cost.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Module 2, 30-Tooth Spur Gear

Module = 2 mm, N = 30, Pressure angle = 20°

  • Pitch Diameter: PD = 2 × 30 = 60.000 mm
  • Outside Diameter: OD = (30 + 2) × 2 = 64.000 mm
  • Root Diameter: RD = (30 - 2.5) × 2 = 55.000 mm
  • Base Circle: DB = 60 × cos(20°) = 56.382 mm
  • Addendum: a = 1 × 2 = 2.000 mm
  • Dedendum: d = 1.25 × 2 = 2.500 mm
  • Circular Pitch: p = π × 2 = 6.283 mm
  • Tooth Thickness: t = 6.283 / 2 = 3.142 mm

Example 2: Gear Ratio Design

Need a 4:1 reduction with module 3 gears, input speed 1800 RPM.

  • Select pinion: N₁ = 20 teeth (above the 17-tooth minimum for 20° PA)
  • Gear: N₂ = 20 × 4 = 80 teeth
  • Pinion PD: 3 × 20 = 60 mm
  • Gear PD: 3 × 80 = 240 mm
  • Center Distance: (60 + 240) / 2 = 150 mm
  • Output Speed: 1800 / 4 = 450 RPM
  • Output Torque: 4 × input torque (minus friction losses)

Frequently Asked Questions

What is gear module?

Module is the metric unit for gear tooth size, defined as the pitch diameter divided by the number of teeth: m = PD/N. It is expressed in millimeters. Two gears must have the same module to mesh correctly. Standard modules per ISO are: 0.5, 0.8, 1.0, 1.25, 1.5, 2.0, 2.5, 3.0, 4.0, 5.0, 6.0, 8.0, 10.0, and larger.

How do I convert module to diametral pitch?

Diametral Pitch = 25.4 / Module. For example, module 2 mm = 25.4/2 = 12.7 DP. In practice, you would round to the nearest standard DP (12 or 14). The conversion is exact but standard sizes in metric and Imperial do not line up perfectly.

Can I mesh gears with different pressure angles?

No. Both gears in a meshing pair must have the same pressure angle. A 20° gear cannot mesh with a 14.5° gear. Attempting to do so will result in improper tooth contact, excessive wear, noise, and potential failure.

What determines whether I need spur or helical gears?

Use spur gears when: cost is a priority, speed is moderate (under 1000 RPM for large gears), noise is not critical, and axial space is limited. Use helical gears when: quiet operation is needed, speeds are high, loads are heavy, or smooth power transmission is required.

How many teeth should my pinion have?

At minimum, use 17 teeth for 20° pressure angle to avoid undercutting. For power transmission, 18-25 teeth is a good range for the pinion. More teeth gives smoother operation but limits the maximum gear ratio for a given gear size. Using profile shift allows fewer teeth without undercutting.

What is contact ratio and why does it matter?

Contact ratio is the average number of tooth pairs in contact at any instant. It should be at least 1.2 for smooth operation. Values of 1.4-1.8 are typical for spur gears. Higher contact ratios mean smoother, quieter operation and lower tooth stresses because the load is shared between more teeth.

How do I specify a gear for manufacturing?

At minimum, specify: module (or DP), number of teeth, pressure angle, face width, material, heat treatment, surface finish, and AGMA quality grade. Also include tooth thickness tolerance, runout tolerance, and any profile modifications (tip relief, crowning). A gear drawing should reference the applicable AGMA or ISO standard.

What materials are used for gears?

Common gear materials: 4140 and 4340 alloy steel (general purpose), 8620 carburizing steel (high hardness), ductile iron (cost-effective, vibration damping), bronze (worm wheels), nylon/acetal (low load, quiet). Case-hardened steel gears (58-62 HRC surface) can handle 2-3 times the load of through-hardened gears.
