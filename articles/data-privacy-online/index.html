The Price Tag on Your Digital Life

Your personal data has a dollar value, and it is being bought and sold thousands of times per day without your knowledge or meaningful consent. According to research from the Financial Times and Webroot, the average person's data profile is worth approximately $240 per year to advertisers. That figure accounts for your browsing history, purchase behavior, location data, demographic information, and the hundreds of other data points that make up your digital identity.

The total addressable market is staggering. The data broker industry generates an estimated $250 billion in annual revenue globally, according to reports from the Interactive Advertising Bureau and industry analysts. There are over 4,000 data broker companies operating worldwide, most of which you have never heard of and never directly interacted with. Companies like Acxiom, Experian, Oracle Data Cloud, and LiveRamp maintain profiles on hundreds of millions of consumers, each containing 1,500 to 3,000 individual data points.

I started paying attention to data privacy after a personal experience that many readers will find familiar. I searched for running shoes on a Tuesday morning, and by Tuesday afternoon, every website I visited was showing me ads for running shoes. That evening, my email inbox contained a "personalized recommendation" from a retailer I had not visited in months. The precision was unsettling, and it motivated me to understand exactly how the system works.

What I discovered was far more extensive than targeted advertising. The data collection ecosystem is a multi-layered infrastructure that touches nearly every aspect of our digital and increasingly physical lives. This article explains how it works, who profits from it, and what practical steps you can take to limit your exposure.

Inside the Data Broker Industry

Data brokers are companies that collect, aggregate, analyze, and sell personal information about consumers. They operate in the background of the digital economy, and most consumers have no direct relationship with them. You did not sign up for their services. You did not agree to their terms. They built a profile on you anyway.

The industry operates on three tiers. First-party data brokers collect data directly from their own websites and services. Second-party data brokers acquire data through partnerships and data-sharing agreements. Third-party data brokers aggregate data from multiple sources, including public records, commercial databases, social media scraping, and data purchased from other brokers.

The Major Players

Acxiom (now part of IPG) is one of the largest data brokers in the world. They maintain marketing data on approximately 2.5 billion consumers globally, with particularly deep coverage in the United States where they claim to have data on 98% of US households. Their database, called InfoBase, contains over 11,000 possible attributes per person, including purchase history, income estimates, political affiliations, health interests, and life event triggers like marriages, home purchases, and new children.

Experian is better known as a credit bureau, but their data brokerage operations are equally substantial. Their ConsumerView database contains information on 300 million consumers and 126 million households in the US alone. Beyond credit data, Experian sells marketing data covering demographics, lifestyle interests, purchasing behavior, and digital identity attributes.

Oracle Data Cloud (which acquired BlueKai and Datalogix) operates one of the largest third-party data marketplaces. They connect online browsing behavior with offline purchase data, enabling advertisers to measure whether someone who saw an online ad later made a purchase in a physical store. Their data marketplace offers access to data from over 100 data providers covering 5 billion consumer IDs globally.

LiveRamp specializes in identity resolution, which means connecting the various identifiers associated with a single person (email addresses, phone numbers, device IDs, cookies, IP addresses) into a unified profile. This "identity graph" is what allows advertisers to follow you across devices and platforms. LiveRamp connects data from over 500 sources and maintains identity graphs on hundreds of millions of consumers.

How Data Brokers Make Money

Data brokers monetize personal information through several business models. Selling raw data is the most straightforward: a marketer pays per record or per thousand records for a specific audience segment. Audience segments might include "parents of children under 5 who earn over $100,000" or "people who recently searched for home insurance." Pricing varies widely, but a single consumer record typically sells for $0.005 to $0.50 depending on the specificity and recency of the data.

What They Know About You

The scope of data collected about the average internet user is difficult to comprehend. Here is a partial inventory of what a typical data broker profile contains, organized by category.

Identity and Contact

Full name, date of birth, home address (current and previous), email addresses (all of them), phone numbers, Social Security number (in some cases), driver's license number, household composition (spouse, children, ages), education level, occupation, employer name.

Financial Profile

Estimated household income, net worth range, credit score tier, home ownership status, home value, mortgage amount, vehicle ownership and make/model, investment accounts, bank accounts, credit card types, loan amounts, payment history patterns, bankruptcy records.

Online Behavior

Complete browsing history (from tracking cookies and ISP data), search queries, social media activity, app usage patterns, content consumption habits (articles read, videos watched, podcasts listened to), email open and click behavior, online purchase history, shopping cart abandonments, product reviews written, forum participation.

Location and Movement

Real-time location (from phone GPS), historical location patterns, places visited (stores, restaurants, offices, medical facilities, places of worship), commute patterns, travel destinations, time spent at each location.

Health and Lifestyle

Health conditions searched for, medications searched for or purchased, fitness tracker data, diet preferences, sleep patterns, medical provider visits (inferred from location data), health insurance type, mental health app usage.

Social and Political

Political party registration, campaign donations, petition signatures, social media connections, group memberships, religious affiliation (inferred from location data and browsing), dating app usage, relationship status changes.

The composite picture: No single company has all of this data on every person. But data brokers specialize in combining fragments from hundreds of sources into detailed profiles. The company that has your browsing history sells it to the company that has your financial data, and a third company combines both to build a profile that is more detailed than anything you would voluntarily share with a friend.

How Your Data Gets Collected

Data collection happens through both obvious and non-obvious channels. Understanding these channels is the first step toward controlling what information you share.

Direct Collection (Things You Do Knowingly)

Every time you create an account, fill out a form, make a purchase, sign up for a loyalty program, or post on social media, you are directly providing data. Terms of service agreements, which almost nobody reads in full, typically include permission to share your data with "business partners" and "affiliated companies." A 2024 study found that the average privacy policy requires 18 minutes to read, and users encounter approximately 1,500 privacy policies per year. Reading all of them would require 76 full working days annually.

Indirect Collection (Things That Happen in the Background)

Tracking cookies follow your browsing across websites. Web beacons (invisible 1x1 pixel images) confirm when you open emails and visit pages. Browser fingerprinting identifies your device based on its unique combination of settings, fonts, screen resolution, and hardware. SDKs embedded in mobile apps collect location data, device identifiers, and usage patterns. Wi-Fi probe requests from your phone reveal your device's MAC address to nearby routers, which retail analytics companies use to track foot traffic in stores.

Inferred Data (Things They Calculate)

Beyond what you share and what they observe, data brokers use algorithms to infer additional attributes. If you browse pregnancy-related content and buy prenatal vitamins, they infer you are expecting a child. If your phone visits an oncology center regularly, they infer a health condition. These inferences are not always accurate, but they are added to your profile and sold as if they were factual data points.

Tracking Techniques Explained

The technical methods used to track your online activity have become increasingly sophisticated as consumers and browsers have implemented blocking measures.

Third-Party Cookies

Third-party cookies have been the primary tracking mechanism on the web for over two decades. When you visit a website, the page loads content from dozens of third-party domains: advertising networks, analytics services, social media widgets, and content delivery networks. Each of these third parties can set a cookie in your browser that persists across websites. When you visit a different site that loads content from the same third party, the cookie identifies you, linking your activity across both sites.

A typical web page loads content from 30 to 80 third-party domains. Each of those domains can track your visit. The advertising technology company Criteo, for example, has its tracking pixel on millions of websites. Every time you visit one of those sites, Criteo's cookie identifies you and adds the visit to your browsing profile. This profile is then used to target you with personalized ads across other sites in the Criteo network.

Major browsers have moved to restrict or eliminate third-party cookies. Safari and Firefox blocked them by default several years ago. Google Chrome has implemented its Privacy Sandbox initiative as an alternative, though the timeline has shifted multiple times. Managing your cookies is an essential privacy practice. Our tools can help you understand how cookies work and what data they contain.

Browser Fingerprinting

Browser fingerprinting identifies your device by collecting dozens of attributes that, combined, create a nearly unique identifier. These attributes include your browser type and version, operating system, screen resolution, installed fonts, timezone, language settings, hardware capabilities (GPU, number of CPU cores), and dozens more. The Electronic Frontier Foundation's Panopticlick project found that 83.6% of browsers have a unique fingerprint, and 94.2% of browsers with JavaScript enabled are uniquely identifiable.

Unlike cookies, fingerprinting cannot be cleared or blocked through browser settings. It works by querying browser APIs and measuring rendering behavior. Canvas fingerprinting, for example, renders a hidden image using your browser's graphics engine. Tiny differences in how different hardware and software render the same image create a unique hash. WebGL fingerprinting extends this by querying your GPU's capabilities.

// Simplified example of canvas fingerprinting
const canvas = document.createElement('canvas');
const ctx = canvas.getContext('2d');
ctx.textBaseline = 'top';
ctx.font = '14px Arial';
ctx.fillText('Browser fingerprint test', 2, 2);
const hash = canvas.toDataURL().hashCode();
// This hash is nearly unique to your device

Pixel Tracking

Tracking pixels (also called web beacons) are tiny transparent images embedded in web pages and emails. When the pixel loads, the request to the tracking server transmits information about who viewed the page or opened the email, including the IP address, browser type, time of viewing, and any cookies associated with the tracking domain. Email tracking pixels tell senders exactly when you opened their email, how many times, and on what device. Approximately 70% of marketing emails contain tracking pixels.

Cross-Device Tracking

Cross-device tracking links your activity across your phone, laptop, tablet, smart TV, and other connected devices to a single profile. There are two primary methods. Deterministic matching uses login data: if you sign into Gmail on your phone and your laptop, Google knows both devices belong to you. Probabilistic matching uses algorithms that analyze IP addresses, location patterns, and browsing behavior to infer that devices in the same location at the same times probably belong to the same person.

Companies like Tapad and Drawbridge (now part of LinkedIn) specialize in cross-device identity graphs. Tapad claims to map over 2 billion devices to individual consumers. This means the shoe ad you see on your phone after searching on your laptop is not a coincidence. The tracking infrastructure specifically linked your devices and retargeted you.

Location Tracking

Your smartphone continuously broadcasts its location through GPS, cell tower triangulation, Wi-Fi network scanning, and Bluetooth beacons. Apps that request location permission (weather apps, maps, social media, ride-sharing, food delivery) can access this data and share it with third parties. A 2025 investigation by journalists found that a single popular weather app was sending location data to 40 different companies.

Location data is particularly sensitive because it reveals where you live, where you work, where your children go to school, which medical facilities you visit, which religious institutions you attend, and who you spend time with. Location data brokers like X-Mode (now Outlogic), Placer.ai, and SafeGraph aggregate this information from hundreds of apps and sell it to advertisers, hedge funds, law enforcement agencies, and political campaigns.

Who Buys Your Data and Why

The buyers of personal data span virtually every industry. Understanding who buys data and what they do with it reveals the full economic ecosystem built on your information.

Advertisers and Marketers

Digital advertising is the largest consumer of personal data. The global digital advertising market reached $680 billion in 2025. Advertisers use personal data to target ads at specific audiences, personalize ad creative, determine how much to bid for the right to show you an ad, and measure whether their ads drove purchases. The entire programmatic advertising ecosystem, which handles over 80% of digital ad spending, is built on personal data exchange.

Insurance Companies

Insurance companies purchase data to assess risk and set premiums. Your social media activity, purchasing behavior, and even your browsing history can influence insurance pricing. A study published in the Journal of Consumer Research found that consumers who browse certain categories of websites are statistically correlated with higher insurance claims, and insurers have explored using this data in underwriting. Life insurance companies have purchased gym membership data, health food purchase data, and social media activity data to supplement traditional actuarial factors.

Employers and Recruiters

Background check companies like Checkr and HireRight aggregate personal data for employment screening. Beyond the standard criminal history and credit checks, some screening services include social media analysis, online reputation scoring, and behavioral assessments derived from digital footprints. A 2024 survey by CareerBuilder found that 70% of employers use social media to screen candidates.

Financial Services

Banks and lenders use alternative data to make credit decisions. Traditional credit scores rely on payment history and debt levels, but newer models incorporate rent payments, utility payments, bank transaction patterns, and even social media connections. This can expand access to credit for people with thin credit files, but it also means more of your personal behavior is factored into financial decisions.

Political Campaigns

Political campaigns are aggressive buyers of personal data. Voter files (public records of who is registered and whether they voted, though not how they voted) are combined with consumer data profiles to create detailed models of individual voter preferences. The 2024 US presidential campaigns collectively spent over $1 billion on digital advertising, much of it targeted using personal data. Data brokers sell audience segments like "persuadable voters in swing districts" and "likely donors to conservative causes."

Law Enforcement

Government agencies purchase personal data from commercial data brokers to circumvent the legal protections that would apply if they collected the data directly. The Fourth Amendment requires a warrant for government surveillance, but data purchased from a commercial broker falls into a legal gray area. Investigations by journalists and civil liberties organizations have documented purchases of location data, web browsing data, and social media monitoring tools by federal agencies including the IRS, FBI, DHS, and military intelligence.

The Real Cost of Free Services

The common refrain "if you are not paying for the product, you are the product" understates the situation. Even when you are paying, you are often still the product. Paid streaming services, paid apps, and paid subscription services routinely collect and monetize user data alongside their subscription revenue.

$250B Global data broker industry annual revenue

Consider the economics of a "free" email service. Google's Gmail serves approximately 1.8 billion users. Google's parent company Alphabet generated $307 billion in revenue in 2025, with approximately 80% coming from advertising. Gmail's role in this ecosystem is not simply email delivery. By scanning email content (for ad targeting purposes), analyzing login patterns, and linking email identity to browsing activity across Google's vast network, Gmail provides the identity backbone that makes Google's advertising targeting possible.

Social media platforms operate on the same model at an even larger scale. Meta (Facebook, Instagram, WhatsApp) generated $164 billion in revenue in 2025, with 97% coming from advertising. Every post, like, share, comment, photo, and message contributes to the data profile that Meta uses to target ads. The average Facebook user generates approximately $40 per year in advertising revenue for Meta in North America, and the platform's targeting is precise enough that advertisers pay premium rates for it.

The $240 per year figure for your data may even be conservative when you consider all the entities profiting from it simultaneously. Google, Facebook, Amazon, data brokers, app developers, ISPs, and credit bureaus are all monetizing overlapping sets of your personal information at the same time.

Data Breaches: When Collection Becomes Exposure

The more data that is collected and stored, the greater the consequences when that data is exposed. Data breaches have become a constant feature of digital life, with thousands of breaches reported annually.

The numbers are sobering. In 2025 alone, reported data breaches exposed approximately 6.1 billion records. The average cost of a data breach to the affected company reached $4.88 million, according to IBM's Cost of a Data Breach Report. But the cost to individuals is harder to quantify: identity theft, financial fraud, phishing attacks, and the permanent exposure of personal information that cannot be changed (like your Social Security number or biometric data).

Every company that collects your data is a potential breach target. Data brokers, who aggregate massive amounts of personal information by design, represent particularly high-value targets. The National Public Data breach in 2024 exposed 2.9 billion records from a data broker that most people had never heard of, containing Social Security numbers and personal information that the affected individuals never knowingly provided to that company.

The core problem: You cannot have a data breach without data collection. Every piece of personal information stored in a database is a potential breach victim. The only data that is truly safe from breaches is data that was never collected in the first place. This is why data minimization is the single most effective privacy strategy.

The Legal Landscape in 2026

Privacy regulation has accelerated significantly in recent years, though enforcement remains uneven and the global patchwork of laws creates complexity for both companies and consumers.

European Union: GDPR

The General Data Protection Regulation, in effect since 2018, remains the most comprehensive privacy law globally. GDPR requires explicit consent for data collection, grants individuals the right to access, correct, and delete their data, mandates data breach notification within 72 hours, and imposes fines up to 4% of global annual revenue. Since 2018, GDPR enforcement has resulted in over $4.5 billion in fines against companies including Meta ($1.3 billion), Amazon ($887 million), and Google ($391 million).

United States: State-Level Patchwork

The US still lacks a comprehensive federal privacy law, but state legislation has expanded rapidly. As of 2026, 19 states have enacted comprehensive privacy laws. California's CCPA/CPRA remains the strongest, granting consumers the right to know what data is collected, delete their data, opt out of data sales, and limit the use of sensitive personal information. Virginia, Colorado, Connecticut, Utah, and other states have passed similar but less stringent laws.

A federal privacy bill has been proposed multiple times but has not yet passed. Industry lobbying, disagreements over private right of action (whether individuals can sue companies directly for privacy violations), and debates over federal preemption of state laws have stalled progress. Until a federal law passes, companies must navigate a complex landscape of different state requirements.

Practical Implications

For individuals, these laws provide actionable rights. You can submit data access requests to companies to see what they have collected. You can request deletion. You can opt out of data sales. The challenge is that exercising these rights requires knowing which companies have your data and submitting individual requests to each one. Data broker registries maintained by California and Vermont provide lists of known data brokers, giving you a starting point for deletion requests.

How to Protect Your Data

Complete digital privacy is unrealistic for most people unless you are willing to disconnect entirely. But meaningful reduction in data collection is achievable through a layered approach. Here are the steps I recommend, ordered from easiest to most thorough.

Level 1: Basic Hygiene (15 Minutes)

  • Review and restrict app permissions on your phone, especially location access. Switch apps from "always allow" location to "only while using" or "never" for apps that do not need real-time location (which is most of them).
  • Clear your browser cookies and cache regularly. Better yet, configure your browser to clear cookies when you close it.
  • Use a strong, unique password for every account. Password reuse means that a breach of one account compromises all accounts using the same password.
  • Enable two-factor authentication on every account that supports it, starting with your email accounts. Your email is the skeleton key to your digital life because password reset links go there.
  • Opt out of data sharing in the settings of every app and service you use. Most platforms bury this in privacy or advertising settings, but they do offer opt-outs.

Level 2: Reduce Your Attack Surface (1 Hour)

  • Install a reputable ad and tracker blocker extension. uBlock Origin is the most effective open-source option. It blocks tracking scripts, cookies, and fingerprinting attempts before they execute.
  • Switch your default search engine to DuckDuckGo or Brave Search. Google Search profiles your queries and uses them for ad targeting. Privacy-focused search engines do not.
  • Delete accounts you no longer use. Every dormant account is a data collection point and a potential breach target. Use the "delete account" option, not just "deactivate."
  • Review your social media privacy settings and limit who can see your posts, profile information, and friend list. On Facebook, restrict old posts to "friends only" and disable the option for search engines to link to your profile.
  • Use email aliases for online shopping and service sign-ups. Apple's Hide My Email and similar services create unique forwarding addresses that you can delete if they start receiving spam.

Level 3: Active Defense (Ongoing)

  • Submit data deletion requests to major data brokers. Services like DeleteMe and Privacy Duck automate this process for a fee, or you can submit requests manually using the broker registries maintained by the California Attorney General's office.
  • Use a VPN for browsing to prevent your ISP from logging and selling your browsing history. ISPs in the US are legally allowed to sell browsing data since the repeal of FCC privacy rules in 2017.
  • Consider using separate browsers for separate activities: one for logged-in services (email, banking) and one for general browsing. This prevents tracking companies from linking your logged-in identity to your anonymous browsing.
  • Set up Google Alerts for your name, email, and phone number to monitor when your information appears in new contexts online.
  • Freeze your credit at all three bureaus (Equifax, Experian, TransUnion) to prevent identity thieves from opening new accounts in your name. Credit freezes are free and take 10 minutes to set up.

Browser-Level Privacy Settings

Your browser is the primary interface between you and the tracking infrastructure. Configuring it correctly provides significant protection without changing your browsing habits.

Firefox Privacy Configuration

Firefox offers the strongest built-in privacy protections of any mainstream browser. Its Enhanced Tracking Protection feature, enabled by default, blocks third-party tracking cookies, cryptominers, fingerprinters, and social media trackers. For maximum protection, switch from "Standard" to "Strict" in Firefox privacy settings. This blocks additional trackers and cross-site cookies but may occasionally break websites that rely on third-party cookies for legitimate functionality.

Firefox also supports DNS over HTTPS (DoH), which encrypts your DNS queries to prevent your ISP from seeing which websites you visit. Enable DoH in Firefox network settings and choose a privacy-respecting DNS provider like Cloudflare (1.1.1.1) or Mullvad DNS.

Safari Privacy Features

Safari's Intelligent Tracking Prevention (ITP) was the first major browser feature to block cross-site tracking by default. ITP limits the lifespan of third-party cookies, blocks redirect-based tracking, and caps the persistence of first-party cookies set through CNAME cloaking. Safari also includes built-in fingerprinting protection that presents a simplified system configuration to fingerprinting scripts, reducing your browser's uniqueness.

Chrome Privacy Considerations

Chrome is the most popular browser but offers the weakest default privacy protections, which is unsurprising given that Google's business model depends on data collection. Chrome's Privacy Sandbox initiative introduces alternative tracking mechanisms (Topics API, Attribution Reporting) that are designed to be less invasive than third-party cookies while still enabling targeted advertising. Whether these alternatives represent meaningful privacy improvement is debated among privacy researchers.

If you use Chrome, at minimum enable "Send a Do Not Track request" (though compliance is voluntary), block third-party cookies in settings, and install uBlock Origin. Consider switching to Brave, which is Chromium-based (compatible with Chrome extensions) but includes aggressive built-in tracking protection, ad blocking, and fingerprinting resistance.

Password Security as a Privacy Foundation

password security is foundational to data privacy. If an attacker gains access to your accounts through weak or reused passwords, every privacy measure you have implemented becomes irrelevant. They have direct access to your data.

The statistics on password security are alarming. An analysis of breached credential databases found that the average person reuses each password across 3.9 different services. The most common passwords in 2025 breach data were "123456", "password", "123456789", and "qwerty," all of which can be cracked in under one second by modern hardware. Even a seemingly complex password like "Summer2025!" can be cracked in minutes because it follows a predictable pattern (capitalized word, year, special character).

A truly strong password should be at least 16 characters, include a mix of uppercase, lowercase, numbers, and special characters, and not follow any recognizable pattern. Use our Password Generator to create cryptographically random passwords that meet these criteria. Pair strong unique passwords with a password manager (1Password, Bitwarden, or KeePassXC) that stores them securely so you do not need to memorize them.

password cracking math: A random 8-character password using letters, numbers, and symbols has approximately 6.6 quadrillion possible combinations. A modern GPU can test 100 billion passwords per second, cracking it in under 19 hours. A random 16-character password from the same character set has approximately 4.3 x 10^31 combinations, which would take millions of years to crack. Length is the most important factor in password strength. Generate long, random passwords with our Password Generator.

Enable two-factor authentication on every account that supports it. SMS-based 2FA is better than no 2FA, but authenticator app-based 2FA (Google Authenticator, Authy) and hardware security keys (YubiKey) are significantly more secure because they are not vulnerable to SIM swapping attacks.

Consider checking whether your email addresses or passwords have appeared in known data breaches using services like Have I Been Pwned. If any of your credentials have been compromised, change those passwords immediately and ensure you are not reusing the compromised password on any other service.
