The True Cost of a 30-Year Mortgage: Running the Numbers

A $400,000 house at 7% interest does not cost $400,000. It costs $958,036. The extra $558,036 is interest. That number is the most important financial figure most homebuyers never see until they run an amortization schedule. I ran it, along with every related calculation, and laid it all out here.

By Michael Lip / March 25, 2026 / 24 min read

The Mortgage Payment Formula

The monthly payment on a fixed-rate mortgage is calculated with this formula.

M = P * [r(1+r)n] / [(1+r)n - 1]

Where M is the monthly payment, P is the principal (loan amount), r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate / 12), and n is the total number of payments (years * 12).

For a $400,000 loan at 7% over 30 years: P = 400,000. r = 0.07/12 = 0.005833. n = 360.

M = 400,000 * [0.005833(1.005833)^360] / [(1.005833)^360 - 1]

M = 400,000 * [0.005833 * 8.1165] / [8.1165 - 1]

M = 400,000 * 0.047346 / 7.1165

M = 400,000 * 0.006653

M = $2,661

That $2,661 monthly payment, repeated 360 times, totals $958,036. The $400,000 house cost you $958,036 in cash. The $558,036 difference is pure interest.

Amortization: Why Early Payments Are 87% Interest

Amortization is the schedule that determines how each monthly payment is split between interest and principal. The split changes every month, and it is heavily skewed toward interest at the beginning.

The math is simple. Each month, the interest charge equals the remaining principal multiplied by the monthly rate. Everything left in the payment after interest goes to principal.

Month 1 of a $400,000 loan at 7%:

Interest charge: $400,000 * (0.07/12) = $2,333.33. Principal payment: $2,661.21 - $2,333.33 = $327.88.

87.7% of your first payment is interest. Only 12.3% goes toward paying down the loan.

The Power of Extra Payments

Every extra dollar you pay toward principal reduces the balance that accrues interest for the remaining life of the loan. Early extra payments are worth more than later ones because they prevent interest from compounding for more years.

Timing matters. An extra $200/month starting in year 1 saves $159,866. The same $200/month starting in year 10 saves approximately $78,000. Starting in year 20 saves approximately $18,000. The earlier you begin extra payments, the more interest they prevent from accumulating.

Lump Sum vs. Monthly Extra

If you receive a $10,000 bonus, should you make a single lump-sum payment or spread it across 12 months as extra payments?

The lump sum, applied immediately, reduces the principal instantly and prevents interest from accruing on that $10,000 from day one. Spreading it over 12 months means only $833 of principal reduction per month, with the remaining $9,167 (decreasing by $833 each month) still accruing interest.

Applied at month 12 of a $400,000/7% loan: a $10,000 lump sum saves approximately $31,670 in total interest over the life of the loan. The same $10,000 spread as $833/month over 12 months saves approximately $30,400. The lump sum saves $1,270 more. Not a huge difference, but the math consistently favors applying extra payments as early as possible.

The REAL Monthly Cost of Homeownership

The mortgage payment (principal and interest) is the number your lender quotes. It is not the full cost of owning the home. Several additional expenses are either required by the lender or unavoidable as a homeowner.

Property Taxes

Average U.S. effective property tax rate is 1.1% of assessed value, but this varies wildly. New Jersey averages 2.23%. Hawaii averages 0.29%. On a $500,000 home at 1.25%, property tax is $6,250/year or $521/month. This is often escrowed (collected by the lender as part of your monthly payment).

Homeowners Insurance

Required by every mortgage lender. The national average is approximately $2,000/year for $500,000 in dwelling coverage, or about $167/month. Rates vary dramatically by state and risk factors (coastal, wildfire, flood zone). Florida homeowners may pay $4,000-$8,000/year.

PMI (Private Mortgage Insurance)

Required when your down payment is less than 20% of the purchase price. PMI typically costs 0.5% to 1.5% of the loan amount per year. On a $400,000 loan: 0.5% = $2,000/year ($167/month). 1.0% = $4,000/year ($333/month). 1.5% = $6,000/year ($500/month).

PMI can be removed when you reach 20% equity (80% loan-to-value ratio). On a $500,000 home with 10% down ($50,000), your loan is $450,000. PMI can be removed when the loan balance drops to $400,000 (80% of $500,000). At 7% with regular payments only, this takes approximately 7 years.

Maintenance and Repairs

The standard rule of thumb is 1% of the home's value per year for maintenance. On a $500,000 home: $5,000/year or $417/month. Some years you spend $500 (nothing breaks). Other years you spend $15,000 (new roof, HVAC replacement, plumbing emergency). The 1% average smooths these out over time.

HOA Fees

If applicable. The national median HOA fee is approximately $200/month, but ranges from $50 in suburban subdivisions to $1,000+ in luxury condominiums. Some HOAs cover insurance, exterior maintenance, and amenities. Factor this into your total cost comparison, not as an afterthought.

When Refinancing Makes Mathematical Sense

Refinancing replaces your existing mortgage with a new one, typically at a different interest rate. It costs money (closing costs, typically 2-5% of the loan amount or $8,000-$20,000 on a $400,000 loan), so you need to stay in the home long enough for the savings to exceed the costs.

The Break-Even Calculation

Break-Even Months = Total Closing Costs / Monthly Savings

If your closing costs are $9,000 and the refinance reduces your payment by $300/month: break-even = $9,000 / $300 = 30 months (2.5 years). If you plan to stay in the home for 10 more years, you save $300/month for 7.5 years after break-even = $27,000 net savings.

ARM vs. Fixed Rate: The Gamble Quantified

An adjustable-rate mortgage (ARM) offers a lower initial rate for a fixed period (5, 7, or 10 years), after which the rate adjusts periodically (usually annually) based on a market index plus a margin.

A 5/1 ARM means 5 years at the initial rate, then annual adjustments. Common parameters: initial rate adjustment cap (2%), periodic adjustment cap (2%), lifetime cap (5% above initial rate).

In a rising rate environment, the ARM advantage disappears within 6 years. If rates drop (or stay stable), the ARM continues to save money. If you plan to sell or refinance within 5 years, the ARM saves $11,940 with certainty. If you stay 10+ years in a rising rate environment, the ARM costs more.

The ARM is a bet on future interest rates. If you have strong reason to believe you will move within the initial period (job relocation, planned upsize), the ARM is mathematically favorable. If you plan to stay long-term, the fixed rate eliminates uncertainty.

The Opportunity Cost Question

Should you pay off the mortgage early or invest the extra money? This is the most debated question in personal finance, and the math gives a clear answer with an important caveat.

Your mortgage rate is 7%. If you can earn more than 7% after tax on investments, investing wins mathematically. The S&P 500 has returned about 10% annually over long periods. After capital gains tax (15-20%), the after-tax return is roughly 8-8.5%. That exceeds 7%, so investing should win.

But the mortgage payoff is guaranteed. The investment return is an average that includes years of -20% and years of +30%. If you need the money during a down year, you realize losses. The mortgage payoff gives you a guaranteed, risk-free 7% return on every extra dollar.

My perspective: pay the mortgage if your rate is above 6%. Invest the extra if your rate is below 4%. Between 4% and 6% is a personal risk tolerance decision. And always maximize tax-advantaged retirement accounts (401k match, IRA) before making extra mortgage payments, because the tax benefits of retirement accounts almost always exceed the interest savings of mortgage prepayment.

Closing Costs: The Transaction Tax Nobody Talks About

Buying a home costs 2-5% of the purchase price in closing costs, paid at the time of purchase. On a $500,000 home, that is $10,000 to $25,000 that is neither down payment nor loan.

Typical buyer closing costs include: loan origination fee (0.5-1% of loan), appraisal ($400-$600), title insurance ($1,000-$3,000), attorney fees ($500-$2,000), recording fees ($100-$500), home inspection ($300-$600), prepaid property taxes (2-6 months), prepaid insurance (12 months), and escrow deposits.

Selling costs are even higher: 5-6% agent commissions ($25,000-$30,000 on a $500,000 home), transfer taxes ($1,000-$10,000 depending on state), and seller concessions. Round-trip transaction costs (buying and selling the same home) total 8-10% of the home's value.

This is why buying is not always better than renting, despite the popular narrative. If you buy a $500,000 home, pay $40,000 in round-trip transaction costs, and sell after 3 years for $500,000, you lost $40,000 plus $157,680 in mortgage interest during those 3 years, and your equity only grew by $12,000. Renting for 3 years at $2,500/month costs $90,000. Buying and selling costs $185,680 (net of equity) in this scenario. The break-even point where buying beats renting is typically 5-7 years, depending on appreciation, interest rates, and local rent prices.

How Home Appreciation Affects the Math

A home is a magnified investment. Your 10-20% down payment controls 100% of the home's value. If the home appreciates, your return on the down payment is amplified.

$500,000 home, $100,000 down (20%). After 5 years at 3% annual appreciation: home value = $579,637. Equity from appreciation = $79,637. Return on $100,000 down payment from appreciation alone = 79.6% over 5 years, or about 12.4% annual return. You also built roughly $40,000 in equity from principal payments.

But the flip side is equally powerful. If the home declines 10% in the first 2 years (as happened in many markets during 2008-2011): home value = $450,000. You still owe approximately $385,000 (after 2 years of payments on a $400,000 loan). Your equity went from $100,000 to $65,000. You lost 35% of your down payment.

With only 5% down ($25,000), a 10% decline puts you $25,000 underwater (negative equity). You owe more than the home is worth. This is the amplified nature of real estate: bigger gains but bigger losses too.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much total interest do you pay on a 30-year mortgage?

On a $400,000 loan at 7%, total interest is $558,036 over 30 years. Your monthly payment of $2,661, multiplied by 360 payments, equals $958,036 total. You pay 1.4 times the original loan amount in interest. At 6%, total interest drops to $463,353. At 8%, it rises to $656,684.

Why are early mortgage payments mostly interest?

Because interest is calculated on the remaining balance. With $400,000 outstanding at 7%, monthly interest is $2,333. Only $328 of your $2,661 payment goes to principal (12.3%). As the balance decreases, the interest charge decreases and more of each payment goes to principal. It takes about 15 years before principal exceeds interest in each payment.

How much does an extra $100/month save?

On a $400,000 loan at 7%, paying $100 extra per month saves $94,554 in interest and pays off the loan 4 years 4 months early. An extra $200/month saves $159,866 and shaves 7 years 5 months off the term. Each dollar of extra payment saves roughly $2-3 in interest.

What is the difference between 15-year and 30-year mortgages?

On a $400,000 loan, a 30-year at 7% costs $2,661/month with $558,036 total interest. A 15-year at 6.5% costs $3,484/month with $227,160 total interest. The 15-year is $823 more per month but saves $330,876 overall. You also build equity approximately 4 times faster.

What is PMI and when can it be removed?

PMI is required when your down payment is less than 20%. It costs 0.5-1.5% of the loan amount annually ($167-$500/month on a $400,000 loan). Request removal when you reach 80% LTV. The lender must cancel it automatically at 78% LTV. Making extra payments accelerates PMI removal.

When does refinancing make sense?

When interest savings exceed closing costs within your remaining time in the home. Divide closing costs by monthly payment reduction to find the break-even point. If closing costs are $9,000 and you save $300/month, break-even is 30 months. You need at least a 0.75-1% rate reduction for refinancing to be worthwhile.

What is the real monthly cost of homeownership?

On a $500,000 home with $450,000 loan at 7%: mortgage $2,994, property tax $521, insurance $167, PMI $281, maintenance $417. Total: $4,380/month. The mortgage-only payment represents about 68% of the true monthly cost.

How does a 5/1 ARM compare to a fixed-rate mortgage?

A 5/1 ARM at 6.25% starts at $2,462/month versus $2,661 for a 7% fixed, saving $11,940 in the first 5 years. After year 5, the rate adjusts annually. In a rising rate scenario, the ARM becomes more expensive than fixed by year 6. ARMs favor borrowers who will sell or refinance within the initial fixed period.
