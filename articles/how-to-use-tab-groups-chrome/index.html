Tab Groups Launched in Chrome 83 and Most People Still Ignore Them

Google shipped tab groups in Chrome 83, back in May 2020. That was six years ago. And based on every screen share I have watched, every coworker laptop I have glanced at, and every browser screenshot posted on social media, the vast majority of Chrome users still have a flat row of 40 identical favicons crammed into their tab bar with zero organization whatsoever.

I was one of those people for longer than I care to admit. I would open tabs for a project, open more tabs for a different project, open research tabs for something I was curious about, and eventually reach the point where Chrome showed nothing but tiny favicon squares with no readable titles. Then I would either close everything in a fit of frustration or just open a new window and start the cycle again.

Tab groups solved this problem so effectively that I now consider them one of the most underused features in any browser. The concept is straightforward. You take related tabs, assign them to a named and color-coded group, and suddenly your tab bar transforms from a chaotic strip of indistinguishable icons into clearly labeled sections that you can expand, collapse, and rearrange.

The feature has matured considerably since its initial release. Chrome has added the ability to save groups, improved the visual design, and refined the drag-and-drop behavior. But the core mechanics remain simple enough that you can learn the entire system in about ten minutes and build a habit around it within a week.

This guide walks through everything from creating your first group to building a sustainable workflow around tab groups. I will also cover Tab Group Manager by Zovo, an extension that fills the gaps Chrome leaves around persistent group saving and one-click restoration.

Creating Your First Tab Group

The fastest way to create a tab group is the right-click method. Right-click any tab in your tab bar and select "Add tab to new group" from the context menu. Chrome immediately wraps that tab in a colored underline, opens a small popup where you can name the group and pick a color, and the group is live.

Creating a tab group from a single tab

  1. Right-click any open tab in the tab bar
  2. Select "Add tab to new group" from the context menu
  3. A color dot appears to the left of the tab
  4. Click the dot to open the group settings popup
  5. Type a name for the group
  6. Select a color from the eight available options
  7. Click anywhere outside the popup to confirm

You can also create a group from multiple tabs at once. Hold Ctrl (or Cmd on Mac) and click each tab you want to include. Then right-click any of the selected tabs and choose "Add tabs to new group." All selected tabs move into the new group together. This is much faster than creating a group and then dragging tabs into it one at a time.

For selecting a contiguous range of tabs, click the first tab, hold Shift, and click the last tab in the range. Every tab between those two clicks is selected. Then right-click and add them all to a new group.

Once a group exists, adding more tabs to it is straightforward. Right-click any ungrouped tab and select "Add tab to group," then pick the existing group from the submenu. You can also drag any tab directly into an existing group in the tab bar. Chrome shows a visual indicator when you are hovering over a group so you know the tab will land in the right place.

New tabs opened from links within a grouped tab automatically join that tab's group. If I am in my "Research" group and I Ctrl-click a link to open it in a new tab, the new tab appears inside the Research group rather than floating outside it. This behavior is useful because it keeps related browsing contained without manual intervention.

Color Coding and Naming Conventions That Work

Chrome offers eight group colors: grey, blue, red, yellow, green, pink, purple, and cyan. That might seem limited, but eight colors is actually more than enough when combined with descriptive group names. The key is developing a consistent personal convention and sticking with it.

I spent months trying different approaches before settling on a system that actually works for me. The two most common mistakes I see people make are using colors randomly (which provides no useful information at a glance) and using names that are too vague (like "Stuff" or "Misc").

The specific convention matters less than having one at all. Some people prefer to color-code by client, by urgency level, or by project phase. What matters is that when you glance at your tab bar, the colors communicate something without requiring you to read every group name.

For group names, I keep them short. Two or three words maximum. The name shows in the tab bar and long names eat up horizontal space, which defeats the purpose of organizing tabs in the first place. "Extension Engine" is good. "Working on the Extension Engine Chrome Extension Project" is not.

I have also found it helpful to use a prefix convention for closely related groups. If I am working on a client project, I might have groups named "ClientX Dev," "ClientX Docs," and "ClientX Comms." The shared prefix makes it immediately clear these groups belong to the same project, even though they serve different purposes.

Tip. If you find yourself creating more than six or seven groups at once, that is usually a signal that you have too many contexts open simultaneously. Close or save the groups you are not actively using. Context switching between seven projects is not productivity. It is distraction with extra steps.

Collapsing Groups to Save Space

Collapsing tab groups is the single feature that makes the entire system work at scale. Without collapsing, tab groups are just colored labels. With collapsing, they become manageable compartments that shrink down to a tiny label when you do not need them.

To collapse a group, click its name label in the tab bar. The group folds down to just the colored label, hiding all tabs within it. Click the label again to expand the group and see its tabs. That is the entire interaction, and it is fast enough that it becomes reflexive within a day or two of use.

When I am actively working on one project, I keep that project's group expanded and collapse everything else. My tab bar goes from an overwhelming mess of 50 tabs to one expanded group of 8 tabs and five collapsed labels. I can see the titles of the tabs I am actually using, and the rest stay accessible but out of the way.

There is an important nuance that catches people off guard. Collapsing a group does not free up memory. Every tab in a collapsed group is still fully loaded in RAM and may still be running JavaScript, playing audio, or maintaining network connections. Collapsing is purely a visual organization tool, not a performance optimization.

Chrome's Memory Saver feature (enabled in Settings under Performance) can help here. It automatically discards tabs that have been inactive for a while, including tabs in collapsed groups. When you expand a group and click a discarded tab, Chrome reloads it. This combination of collapsed groups plus Memory Saver gives you both the organizational benefit and actual memory savings.

Note. If you accidentally close a collapsed group, all tabs inside it are closed. Chrome does include closed groups in the "Recently closed" list (accessible via Ctrl+Shift+T or the three-dot menu), but restoring a large group this way is unreliable. This is one of the main problems that Tab Group Manager solves.

Moving Tabs Between Groups

Moving tabs between groups is drag-and-drop. Click a tab and drag it from its current group into another group. Chrome highlights the destination group as you hover over it, confirming where the tab will land. You can also drag a tab out of a group entirely to make it ungrouped, or drag an ungrouped tab into any existing group.

To move multiple tabs at once, select them with Ctrl+Click or Shift+Click and drag the entire selection. This is particularly useful when reorganizing at the start of a new work session. I often find that tabs I opened the previous day belong in different groups than where they ended up, and bulk-dragging lets me clean up the organization in a few seconds.

You can also right-click a tab and use the "Move tab to group" option to move it into a specific existing group or remove it from its current group. This method is more precise than dragging when your tab bar is crowded and drag targets are small.

Entire groups can be rearranged in the tab bar by clicking and dragging the group label. If I want my active project group on the left and my reference tabs on the right, I just drag the group label to the desired position. All tabs within the group move together as a unit.

A less obvious but useful technique is merging groups. Chrome does not have a "merge" button, but you can achieve the same result by selecting all tabs in one group, dragging them into another group, and then deleting the now-empty original group. I do this when I realize that two groups I created separately are really part of the same context.

Moving tabs between windows

Tab groups work within a single Chrome window. You cannot drag a group directly between windows. However, you can right-click a group label and select "Move group to new window," which detaches the entire group into its own window. To merge it back, select the tabs in that window and drag them into a group in your main window.

This limitation is one of my few frustrations with the feature. When I have two Chrome windows open and want to move a group from one to the other, the workflow requires more steps than it should. Selecting all tabs in the group, dragging them to the other window, and then recreating the group there is tedious. Extensions like Tab Group Manager make this easier by letting you save and restore groups across windows.

Keyboard Shortcuts for Tab Groups

Chrome's built-in keyboard shortcut support for tab groups is, to be blunt, limited. There is no default shortcut to create a new group, add a tab to a group, or toggle group collapse. The tab group feature was designed primarily around mouse interaction, which is fine for casual use but slows down keyboard-oriented workflows.

That said, existing Chrome tab shortcuts still work with grouped tabs, and there are workarounds that help.

The Ctrl+Shift+A shortcut deserves special mention. It opens Chrome's tab search interface, which shows all open tabs across all groups and windows. You can type a few characters to filter by tab title or URL, and the search results show which group each tab belongs to. When I have 40 or 50 tabs open, this is the fastest way to jump to a specific one without scrolling through the tab bar.

For people who want true keyboard control over tab groups, the Chrome Extensions API supports custom keyboard shortcuts. Extensions like Tab Group Manager let you assign hotkeys to common group operations. The Chrome shortcuts page at chrome://extensions/shortcuts is where you configure these.

Tip. If you use tab search regularly, consider combining it with consistent group naming. When all my project groups are named with a specific prefix, typing that prefix in tab search instantly filters down to just the tabs from that project. Naming conventions pay off in searchability, not just visual clarity.

Chrome's built-in tab groups have one significant limitation that becomes obvious the moment you close your browser. Groups are tied to your current session. If Chrome crashes, if you close a window by accident, or if you restart your computer, your carefully organized groups may vanish. Chrome introduced a "Save group" feature in version 119 that pins groups to the bookmarks bar, but it is inconsistent. Saved groups sometimes disappear after updates, and the restore process is not always clean.

My Tab Group Workflow

I have been using tab groups for over two years now, and my workflow has stabilized into a pattern that keeps me organized without requiring constant maintenance. Here is how I actually use the system on a typical day.

Morning setup

I open Chrome and restore my active project session through Tab Group Manager. This gives me three or four groups immediately: the project codebase (GitHub, local dev server, deployment dashboard), documentation (API references, framework docs, design specs), communication (GitHub issues, Slack, email), and testing (staging URLs, Chrome DevTools, performance monitoring). All groups start collapsed except the one I plan to work on first.

During the work session

As I work, new tabs open inside whatever group is currently expanded. If I need to look something up, I open a new tab, do the search, and the result tabs stay in context. When I switch to a different group, I collapse the current one first. This keeps my tab bar clean and forces a deliberate context switch rather than absent-minded tab-hopping between projects.

When tabs accumulate that do not fit any existing group, I let them pile up as ungrouped tabs at the end of the tab bar. Every hour or so, I spend 30 seconds sorting them. Most get dragged into an existing group. Some get closed because I already found what I needed. Occasionally one sparks a new group if I have started exploring a new topic.

End of day

Before closing Chrome, I save my current groups through Tab Group Manager. I close tabs I will not need tomorrow but keep the group structures intact. If a project is on hold, I save the group and then close it. If a project is active, I leave the saved version updated so it survives any unexpected closure overnight.

Weekly cleanup

Once a week, usually Friday afternoon, I review my saved groups in Tab Group Manager and delete any that are no longer relevant. Projects that shipped, research that concluded, and reference tabs I no longer need all get cleaned out. This prevents the saved groups list from becoming its own form of clutter.

The entire system takes about two minutes of daily maintenance. The time I save by not searching through 50 unorganized tabs, not losing important pages to accidental closure, and not rebuilding context after a browser restart more than justifies those two minutes.

Tab Groups vs Multiple Windows vs Profiles

Tab groups are not the only way to organize your Chrome experience. Multiple windows and Chrome profiles are older approaches that serve overlapping purposes. Understanding when to use each method, and when to combine them, makes a meaningful difference in how well the system works for you.

Tab groups within a single window

This is the lightest-weight approach. All your tabs live in one window, organized by colored groups. You can see everything at a glance, switch between contexts by expanding and collapsing groups, and drag tabs between groups freely.

The strength is simplicity. There is one window in your taskbar, one Alt+Tab target, and one unified tab bar. The weakness is scale. When you have more than about eight groups, the tab bar gets crowded even with most groups collapsed. And because all groups share one window, closing the window closes everything.

Multiple windows

Putting different projects in separate windows provides strong visual separation. Each window has its own tab bar, and you can use your operating system's window management (tiling, virtual desktops, Alt+Tab) to switch between them.

The strength is isolation. Closing one window does not affect others. You can put different windows on different monitors. Each window can use its own tab groups for sub-organization. The weakness is fragmentation. It is harder to move tabs between windows. You lose the single-glance overview of all your work. And Chrome's memory usage increases because each window runs as a separate process group.

Chrome profiles

Profiles are the most heavyweight separation. Each profile has its own extensions, bookmarks, history, saved passwords, and signed-in accounts. Profiles are completely isolated from each other.

The strength is total separation. Your work profile's extensions do not interfere with your personal profile. You can sign into different Google accounts. Cookies and login sessions are completely separate. The weakness is friction. Switching profiles opens a new Chrome window, and you cannot drag tabs between profiles at all. Profiles are meant for separating identities, not organizing tabs.

My recommendation is to combine all three based on what each does best. Use profiles for identity separation: one for work, one for personal. Within each profile, use tab groups for project-level organization. Use separate windows only when you need to put different work on different monitors or when a project is significant enough to deserve its own dedicated workspace.

I personally run two Chrome profiles (work and personal). Within my work profile, I use five to seven tab groups on a typical day. Occasionally I will pop a group into its own window when I need it on a second monitor for reference while coding. This layered approach gives me the benefits of all three methods without the downsides of relying on any single one.
