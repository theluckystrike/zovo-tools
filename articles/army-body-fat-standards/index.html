Every soldier in the United States Army must meet body composition standards defined in Army Regulation 600-9 (AR 600-9), "The Army Body Composition Program." These standards apply to all active duty, Reserve, and National Guard soldiers regardless of rank, MOS, or duty assignment. Failing to meet these standards triggers enrollment in the Army Body Composition Program (ABCP), which carries significant career consequences including suspension of promotions and potential separation.

This guide covers every aspect of the Army body fat assessment process, from the initial height and weight screening to the tape test procedure, the body fat formulas, limits by age and gender, what happens when soldiers fail, and practical strategies for meeting the standard. The Two-Step Assessment Process

The Army's body composition assessment has two phases. The first phase is the height and weight screening. Every soldier is weighed and measured for height. If the soldier's weight is at or below the screening weight listed in the AR 600-9 tables for their height, age, and gender, they pass and no further assessment is needed.

The second phase is the tape test, which only applies to soldiers who exceed their screening weight. The tape test uses circumference measurements to estimate body fat percentage using a Department of Defense formula. If the soldier's estimated body fat is at or below the maximum allowable percentage for their age and gender, they pass despite exceeding the screening weight.

Soldiers are assessed at least once every six months, typically in conjunction with the Army Physical Fitness Test (APFT) or Army Combat Fitness Test (ACFT). Commanders may direct additional assessments as needed. The assessment is also required for entry into the Army (initial accession standards), attendance at military schools, and certain personnel actions.

The Tape Test Procedure Step by Step

The tape test must be administered by trained personnel following the exact procedure specified in AR 600-9. Deviation from the standard procedure invalidates the results. Here is how it works for each gender.

Tape Test for Males

Two measurement sites are used for males: the neck and the abdomen.

Neck measurement. The soldier stands with the head erect, looking straight ahead. The measurer places the tape around the neck just below the larynx (Adam's apple). The tape is kept perpendicular to the long axis of the neck. The measurer ensures the tape does not contact the chin. The tape is snug but not compressing the skin. Three measurements are taken and the average is recorded to the nearest half inch, rounding up.

Abdomen measurement. The soldier stands erect with arms at the sides and feet together. The measurer places the tape horizontally around the abdomen at the level of the navel (belly button). The tape must be level all the way around and snug but not compressing the skin. The soldier breathes normally and the measurement is taken at the end of a normal exhalation. Three measurements are taken and the average is recorded to the nearest half inch, rounding down.

The body fat formula for males uses height, neck circumference, and abdomen circumference:

% Body Fat (Male) = 86.010 x log10(abdomen - neck) - 70.041 x log10(height) + 36.76

For example, a male soldier who is 70 inches tall with a 16-inch neck and a 36-inch abdomen:

Abdomen - Neck = 36 - 16 = 20

log10(20) = 1.3010

log10(70) = 1.8451

% Body Fat = 86.010 x 1.3010 - 70.041 x 1.8451 + 36.76

% Body Fat = 111.90 - 129.23 + 36.76

% Body Fat = 19.43%

Tape Test for Females

Three measurement sites are used for females: the neck, the natural waist, and the hips.

Neck measurement. Same procedure as males. The tape goes around the neck just below the larynx, perpendicular to the long axis of the neck. Three measurements, averaged, recorded to the nearest half inch rounding up.

Waist measurement. The soldier stands erect with the abdomen relaxed. The measurer identifies the natural waist, which is the narrowest point of the torso as viewed from the front, usually midway between the lowest rib and the top of the hip bone (iliac crest). The tape is placed horizontally at this level. Three measurements are taken and the average is recorded to the nearest half inch, rounding down.

Hip measurement. The soldier stands erect with feet together. The measurer identifies the widest point of the buttocks as viewed from the side. The tape is placed horizontally at this level, ensuring it is level all the way around. Three measurements are taken and the average is recorded to the nearest half inch, rounding down.

The body fat formula for females uses height, neck circumference, waist circumference, and hip circumference:

% Body Fat (Female) = 163.205 x log10(waist + hip - neck) - 97.684 x log10(height) - 78.387

For example, a female soldier who is 64 inches tall with a 13.5-inch neck, a 28-inch waist, and a 38-inch hip:

Waist + Hip - Neck = 28 + 38 - 13.5 = 52.5

log10(52.5) = 1.7202

log10(64) = 1.8062

% Body Fat = 163.205 x 1.7202 - 97.684 x 1.8062 - 78.387

% Body Fat = 280.74 - 176.40 - 78.387

% Body Fat = 25.95%

Body Fat Limits by Age and Gender

The maximum allowable body fat percentages are defined in AR 600-9 Table 3-1. These limits account for natural differences in body composition between genders and the tendency for body fat to increase with age.

Women are permitted higher body fat percentages because female physiology requires more essential fat for reproductive health, hormonal function, and organ protection. Essential fat in women is approximately 10 to 13% compared to 2 to 5% in men. The Army's limits account for this biological difference while still maintaining a standard for military readiness.

Initial entry standards for accession (entering the Army) are stricter. Males entering the Army must be at or below 26% body fat regardless of age. Females must be at or below 32% regardless of age. The higher limits for older age groups apply only to soldiers already serving.

What Happens When You Fail

Failing the body composition assessment triggers a series of consequences that escalate over time if the soldier does not improve. The system is designed to give soldiers a chance to meet the standard, but persistent failure leads to separation.

Immediate Consequences

A DA Form 5500 (for males) or DA Form 5501 (for females) is completed documenting the tape test results and body fat percentage. The soldier is flagged under AR 600-8-2 with a non-transferable flag that suspends favorable personnel actions.

Favorable actions that are suspended include promotions, reenlistment, attendance at military or civilian schools, award recommendations, and assumption of command. The flag remains in effect until the soldier meets the body fat standard.

Enrollment in the Army Body Composition Program

The soldier is formally enrolled in the ABCP. Requirements during enrollment include monthly counseling by the chain of command, monthly weigh-ins and tape tests, participation in a mandatory exercise program (minimum 5 days per week), and nutritional counseling through the installation's Army Wellness Center or dietitian.

Progress requirements are specific. The soldier must show a minimum loss of 1% body fat per month, or a weight loss of 3 to 8 pounds per month (depending on how far over the standard they are). The commander determines the specific monthly goal based on how much the soldier needs to lose.

Consequences of Failing to Progress

If a soldier does not meet the monthly progress requirement, the commander must initiate a bar to continued service or an administrative separation action. Soldiers who remain in the ABCP for 6 months without meeting the body fat standard face separation. Soldiers who meet the standard, are removed from the ABCP, and then fail again within 36 months face separation on the second enrollment.

Separation for failure to meet body fat standards is typically under Chapter 18 of AR 635-200, which provides for an honorable discharge unless other misconduct is involved. However, the separation itself can impact future benefits, educational opportunities, and the soldier's ability to return to military service.

The Accuracy and Limitations of the Tape Test

The tape test is the subject of frequent debate within the military community. Compared to gold-standard methods like DEXA scanning (dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry), hydrostatic weighing, or the Bod Pod (air displacement plethysmography), the tape test has a standard error of estimate of approximately 3 to 4 percentage points.

This means a soldier whose true body fat is 22% might measure anywhere from 18% to 26% on the tape test. For soldiers near the limit, this margin of error can be the difference between passing and failing.

The tape test tends to overestimate body fat in soldiers with muscular builds. A soldier with significant muscle mass in the torso will have a larger abdominal circumference even with low actual body fat. Conversely, the test tends to underestimate body fat in soldiers with thin torsos who carry more fat on their arms and legs, because the formula only measures the neck, abdomen, and (for females) waist and hips.

The Army has periodically considered alternative measurement methods. In 2023, the Army conducted a pilot program evaluating handheld bioelectrical impedance devices as a potential replacement or supplement to the tape test. As of 2026, the tape test remains the official standard, though ongoing research continues to evaluate alternatives.

Despite its limitations, the tape test has practical advantages. It requires only a cloth measuring tape, can be administered anywhere including field environments, requires minimal training to perform, and produces results immediately. These factors make it suitable for assessing large numbers of soldiers across diverse locations.

Measurement Technique Details That Matter

Small variations in measurement technique can change results by 1 to 2 percentage points, which matters for soldiers near the limit. These details are specified in AR 600-9 and DA Pamphlet 600-9.

The measuring tape must be a non-stretchable tape, not a metal measuring tape or a tailor's tape that has been stretched through use. Tapes should be checked periodically against a ruler or yardstick. A tape that has stretched by even a quarter inch can change the body fat calculation.

Tension on the tape is critical. The instruction is "snug but not compressing the skin." In practice, this means the tape contacts the skin all the way around without causing visible indentation. Too loose and the measurement reads high. Too tight and it reads low. The same person should ideally do all measurements for consistency.

Three measurements at each site are mandatory, not optional. If any single measurement differs from the others by more than one inch, all three measurements at that site should be retaken. The average of the three acceptable measurements is used. This averaging process reduces random error from slight differences in tape placement.

The rounding rules differ between measurement sites. Neck measurements are rounded to the nearest half inch, rounding up (which favors the soldier, as a larger neck measurement reduces the calculated body fat). Abdomen, waist, and hip measurements are rounded to the nearest half inch, rounding down (which also favors the soldier, as smaller torso measurements reduce the calculated body fat).

Time of day matters. Measurements taken in the morning before eating typically show smaller abdominal circumference than measurements taken after a large meal in the evening. AR 600-9 does not specify a time of day, but soldiers should request a morning measurement if possible.

Strategies for Meeting the Standard

Meeting Army body fat standards requires attention to two variables: reducing body fat and maintaining or increasing neck circumference relative to torso measurements. The formula mathematically rewards a larger neck-to-abdomen difference for males and a larger neck relative to waist and hips for females.

Fat Loss Fundamentals

Reducing actual body fat requires a sustained caloric deficit. A deficit of 500 to 750 calories per day produces fat loss of approximately 1 to 1.5 pounds per week, which corresponds to roughly 0.5 to 1.0 percentage points of body fat per month for most soldiers. This rate aligns with the Army's minimum progress requirement of 1% per month.

Protein intake during a caloric deficit should be 0.7 to 1.0 grams per pound of body weight per day. Higher protein intake preserves lean muscle mass during weight loss and helps maintain the metabolic rate. For a 200-pound soldier, this means 140 to 200 grams of protein daily.

Cardiovascular exercise and resistance training together produce better body composition changes than either alone. Running alone may reduce weight but also reduces muscle mass, which can actually worsen the tape test numbers by reducing neck circumference while the abdomen shrinks proportionally less.

Neck Training

The neck circumference subtracts from the torso measurements in the body fat formula. Every half inch of neck growth reduces calculated body fat by approximately 1 percentage point. Neck-specific resistance exercises include neck curls (flexion), neck extensions, lateral flexion, and shrugs.

A simple neck training protocol involves 3 sets of 15 to 20 repetitions of each movement, 2 to 3 times per week, using a neck harness, manual resistance, or a plate held behind the head. Neck growth is slow but measurable. Most soldiers can add 0.5 to 1 inch of neck circumference over 3 to 6 months of dedicated training.

Hydration and Sodium Management

Water retention can add 0.5 to 1.5 inches to abdominal circumference. In the 48 to 72 hours before a tape test, reduce sodium intake to below 2,000 mg per day (the average American consumes 3,400 mg). Increase water intake to at least 1 gallon per day, which paradoxically helps the body release retained water. Avoid alcohol, which promotes dehydration and subsequent water retention.

Timing and Meal Planning

Avoid eating within 3 hours of the tape test. Food volume in the stomach and intestines adds to abdominal circumference. A morning test on an empty stomach typically produces the smallest abdominal measurement. If the test is in the afternoon, eat only small, low-fiber, low-sodium meals earlier in the day.

AR 600-9 Exemptions and Special Cases

Certain soldiers may receive temporary or permanent exemptions from the body composition assessment. Pregnant soldiers are exempt from weigh-ins and tape tests from the date of pregnancy confirmation until 180 days postpartum. Soldiers on a temporary medical profile that prevents physical activity may receive a temporary exemption at the commander's discretion, but the exemption must be documented and reviewed monthly.

Soldiers who are hospitalized or on convalescent leave are exempt for the duration plus 30 days. Soldiers deploying to a combat zone may have their ABCP enrollment timeline paused, depending on the specific deployment conditions and commander's guidance.

There is no exemption for body type, genetics, or previous athletic background. A former competitive powerlifter who carries significant muscle mass and exceeds the screening weight must still pass the tape test. If the tape test formula estimates their body fat above the limit, they are enrolled in the ABCP regardless of their actual fitness level.

Historical Context and Ongoing Changes

AR 600-9 has been revised multiple times since its original publication. The body fat limits have remained relatively stable, but the implementation details have evolved. In 2019, the Army briefly considered raising the body fat limits by 2% across all categories, but the proposal was not adopted. In 2023, the Army began a complete health and fitness initiative (H2F) that integrates body composition standards with overall physical readiness, sleep, nutrition, and mental health.

The H2F system places dietitians, strength coaches, and physical therapists at the brigade level, giving soldiers better access to professional guidance for meeting body composition standards. This represents a shift from a punitive-only approach to one that provides resources for improvement alongside the existing standards.

As of 2026, AR 600-9 remains in effect as the governing regulation. Soldiers should always reference the most current version of the regulation and consult their chain of command for unit-specific policies regarding body composition assessments.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the Army body fat limits for males?

For male soldiers, the Army body fat limits are 26% for ages 17 to 20, 26% for ages 21 to 27, 28% for ages 28 to 39, and 30% for ages 40 and over. These limits apply under AR 600-9 and are assessed using the circumference-based tape test method when a soldier exceeds the screening weight in the height/weight table.

What are the Army body fat limits for females?

For female soldiers, the Army body fat limits are 32% for ages 17 to 20, 32% for ages 21 to 27, 34% for ages 28 to 39, and 36% for ages 40 and over. Women have higher allowable body fat percentages because females naturally carry more essential fat than males. The tape test measurement sites for women differ from men, using neck, waist, and hip circumferences.

How does the Army tape test work?

The Army tape test measures circumferences at specific body sites. For males, the neck and abdomen (at the navel) are measured. For females, the neck, natural waist, and hips (at the widest point) are measured. Each site is measured three times and the average is recorded to the nearest half inch. The circumference values are then entered into the Department of Defense body fat formula, which uses logarithmic calculations along with the soldier's height to estimate body fat percentage.

What happens if you fail the Army body fat test?

Soldiers who exceed body fat standards are enrolled in the Army Body Composition Program (ABCP). They are flagged, which suspends favorable personnel actions including promotions, awards, and school attendance. They receive monthly counseling and are retaped every 30 days. They must show a minimum loss of 1% body fat per month or 3 to 8 pounds of weight loss per month. Soldiers who fail to make satisfactory progress after 6 months, or who are enrolled in the ABCP twice within a 36-month period, face separation from the Army.

Is the Army tape test accurate?

The Army tape test has a standard error of approximately 3 to 4 percentage points compared to more accurate methods like DEXA scanning or hydrostatic weighing. It tends to overestimate body fat in muscular individuals because large neck and waist measurements can exist on lean, muscular bodies. It can also underestimate body fat in individuals who carry more fat on their extremities rather than their torso. Despite its limitations, the tape test remains the standard because it requires no equipment beyond a measuring tape and can be administered anywhere.

Can you fail height and weight but pass the tape test?

Yes. The height and weight screening table is only the first step. If a soldier exceeds the screening weight for their height, they are automatically taped. Many soldiers, especially those with significant muscle mass, exceed the screening weight but have body fat percentages well within the allowable limits. In this case, the soldier passes and is not enrolled in the ABCP. The tape test is the definitive measurement, not the scale.

How should you prepare for the Army tape test?

In the days before a tape test, stay well hydrated because dehydration can cause water retention that increases circumference measurements. Avoid high-sodium foods for 48 to 72 hours before the test, as excess sodium promotes water retention especially around the abdomen. Do not eat a large meal within 2 to 3 hours of the test. Some soldiers do light abdominal exercises the morning of the test to temporarily tighten the midsection, though the effect is minimal. Long-term preparation should focus on reducing actual body fat through caloric deficit and maintaining or building neck muscle through resistance training.

What is the Army screening weight table?

The screening weight table lists the maximum allowable weight for each height before a soldier must be taped. For example, a 70-inch (5 foot 10) male aged 21 to 27 has a screening weight of 192 pounds. A 64-inch (5 foot 4) female aged 21 to 27 has a screening weight of 150 pounds. The table accounts for age, with slightly higher allowances for older soldiers. Soldiers at or below their screening weight are not required to be taped regardless of their appearance.

Does the Army use BMI instead of the tape test?

No. The Army does not use BMI (Body Mass Index) as a body composition standard. BMI is a ratio of weight to height that does not distinguish between muscle and fat. A soldier with 10% body fat and heavy muscle mass could have a BMI of 30 (classified as obese), which would be misleading. The Army uses the height/weight screening table as a first filter, and the circumference-based tape test as the official body fat measurement. BMI is not part of the AR 600-9 assessment process.
