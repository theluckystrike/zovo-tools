How Compound Interest Really Works: The Math They Didn't Teach You in School

I have watched people spend decades confused about compound interest because nobody walked them through the actual arithmetic. This is that walkthrough. Real formulas, real dollar amounts, real comparisons. No hand-waving.

By Michael Lip / March 25, 2026 / 22 min read

The Formula That Runs Your Financial Life

Every savings account, every retirement plan, every credit card balance, every mortgage runs on the same equation. If you understand this formula, you understand more about personal finance than most people learn in a lifetime.

A = P(1 + r/n)nt

Five variables. That is all there is.

  • A = the final amount (what you end up with)
  • P = principal (the starting amount)
  • r = annual interest rate (as a decimal, so 7% = 0.07)
  • n = number of times interest compounds per year
  • t = time in years

Let me walk through a specific example with every step shown. You deposit $10,000 into an account that pays 7% annual interest, compounded monthly, and you leave it alone for 30 years.

Step 1: Identify the variables. P = 10,000. r = 0.07. n = 12 (monthly). t = 30.

Step 2: Calculate r/n. That is 0.07 / 12 = 0.005833.

Step 3: Calculate nt. That is 12 * 30 = 360 compounding periods.

Step 4: Calculate (1 + r/n). That is 1 + 0.005833 = 1.005833.

Step 5: Raise to the power of nt. 1.005833^360 = 8.1165.

Step 6: Multiply by P. 10,000 * 8.1165 = $81,165.

Your $10,000 turned into $81,165. You earned $71,165 in interest without doing anything. That is the entire story of compound interest: interest earns interest, and given enough time, the interest on the interest dwarfs your original deposit.

Simple Interest vs. Compound Interest, Side by Side

Simple interest uses a different formula: A = P(1 + rt). No exponent. The interest is calculated only on the original principal, never on accumulated interest.

That $10,000 at 7% simple interest for 30 years: A = 10,000(1 + 0.07 * 30) = 10,000 * 3.1 = $31,000. You earned $21,000 in interest.

Compound interest on the same amount: $81,165. You earned $71,165.

The compound version produced $50,165 more. That extra money is interest earned on interest. In year 1, both simple and compound interest earn roughly the same amount ($700). But by year 20, simple interest still earns $700 per year while compound interest earns over $2,800 per year because the base has grown. By year 30, compound interest earns over $5,300 per year.

Daily vs. Monthly vs. Annual Compounding: Does It Actually Matter?

Banks advertise "daily compounding" as if it is a major selling point. I wanted to know exactly how much it matters, so I ran the numbers for $50,000 at 5% over various time periods with different compounding frequencies.

If you are comparing two savings accounts and one compounds daily while the other compounds monthly, look at the APY (which already accounts for compounding frequency) rather than the compounding schedule. The APY is the number that actually tells you what you will earn.

APR vs. APY: The Number Banks Want You to Confuse

APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is the stated rate before compounding. APY (Annual Percentage Yield) is the effective rate after compounding. When a bank advertises a savings account, they show you the APY because it is the bigger number. When a credit card company advertises a rate, they show you the APR because it is the smaller number.

The conversion formula from APR to APY is:

APY = (1 + APR/n)n - 1

At low rates, the difference is small. A 2% APR compounded monthly gives an APY of 2.02%. At high rates, the difference is significant. A 24% APR compounded daily gives an APY of 27.11%. That 3.11 percentage point difference on a credit card balance is hundreds of dollars per year in additional interest that the APR number did not prepare you for.

The Rule of 72: Quick Math That Actually Works

The Rule of 72 is a mental math shortcut for estimating how long it takes money to double at a given interest rate. Divide 72 by the annual rate.

Years to Double = 72 / Annual Interest Rate (%)

At 6%, money doubles in 72/6 = 12 years. At 8%, 72/8 = 9 years. At 10%, 72/10 = 7.2 years. At 12%, 72/12 = 6 years.

I find this rule genuinely useful for quick sanity checks. If someone offers me an investment that "doubles in 4 years," the Rule of 72 tells me they are claiming an 18% annual return. That is possible in short bursts but extremely rare as a sustained rate. The S&P 500 has averaged about 10% annually since 1926, which means doubling roughly every 7.2 years.

The Rule of 72 Applied to Inflation

This rule works for anything that grows (or shrinks) at a compound rate. At 3% annual inflation, the purchasing power of cash halves in 72/3 = 24 years. That means $100,000 sitting in a checking account earning 0% interest buys what $50,000 buys today, 24 years from now. At 7% inflation (which the U.S. experienced in 2021-2022), purchasing power halves in just over 10 years.

This is why not investing carries its own risk. Cash does not stay the same value. It erodes.

401k Math: $500/Month Starting at 25 vs. 35

This is the comparison that I think should be shown to every 22-year-old on the day they get their first job. I have run it with specific numbers so there is no ambiguity.

Assumptions: $500/month contribution. 8% annual return (roughly the inflation-adjusted S&P 500 average). Monthly compounding. No employer match (we will add that separately).

Starting at Age 25, Retiring at 65 (40 years)

Monthly contribution: $500. Total contributions: $500 * 12 * 40 = $240,000. Using the future value of annuity formula: FV = 500 * [((1 + 0.08/12)^480 - 1) / (0.08/12)].

Final balance: $1,745,504. Interest earned: $1,505,504. Your contributions represent 13.7% of the final balance. Compound interest generated 86.3%.

Starting at Age 35, Retiring at 65 (30 years)

Monthly contribution: $500. Total contributions: $500 * 12 * 30 = $180,000. FV = 500 * [((1 + 0.08/12)^360 - 1) / (0.08/12)].

Final balance: $745,180. Interest earned: $565,180. Your contributions represent 24.2% of the final balance.

The $1,000,324 Cost of Waiting

Starting 10 years later costs $1,000,324 in final balance. The extra contributions from those 10 years total only $60,000. The remaining $940,324 is compound interest that those early contributions would have generated over 40 years instead of 30.

Put differently: each dollar invested at age 25 grows to $21.72 by age 65 at 8% compounded monthly. Each dollar invested at age 35 grows to $10.06. Early dollars are worth more than twice as much as later dollars, not because of magic, but because of time in the exponent.

Adding Employer Match to the Picture

Most 401k plans include an employer match, often 50% of your contribution up to 6% of salary, or 100% up to 3-4% of salary. Not contributing enough to get the full match is literally leaving free money on the table.

Let me add a typical employer match to the scenarios above. Assume a $75,000 salary with 50% match on contributions up to 6% of salary.

Your 6% contribution: $375/month. Employer adds: $187.50/month. Total going into the account: $562.50/month.

At 8% for 30 years (starting at 35, retiring at 65): your contributions total $135,000. Employer match totals $67,500. Combined contributions: $202,500. Final balance: $838,328. Interest earned: $635,828.

The employer match alone, that $187.50/month, compounds to $279,105 over 30 years. Someone who did not know about the match and contributed only $375/month without it would end up with $559,224. The match adds $279,105 of free growth.

And if salary grows at 3% per year, both contributions and match increase proportionally, pushing the final balance even higher. A savings goal calculator helps you determine how much you need to save monthly to hit a specific retirement target.

Compound Interest Working Against You: Credit Card Debt

Everything I have described so far applies in reverse when you are the borrower. And the interest rates on consumer debt make the compounding brutal.

The average credit card APR in 2026 is 22.8%. Credit cards compound daily. Let me trace what happens to a $6,000 credit card balance if you make only minimum payments.

Minimum payment rules vary by issuer, but a common formula is 2% of the outstanding balance or $25, whichever is greater.

Month 1: Balance $6,000. Interest for the month: $6,000 * (0.228/365) * 30 = $112.44. New balance: $6,112.44. Minimum payment: 2% of $6,112.44 = $122.25. Applied to principal: $122.25 - $112.44 = $9.81. Remaining balance: $5,990.19.

Read that again. You paid $122.25, and only $9.81 went toward reducing your debt. The other 92% went to interest.

Month 2: Balance $5,990.19. Interest: $112.26. Minimum payment: $121.76. Applied to principal: $9.50. Remaining balance: $5,980.69.

At this rate, it takes approximately 313 months (26 years) to pay off the original $6,000 balance. Total interest paid: $11,577. Total amount paid: $17,577 for a $6,000 purchase.

The minimum payment trap works because the payment shrinks as the balance shrinks. You start at $122/month but the payment drops to $80, then $50, then $25. Each reduction extends the timeline and maximizes total interest. Credit card companies designed this system deliberately.

The Compounding Curve: Why the Last 10 Years Matter Most

One of the most counterintuitive aspects of compound interest is that the majority of the growth happens at the end. Let me show this with a specific example.

In the first decade, the account grew by $91,473. In the fourth decade, it grew by $1,000,324. The growth in the final decade is more than ten times the growth in the first decade. This is exponential growth made tangible.

This pattern has a practical implication for people approaching retirement. If you have been investing for 30 years and your balance is $745,180, the temptation might be to think "I have been doing this forever and only have $745k." But the next 10 years would add another million dollars. Pulling out early means missing the steepest part of the curve.

Real Return vs. Nominal Return

All the numbers above are nominal (not adjusted for inflation). For long-term planning, you need to think in real terms: what your money will actually buy in the future.

The rough formula for real return is:

Real Return = Nominal Return - Inflation Rate

The more precise formula (for the exacting among us) is:

Real Return = (1 + Nominal) / (1 + Inflation) - 1

At 10% nominal and 3% inflation: real return = 1.10/1.03 - 1 = 6.8%. Over 30 years, the difference between calculating with 10% nominal and 6.8% real is massive.

Calculation Method$10,000 at 30 YearsWhat It Actually Means
10% nominal$174,494Future dollars (inflated)
6.8% real$71,880Today's purchasing power

$174,494 sounds incredible. $71,880 sounds more modest. Both are the same outcome, just measured differently. The $71,880 figure is what your money will actually buy in terms of goods and services valued at today's prices.

Neither number is "wrong," but planning your retirement on nominal numbers without accounting for inflation will leave you short. When I plan, I use real returns. When I want motivation to keep investing, I look at nominal numbers. Know which one you are looking at.

The Continuous Compounding Limit

If you keep increasing the compounding frequency (daily, hourly, by the minute, by the second), you approach a mathematical limit called continuous compounding. The formula uses Euler's number (e, approximately 2.71828).

A = Pert

For $10,000 at 7% for 30 years: A = 10,000 * e^(0.07 * 30) = 10,000 * e^2.1 = 10,000 * 8.1662 = $81,662.

Compare to monthly compounding: $81,165. Continuous compounding adds $497 over 30 years. The theoretical maximum from infinite compounding frequency is not meaningfully different from daily compounding.

This is mathematically elegant but practically irrelevant. I include it because I find it satisfying that the universe of compound interest has a clean upper bound, and because understanding it helps you recognize when someone is overselling the importance of compounding frequency.

Compound Interest and Taxes: The Hidden Drag

In a taxable brokerage account, you owe taxes on dividends and realized capital gains each year. This tax drag reduces the effective compound rate.

If your investments return 10% annually and you face a 24% federal tax rate on those gains, your after-tax return is roughly 7.6%. Over 30 years, the difference between compounding at 10% and compounding at 7.6% is enormous.

Why Most People Underestimate Compound Interest

Humans are bad at intuiting exponential growth. Our brains evolved to think linearly. If something grows by $700 this year, we instinctively expect it to grow by about $700 next year. But compound interest does not work linearly. The growth accelerates because each increment of growth increases the base for the next increment.

There is a well-known experiment where people are asked: "Would you rather have $1 million today or a penny that doubles every day for 30 days?" Most people take the million. The penny reaches $5,368,709.12 on day 30. On day 20, it is only $5,242.88, which is why most people's intuition fails: the first two-thirds of the timeline look unimpressive.

This same psychological pattern explains why young people do not save for retirement. At age 25, investing $500/month feels pointless because after 5 years you have $36,738, most of which is your own contributions. The account does not look like it is "doing anything." But those early contributions are the seeds of the exponential curve. Abandoning the plan because year 5 looks boring means missing year 35, when the account is growing by $100,000+ per year on autopilot.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the compound interest formula?

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt). P is the principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is compounding periods per year, and t is time in years. For $10,000 at 7% compounded monthly for 30 years: A = 10,000(1 + 0.07/12)^(360) = $81,165.

Does daily compounding earn significantly more than monthly?

Less than most people think. On $50,000 at 5% over 20 years, monthly yields $135,632 and daily yields $135,906. That is $274 over two decades. The jump from annual to monthly is far more meaningful: $132,665 vs $135,632, a $2,967 gap.

How accurate is the Rule of 72?

Very accurate between 4% and 15%. At 8%, the Rule of 72 predicts 9.0 years to double; the actual answer is 9.01 years. Below 4% and above 15%, the error grows. At 20%, it predicts 3.6 years but the actual is 3.8 years. For everyday financial planning, it is reliable enough to use without a calculator.

How much does starting 10 years earlier really matter?

At $500/month and 8% return: starting at age 25 gives you $1,745,504 by 65. Starting at 35 gives you $745,180. The 10-year head start creates a $1,000,324 difference. Only $60,000 of that is additional contributions. The remaining $940,324 is compound interest on those early contributions.

How does compound interest work against me with credit cards?

Credit cards compound daily at rates averaging 22.8%. A $6,000 balance with minimum payments takes 26 years to pay off and costs $11,577 in interest. You end up paying $17,577 total for the original $6,000. The daily compounding means interest generates its own interest immediately.

What is the difference between APR and APY?

APR is the stated rate without compounding. APY includes the effect of compounding. A 5% APR compounded daily produces a 5.13% APY. For credit cards at 24% APR compounded daily, the APY is 27.11%. Always compare APY for savings accounts and be aware of the APY on debt.

Does inflation cancel out compound interest?

Not unless your return equals the inflation rate. At 10% nominal return and 3% inflation, your real return is about 6.8%. $10,000 at 6.8% real return for 30 years becomes $71,880 in today's purchasing power. That is still a 618% gain. Cash at 0% loses 59% of its purchasing power over the same period.

Is there a formula for regular monthly contributions?

Yes. The future value of an annuity formula: FV = PMT * [((1 + r/n)^(nt) - 1) / (r/n)]. For $500/month at 8% for 30 years: FV = $745,180. Your total contributions were $180,000. Compound interest generated $565,180, over three times your investment.
