Definition: According to Wikipedia, A barcode is a method of representing data in a visual, machine-readable form. Initially, barcodes represented data by varying the widths, spacings, and sizes of parallel lines. These linear barcodes can be scanned by optical scanners called barcode readers.

A Brief History of Barcode Technology

The barcode concept predates the modern computer. In 1948, Bernard Silver and Norman Joseph Woodland began working on a system to automatically read product information during checkout. Woodland's first design used patterns inspired by Morse code, extended into thin and thick lines drawn in the sand on a Miami beach. Their 1952 patent described a "bull's eye" circular pattern, but the technology to reliably read it did not exist yet.

The first real-world barcode application came from an unexpected place. In the late 1960s, the Association of American Railroads adopted a system called KarTrak that used colored stripes on railroad cars to track rolling stock. Each car carried a reflective label with blue and red stripes encoding a six-digit company identifier and a four-digit car number. The system worked in controlled conditions but failed when labels got dirty, and the project was abandoned by 1978.

The breakthrough came on June 26, 1974, when a pack of Wrigley's Juicy Fruit chewing gum became the first product scanned with a UPC barcode at a Marsh supermarket in Troy, Ohio. The Universal Product Code had been adopted by the grocery industry in 1973, and it changed retail permanently. By the early 1980s, barcode scanners were standard equipment in American supermarkets. Today, over 6 billion barcodes are scanned globally every single day.

How Barcodes Encode Data

Every barcode, whether it is a simple UPC label or a complex QR code, operates on the same fundamental principle: encoding data as a pattern of contrasting elements that a machine can read optically. Understanding this encoding is essential if you plan to generate barcodes programmatically.

1D Barcode Encoding

One-dimensional (linear) barcodes store data in the widths of parallel bars and spaces. A scanner reads the barcode by measuring the reflectance of light across a single horizontal line. Dark bars absorb light, white spaces reflect it, and the scanner converts these reflectance transitions into a binary signal.

Most 1D barcode types use a modular encoding system. The entire barcode width is divided into equal-width modules. Each character is represented by a specific pattern of dark and light modules. For example, in UPC-A, each digit is encoded as a 7-module pattern. The digit "0" on the left side is encoded as 0001101 (three spaces, two bars, one space, one bar), while the same digit on the right side uses the complementary pattern 1110010.

Start and stop characters are critical structural elements. They tell the scanner where the barcode begins and ends, and they also establish the module width for decoding the rest of the symbol. In Code 128, the start character also indicates which of three character sets (A, B, or C) is being used initially.

Check digits provide error detection. The final digit of a UPC or EAN barcode is calculated from the preceding digits using a modulo-10 algorithm. If the scanned data does not produce the correct check digit, the scanner rejects the read. This catches single-digit errors and most transposition errors.

2D Barcode Encoding

Two-dimensional barcodes store data in both horizontal and vertical dimensions, dramatically increasing capacity. They come in two subcategories. Stacked barcodes like PDF417 are essentially multiple 1D barcodes stacked on top of each other. Matrix barcodes like QR codes use a grid of square modules where each module represents a single bit.

Matrix barcodes include finder patterns, alignment patterns, and timing patterns that allow the scanner to determine the symbol's position, size, orientation, and the boundaries of the data grid. QR codes, for instance, have three large finder patterns in three corners, plus smaller alignment patterns distributed through the symbol for larger versions.

Error correction is what separates 2D barcodes from their 1D predecessors. Using Reed-Solomon error correction codes, formats like QR and Data Matrix can reconstruct missing or damaged data. A QR code at the highest error correction level (H) can lose up to 30% of its modules and still be decoded correctly. This is why QR codes remain scannable even with logos placed over parts of the pattern.

1D Barcode Types · The Linear Standards

UPC-A (Universal Product Code)

UPC-A is the 12-digit barcode found on virtually every retail product sold in the United States and Canada. The first digit is a number system digit (0 for most standard products, 2 for variable-weight items, 3 for pharmaceuticals). The next five digits identify the manufacturer, the following five identify the specific product, and the final digit is a check digit.

Each UPC-A barcode is exactly 95 modules wide: 3 modules for the start guard, 42 modules for six left-side digits, 5 modules for the center guard, 42 modules for six right-side digits, and 3 modules for the end guard. This fixed structure means every UPC-A barcode has exactly the same width-to-height ratio when printed at the same module size.

UPC-E

UPC-E is a compressed version of UPC-A designed for small packages where a full-size barcode will not fit. It uses a zero-suppression technique to compress a 12-digit UPC-A number into 6 displayed digits (8 digits total including the number system and check digit). Only UPC-A codes with specific patterns of zeros in the manufacturer or product code can be compressed to UPC-E.

EAN-13 and EAN-8

The European Article Number system extended UPC to work internationally. EAN-13 encodes 13 digits: a 2 or 3-digit country code, a manufacturer code, a product code, and a check digit. Every valid UPC-A code is also a valid EAN-13 code with a leading zero. EAN-8 is the compact version, using just 8 digits for small products. It requires a separate application to GS1 since the compressed format leaves no room for a standard manufacturer prefix.

Code 39

Code 39 (also called Code 3 of 9) was the first alphanumeric barcode symbology. Each character is encoded as 9 elements -- 5 bars and 4 spaces -- with exactly 3 of those 9 elements being wide. This self-checking property means Code 39 does not technically require a check digit, though one is often added for critical applications.

Code 39 supports uppercase letters A through Z, digits 0 through 9, and seven special characters (- . $ / + % and space). Its character set is limited compared to Code 128, and it produces wider barcodes for the same data, but it remains widely used in government and defense applications (the U.S. Department of Defense adopted it as the standard for LOGMARS).

Code 128

Code 128 is the workhorse of modern 1D barcodes. It encodes all 128 ASCII characters using three character sets. Set A covers control characters and uppercase letters. Set B covers uppercase and lowercase letters plus symbols. Set C encodes pairs of digits, making it the most efficient way to encode long numeric strings. A single barcode can switch between character sets mid-stream using special code characters.

Each Code 128 character is represented by 11 modules arranged as 3 bars and 3 spaces. The start character (one of three, indicating the initial character set), the data characters, a modulo-103 check character, and a fixed stop pattern of 13 modules make up the complete symbol. Code 128 produces significantly more compact barcodes than Code 39 for the same data.

ITF-14 (Interleaved 2 of 5)

ITF-14 is designed specifically for marking shipping cartons and outer packaging. It encodes exactly 14 digits (a GTIN-14) using interleaved 2-of-5 encoding, where each character uses 5 elements -- 2 wide and 3 narrow. Pairs of characters are interleaved, with the first character encoded in bars and the second in spaces. The "14" in ITF-14 refers to the 14-digit data content. ITF-14 is deliberately printed with a thick bearer bar frame around the symbol, which prevents partial scans from producing incorrect reads when the scanner beam hits the top or bottom edge of the barcode.

2D Barcode Types · Beyond the Line

QR Code (Quick Response)

Denso Wave, a subsidiary of Toyota, created the QR code in 1994 to track automotive parts during manufacturing. The format was released as an open standard, which is the primary reason for its massive adoption. Today, QR codes appear on everything from restaurant menus to cryptocurrency wallets.

A QR code consists of several structural elements. Three finder patterns (the large squares in three corners) allow a scanner to locate and orient the symbol from any angle. Timing patterns (alternating dark and light modules) run between the finder patterns to establish the grid coordinates. Format information (encoded adjacent to the finder patterns) specifies the error correction level and mask pattern. Version information (for versions 7 and above) is encoded in two blocks near the finder patterns.

QR codes come in 40 versions, from Version 1 (21 x 21 modules) to Version 40 (177 x 177 modules). Each version increase adds 4 modules to each side. The data capacity depends on both the version and the error correction level chosen.

QR Code · Data Modes

QR codes support four primary data encoding modes, and the mode determines how efficiently data is packed into the available modules.

Numeric mode encodes digits 0 through 9. Three digits are packed into 10 bits, giving the highest density. A Version 1 QR code with low error correction can hold 41 numeric characters.

Alphanumeric mode encodes digits, uppercase letters, and nine special characters (space, $, %, *, +, -, ., /, :). Two characters are packed into 11 bits. The same Version 1 symbol holds 25 alphanumeric characters.

Byte mode encodes raw 8-bit data, typically ISO 8859-1 or UTF-8. Each character takes 8 bits, and Version 1 holds 17 bytes.

Kanji mode encodes double-byte Shift JIS characters. Each character takes 13 bits, and Version 1 holds 10 Kanji characters.

A single QR code can mix modes within the same symbol, switching between them using mode indicators embedded in the data stream. This means a QR code containing a URL can use alphanumeric mode for the "HTTPS://" prefix and byte mode for the rest of the path.

QR Code · Error Correction Levels

| Level | Recovery Capacity | Typical Use Case | Data Overhead |

| --- | --- | --- | --- |

| L (Low) | ~7% of codewords | Clean environments, maximum data capacity | Lowest |

| M (Medium) | ~15% of codewords | General purpose, default for most generators | Moderate |

| Q (Quartile) | ~25% of codewords | Industrial environments, some damage expected | High |

| H (High) | ~30% of codewords | Logo embedding, harsh environments, outdoor signage | Highest |

Choosing the right error correction level is a tradeoff. Higher error correction means more of the QR code is dedicated to redundancy, which either reduces data capacity at the same version or requires bumping to a higher version (larger physical size) for the same data. If you are generating QR codes for digital screens where the image will be pristine, Level L gives you the most compact symbol. If you are printing QR codes on warehouse labels that may get scuffed, Level Q or H is worth the size increase.

Data Matrix

Data Matrix is a 2D matrix barcode that excels in industrial marking applications. It can be printed as small as 2mm x 2mm while remaining scannable, making it the standard for marking electronic components, surgical instruments, and aerospace parts. The U.S. Electronic Industries Alliance adopted Data Matrix (specifically the ECC 200 variant) for component marking, and NASA uses it to track parts on the International Space Station.

Data Matrix ECC 200 uses Reed-Solomon error correction and can recover from up to 30% data loss, similar to QR codes at Level H. The symbol sizes range from 10 x 10 modules to 144 x 144 modules, with rectangular variants available (such as 8 x 18 or 12 x 36). Each symbol has an "L-shaped" finder pattern along two sides and an alternating clock pattern along the other two sides.

For developers, Data Matrix has a practical advantage over QR codes when encoding short data strings. A Data Matrix symbol encoding 10 alphanumeric characters is physically smaller than the equivalent QR code because Data Matrix does not need the three large finder patterns that QR codes require.

PDF417

PDF417 is a stacked linear barcode, meaning it is structured as multiple rows of linear barcodes arranged vertically. The "417" refers to the encoding pattern: each codeword is 17 modules long, composed of 4 bars and 4 spaces. PDF417 can encode up to 1,850 alphanumeric characters or 2,710 numeric digits, making it one of the highest-capacity barcodes in common use.

The format is widely used for identification documents. U.S. driver's licenses and state IDs encode all the cardholder's data in a PDF417 barcode on the back. Airline boarding passes use PDF417 to encode flight information, passenger data, and security codes. The format supports both text and binary data, and it has three error correction levels (0 through 8) that allow recovery from varying degrees of damage.

PDF417 has a significant advantage for document applications: it does not require a 2D image sensor to scan. Because it is structured as stacked rows, a standard linear laser scanner can read it by sweeping across all rows, though this is slower than reading with a 2D imager.

Aztec Code

Aztec Code was developed by Andrew Longacre at Welch Allyn in 1995. It is built around a central finder pattern (a bull's eye of concentric squares) rather than the corner-based finder patterns used by QR codes. This design means Aztec codes do not require a quiet zone (blank border) around the symbol, which is a real advantage when label space is tight.

Aztec codes range from Compact (15 x 15 modules, up to 53 characters) to Full Range (151 x 151 modules, up to 3,832 characters). Error correction is configurable from 5% to 95% of symbol capacity, giving developers fine-grained control over the size-versus-reliability tradeoff.

The format's most visible deployment is in European rail ticketing. Deutsche Bahn, SNCF, and many other European rail operators use Aztec codes for mobile tickets. The International Air Transport Association (IATA) also selected Aztec Code as an alternative to PDF417 for boarding pass barcodes (Bar Coded Boarding Pass standard, Resolution 792).

GS1 Standards and Product Identification

GS1 is the global organization that manages barcode standards for commerce. If you are generating barcodes for retail products, logistics, or healthcare, you will encounter GS1 standards constantly. Understanding the GS1 system is non-negotiable for any developer building supply chain or retail software.

The Global Trade Item Number (GTIN) is the foundation. GTIN-12 is the number encoded in a UPC-A barcode. GTIN-13 is the number encoded in an EAN-13. GTIN-14 is used for shipping containers and is encoded in ITF-14. All of these are part of the same numbering system, and a GTIN-12 can always be expressed as a GTIN-13 by adding a leading zero.

GS1 Application Identifiers (AIs) extend the basic product number with additional data fields. AI (01) is the GTIN itself. AI (10) is the batch or lot number. AI (17) is the expiration date. AI (21) is the serial number. These AIs can be concatenated into a single barcode, typically encoded as a GS1-128 barcode (which uses Code 128 encoding with special GS1 formatting rules) or a GS1 DataMatrix symbol.

"GS1 standards are used by more than 2 million companies across 25 industries in over 100 countries. The GS1 system provides the foundation for traceability, product authentication, and supply chain visibility worldwide."

Source: GS1 Annual Report 2024

For healthcare applications, the GS1 DataMatrix is mandatory in many jurisdictions. The EU Falsified Medicines Directive requires a GS1 DataMatrix encoding the GTIN, serial number, batch number, and expiration date on every prescription drug package. Similar regulations exist in the United States under the Drug Supply Chain Security Act (DSCSA).

Generating Barcodes Programmatically

The JavaScript system has mature, well-maintained libraries for building a barcode generator into your application, covering every format discussed in this article.

JsBarcode for 1D Formats

JsBarcode is the standard library for generating 1D barcodes in JavaScript. It supports UPC-A, UPC-E, EAN-13, EAN-8, Code 39, Code 128, ITF-14, and several other formats. It renders to SVG, Canvas, or IMG elements.

// Generate a Code 128 barcode
import JsBarcode from 'jsbarcode';

// Render to an SVG element
JsBarcode("#barcode-svg", "SHIP-2024-00847", {
  format: "CODE128",
  width: 2,
  height: 100,
  displayValue: true,
  fontSize: 14,
  font: "monospace",
  textMargin: 8,
  background: "#ffffff",
  lineColor: "#000000"
});

// Generate an EAN-13 barcode for a retail product
JsBarcode("#product-barcode", "5901234123457", {
  format: "EAN13",
  flat: true,
  width: 2,
  height: 80
});

JsBarcode automatically calculates check digits for formats that require them. If you pass a 12-digit string with the EAN13 format, it will compute and append the 13th check digit. If you pass all 13 digits, it validates the check digit and throws an error if it is incorrect. This behavior prevents a common source of bugs in barcode generation.

qrcode.js and node-qrcode for 2D Formats

For QR code generation, the qrcode npm package (also known as node-qrcode) handles both browser and Node.js environments. It supports all four error correction levels, automatic version selection, and multiple output formats including SVG, Canvas, PNG buffer, and data URL.

import QRCode from 'qrcode';

// Generate a QR code as a data URL for embedding in HTML
const dataUrl = await QRCode.toDataURL('https://zovo.one', {
  errorCorrectionLevel: 'M',
  type: 'image/png',
  width: 300,
  margin: 2,
  color: {
    dark: '#000000',
    light: '#ffffff'
  }
});

// Generate as SVG string (ideal for crisp rendering at any size)
const svgString = await QRCode.toString('https://zovo.one', {
  type: 'svg',
  errorCorrectionLevel: 'H',
  width: 256
});

// Node.js: save directly to file
await QRCode.toFile('./output/ticket-qr.png', 'TICKET-EVT-2024-7731', {
  errorCorrectionLevel: 'Q',
  width: 400
});

For Data Matrix generation in JavaScript, the bwip-js (Barcode Writer in Pure JavaScript) library is the most complete option. It supports over 100 barcode types including Data Matrix, PDF417, Aztec, MaxiCode, and all the 1D formats. The tradeoff is a larger bundle size compared to the specialized libraries.

import bwipjs from 'bwip-js';

// Generate a Data Matrix as a PNG buffer
const pngBuffer = await bwipjs.toBuffer({
  bcid: 'datamatrix',
  text: '01034531200000111719112510ABCD1234',
  scale: 3,
  padding: 5
});

// Generate a PDF417 for an ID document
const pdf417Buffer = await bwipjs.toBuffer({
  bcid: 'pdf417',
  text: 'DL00410477DLDAAJOHNSON,MICHAEL,ANTHONY',
  scale: 2,
  columns: 4,
  rows: 12
});

Real-World Applications by Industry

Retail and E-Commerce

Retail is where barcodes began and where they remain most visible. UPC-A and EAN-13 handle point-of-sale scanning. GS1-128 (Code 128 with GS1 formatting) is used on shipping cartons with Application Identifiers encoding ship-to postal codes, carton weight, and purchase order numbers. Many retailers now also require ASN (Advanced Shipping Notice) integration, where the barcode data on each carton matches the electronic data sent ahead via EDI.

E-commerce fulfillment centers rely heavily on Code 128 for internal bin location labels, pick tickets, and packing slips. Amazon's proprietary FNSKU (Fulfillment Network Stock Keeping Unit) is encoded as a Code 128 barcode, and every product sent to an Amazon warehouse must carry one.

Healthcare and Pharmaceuticals

Barcode scanning in healthcare prevents medication errors. The FDA mandates that most prescription drugs carry a linear barcode encoding the National Drug Code (NDC). Hospitals implement Barcode Medication Administration (BCMA) systems where nurses scan both the patient's wristband and the medication package before administering any drug. Studies have shown that BCMA systems reduce medication errors by 41% to 65%.

The shift to 2D barcodes is accelerating in healthcare. GS1 DataMatrix is becoming required on individual drug packages in the EU, US, and many other markets. The 2D format encodes not just the product identifier but also the serial number, lot number, and expiration date, enabling full unit-level traceability through the supply chain.

Manufacturing and Logistics

Automotive manufacturers use Data Matrix codes for direct part marking (DPM), where the barcode is permanently etched, laser-marked, or dot-peened directly onto metal or plastic parts. These marks must survive the entire lifecycle of the part, including painting, heat treatment, and years of use. Data Matrix is preferred for DPM because of its compact size and high error correction capability.

Logistics companies use a mix of barcode types. UPS, FedEx, and DHL use proprietary barcode formats derived from Code 128 and MaxiCode for package tracking. The MaxiCode symbol (a hexagonal grid around a central bull's eye) is required on all UPS shipping labels and encodes the delivery postal code, country code, and service class in a fixed 1-inch-square symbol.

Transportation and Ticketing

Airline boarding passes use either PDF417 or Aztec codes following IATA standards. The barcode encodes the passenger name, flight number, date, origin and destination airports, seat assignment, and a security check string. Mobile boarding passes typically use Aztec codes because they scan reliably on phone screens at varying brightness levels and do not require the quiet zone that other formats need.

Public transit systems increasingly use QR codes for mobile ticketing. The London Underground, Tokyo Metro, and hundreds of other transit agencies accept QR-code tickets on smartphone screens. The QR code typically encodes a signed token that the gate reader validates against a backend system, rather than containing the ticket data itself.

Document Management

Many document processing workflows embed barcodes to automate routing and indexing. Insurance claim forms, tax documents, and legal filings often include a Code 39 or Code 128 barcode encoding a document identifier. When the document is scanned, the barcode is read automatically and the document is filed in the correct digital folder without manual data entry.

Choosing the Right Barcode Type for Your Project

After building 20+ systems that generate barcodes, I have developed a simple decision framework that works for almost every use case.

If you are encoding a product for retail sale, the answer is UPC-A (in North America) or EAN-13 (everywhere else). You need a GS1 company prefix, and there is no alternative.

If you are encoding short alphanumeric strings for internal tracking (warehouse bins, asset tags, work orders), Code 128 is the default choice. It is compact, universally supported by scanners, and encodes the full ASCII character set.

If you need to encode URLs, contact information, or any data that will be scanned by smartphone cameras, QR codes are the only practical choice. Their adoption is so widespread that every modern smartphone camera app includes a QR reader.

If you are marking small parts or need the most compact 2D symbol for short data strings, Data Matrix is the right call. It outperforms QR codes on physical size when the data payload is under about 100 characters.

If you are building a ticketing or identity document system, PDF417 gives you the highest data capacity of any commonly-used format, and its stacked structure means it can be printed as a narrow, wide rectangle that fits well on card-sized documents.

If you need a barcode that works without a quiet zone, or you are integrating with European rail ticketing systems, Aztec Code is the answer.

Technical Implementation Tips

A few practical notes from building barcode generators and scanners in production.

Print resolution matters enormously for 1D barcodes. The minimum bar width (X dimension) must be at least 0.264mm (10.4 mils) for UPC and EAN barcodes at 100% magnification. Printing below this size causes scan failures. For thermal printers commonly used in warehouses, I recommend an X dimension of at least 0.33mm to account for dot gain (ink spread).

Contrast ratio is as important as resolution. The minimum reflectance difference between bars and spaces should be at least 37.5% per ANSI/ISO standards. Dark blue or dark green bars on a white background work. Red bars on white do not, because most barcode scanners use red laser diodes and red bars are invisible to them.

For QR codes on screens, avoid low brightness and high compression. JPEG artifacts can distort module edges enough to cause read failures. Use PNG or SVG for QR codes whenever possible. If you must use JPEG, set the quality to 95% or higher.

When generating barcodes in server-side applications, prefer SVG output over raster formats. SVG barcodes render crisply at any size and any DPI, which eliminates an entire class of bugs related to scaling and resolution. Convert to raster (PNG at 300 DPI or higher) only at the final output stage when sending to a printer.

Quiet zones are not optional for most formats. UPC-A requires a minimum quiet zone of 9 modules on the left and 9 modules on the right. QR codes require a 4-module quiet zone on all four sides. Cropping into the quiet zone is one of the most common causes of scan failures in production systems. The only mainstream format that does not require a quiet zone is Aztec Code.

The Future of Barcode Technology

GS1 has initiated a global migration called "Sunrise 2027" that will transition retail point-of-sale scanning from 1D barcodes to 2D formats, primarily QR codes encoded with GS1 Digital Link syntax. Instead of encoding just a GTIN, these new QR codes will encode a URL like https://id.gs1.org/01/05412345000013/10/ABC123 that serves as both a scannable product identifier and a web link to product information.

This transition does not mean 1D barcodes are dying. They will remain in use for decades in warehousing, manufacturing, and anywhere that high-speed linear scanning is needed. But the point-of-sale use case -- which is how most people encounter barcodes -- will gradually shift to 2D formats that can carry richer data.

For developers, this means that building a barcode generator today should include both 1D and 2D capabilities. The applications scanning these codes are increasingly expecting GS1 Digital Link URIs inside QR symbols, and your generation tools need to produce symbols that comply with the evolving standards.

The fundamentals stay the same regardless of your use case · printing UPC labels for a retail product, generating QR codes for a mobile app, or etching Data Matrix symbols onto aerospace components. Choose the right format for your data type and environment. Set appropriate error correction for your scanning conditions. Respect quiet zones and minimum print sizes. And test with actual scanners in actual conditions, because the scanner on your desk and the scanner at the end of a warehouse aisle behave very differently.
