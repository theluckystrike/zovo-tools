The Texas Paycheck Reality

Texas is one of nine states with no personal income tax. That single fact draws thousands of relocations every year from high-tax states like California and New York. But the assumption that "no state tax equals a bigger paycheck" glosses over everything else that gets pulled from your gross pay before it reaches your bank account.

I have spent years building paycheck estimator tools for Texas workers, and the most common mistake I see is treating no state income tax as the whole story. Federal income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and voluntary pre-tax deductions still carve significant amounts out of every payroll check. A $75,000 salary in Texas does not produce $75,000 of spending money. It produces roughly $59,000 to $61,000, depending on your filing status and elections.

This guide walks through every deduction that appears on a Texas pay stub, provides the 2024 and 2025 federal tax brackets, includes a full paycheck breakdown for a $75,000 salary, and compares Texas take-home pay against the two highest-tax states in the country. What Texas Does and Does Not Tax

Texas imposes no state income tax on wages, salaries, or investment income for individuals. Article VIII, Section 1-e of the Texas Constitution prohibits a personal income tax unless voters approve it through a statewide referendum, and no such referendum has ever passed. That constitutional protection is what separates Texas from states that simply have not enacted an income tax yet through legislation.

However, Texas still collects revenue. The state relies heavily on sales tax (6.25% state rate, up to 8.25% with local additions), property taxes that rank among the highest in the nation, and franchise taxes on businesses. So while your paycheck itself avoids a state income tax line item, you still contribute to state revenue through spending and property ownership.

What does show up on a Texas pay stub is entirely federal. Here is the short list of mandatory deductions that appear on every W-2 employee paycheck in the state.

The employee-side deductions are what reduce your take-home pay. FUTA and SUTA are employer costs that do not appear on your pay stub at all.

Federal Income Tax Brackets · 2024 and 2025

Federal income tax uses a progressive marginal system. You do not pay 22% on your entire income just because your salary falls in the 22% bracket. You pay 10% on the first portion, 12% on the next, and 22% only on the amount that exceeds the lower bracket thresholds.

2024 Brackets · Single Filers

For 2025, the IRS adjusted the brackets upward for inflation. The single filer 10% bracket rises to $11,925, the 12% bracket ceiling moves to $48,475, and the 22% bracket extends to $103,350. The rates themselves remain unchanged. These adjustments prevent "bracket creep," where inflation pushes workers into higher brackets without any real increase in purchasing power.

The standard deduction for 2024 is $14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married couples filing jointly. For 2025, those figures rise to $15,000 and $30,000, respectively. The standard deduction reduces your gross income before tax brackets apply, which is why taxable income is always lower than your salary.

Source: IRS Revenue Procedure 2023-34 (2024 figures) and Revenue Procedure 2024-40 (2025 figures)

FICA Explained · Social Security and Medicare

FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act, and it funds two programs: Social Security (officially Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance, or OASDI) and Medicare. These are not optional. Every W-2 employee in every state, including Texas, pays FICA on every paycheck.

Social Security Tax

The employee rate is 6.2% of gross wages, and your employer pays a matching 6.2%. For 2024, this applies to the first $168,600 of wage income. Once your year-to-date earnings exceed that cap, Social Security withholding stops for the rest of the calendar year.

For someone earning $168,600 or more, the maximum employee Social Security tax in 2024 is $10,453.20. If you earn $75,000, you pay the full 6.2% because you never reach the cap.

For 2025, the Social Security wage base increases to $176,100, raising the maximum employee contribution to $10,918.20.

Medicare Tax

The base Medicare rate is 1.45% of all wages with no income cap. Unlike Social Security, Medicare applies to every dollar you earn regardless of how high your salary goes.

The Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% applies to wages exceeding $200,000 for single filers (or $250,000 for married filing jointly). This surtax is only paid by the employee. Your employer does not match it. For a single filer earning $250,000, the calculation would be 1.45% on the full $250,000 plus 0.9% on the $50,000 above the $200,000 threshold.

Self-Employment and FICA

If you are self-employed in Texas, you pay both the employee and employer portions of FICA, which is called the self-employment tax. That totals 15.3% on net self-employment earnings (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare). The IRS allows you to deduct the employer-equivalent half (7.65%) as an adjustment to income on your tax return, which slightly reduces your income tax. More on this in the self-employment section below.

The Standard Deduction and Its Impact on Your Paycheck

The standard deduction is the single largest factor that separates gross income from taxable income for most workers. In 2024, the standard deduction is $14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married couples filing jointly. In 2025, those rise to $15,000 and $30,000.

Many people search for a texas pay stub calculator and enter their gross salary expecting to see federal tax calculated on that full amount. But your employer's payroll system accounts for the standard deduction when calculating withholding (through the W-4 form), so your actual per-paycheck withholding is lower than the raw bracket math on gross salary would suggest.

For context, the $14,600 standard deduction effectively shields that amount from all federal income tax. At the lowest bracket, that saves a single filer at least $1,460 in federal taxes (10% of $14,600). For higher earners whose $14,600 would otherwise be taxed at 22% or 24%, the savings are even larger.

If you itemize deductions instead (mortgage interest, state and local taxes up to $10,000, charitable donations), you forego the standard deduction in favor of your itemized total. In Texas, the lack of state income tax means you cannot deduct state income taxes on Schedule A, which makes itemizing less attractive for Texas residents compared to those in high-tax states. Most Texas filers take the standard deduction.

Pre-Tax Deductions That Reduce Your Tax Bill

Beyond the mandatory deductions, many employers offer benefits that come out of your paycheck before taxes are calculated. These pre-tax deductions lower your taxable income, which reduces both your federal income tax and, in some cases, your FICA taxes.

401(k) and 403(b) Contributions

Traditional 401(k) contributions are deducted from your gross pay before federal income tax is calculated. For 2024, the employee contribution limit is $23,000 ($30,500 if you are 50 or older). If you contribute $10,000 per year to a traditional 401(k) on a $75,000 salary, your taxable income drops from $60,400 (after the standard deduction) to $50,400, saving you $2,200 in federal income tax at the 22% bracket.

Note that traditional 401(k) contributions still incur FICA taxes. Social Security and Medicare are calculated on gross wages before 401(k) deferrals. Roth 401(k) contributions, by contrast, are made with after-tax dollars and do not reduce your current taxable income.

Health Insurance Premiums

Employer-sponsored health insurance premiums paid through a Section 125 cafeteria plan are pre-tax. They reduce both your federal income tax and your FICA taxes. If you pay $300 per month ($3,600 annually) for health insurance through your employer's plan, your taxable income for federal purposes drops by $3,600, and your Social Security and Medicare wages drop by the same amount.

For the $75,000 earner, that $3,600 health insurance deduction saves approximately $792 in federal income tax (at the 22% bracket) and $275 in FICA taxes, for a total tax savings of about $1,067 per year.

Health Savings Account (HSA) Contributions

If you have a high-deductible health plan, HSA contributions are pre-tax and also avoid FICA when made through payroll deduction. For 2024, the HSA contribution limit is $4,150 for self-only coverage and $8,300 for family coverage. HSA funds grow tax-free and can be withdrawn tax-free for qualified medical expenses, making them one of the most tax-efficient savings vehicles available.

Flexible Spending Accounts (FSA)

Dependent care FSAs (up to $5,000 per household) and healthcare FSAs (up to $3,200 for 2024) are both pre-tax through payroll, reducing both income tax and FICA. The trade-off is the "use it or lose it" provision. Any unspent FSA balance at the end of the plan year is forfeited, though employers may offer a $640 rollover or 2.5-month grace period.

Understanding Your W-4 Form and Its Effect on Withholding

The W-4 form you fill out when starting a job (or update at any time) tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. Since Texas has no state income tax form, the W-4 is the only withholding document that affects your paycheck calculations.

The current W-4 (redesigned in 2020) no longer uses "allowances." Instead, it asks for specific dollar adjustments.

Key Fields That Affect Your Withholding

Step 1 is your filing status, which determines which set of tax brackets your employer uses. Single, Married Filing Jointly, and Head of Household each produce different withholding amounts on the same salary.

Step 2 applies if you hold multiple jobs or your spouse also works. Checking the box in Step 2(c) increases withholding to account for the combined income pushing you into higher brackets.

Step 3 lets you claim dependents. Each qualifying child under 17 reduces your annual withholding by $2,000 (spread across your paychecks), and other dependents reduce it by $500 each. This reflects the Child Tax Credit and Credit for Other Dependents.

Step 4(a) covers other income not from jobs, such as interest, dividends, or retirement income, that you want accounted for in withholding so you do not owe at tax time.

Step 4(b) lets you enter deductions beyond the standard deduction. If you plan to itemize and your deductions exceed the standard deduction, entering the difference here reduces withholding.

Step 4(c) lets you request additional withholding per pay period. This is useful if you consistently owe taxes or have income sources not subject to withholding.

Common W-4 Mistakes in Texas

Because Texas has no state tax, some workers assume they can skip W-4 optimization. That is a mistake. The W-4 controls your federal withholding, and getting it wrong means either a large tax refund (meaning you gave the government an interest-free loan all year) or a surprise tax bill in April.

The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov is the best tool for calibrating your W-4. I recommend running it at least once a year and after any major life change (marriage, new child, job change, or significant raise).

Self-Employment Tax in Texas

Freelancers, independent contractors, and sole proprietors in Texas face a different payroll tax guide situation. Instead of splitting FICA with an employer, self-employed workers pay both halves through the self-employment tax.

How Self-Employment Tax Is Calculated

The self-employment tax rate is 15.3%, broken into 12.4% for Social Security and 2.9% for Medicare. However, the IRS first reduces your net self-employment income by 7.65% (the employer-equivalent portion) before applying the tax. This means the effective self-employment tax rate on your net income is about 14.13%.

Self-employment tax on $75,000 net income:
$75,000 x 92.35% → $69,262.50 (SE tax base)
→ Social Security: $69,262.50 x 12.4% → $8,588.55
→ Medicare: $69,262.50 x 2.9% → $2,008.61
→ Total SE tax → $10,597.16

Compare that to the W-2 employee's FICA total of $5,737.50. The self-employed worker pays $4,859.66 more because they cover both the employee and employer shares. This is the most significant extra cost of self-employment, and it hits Texas freelancers the same as those in any other state.

Deducting Half of Self-Employment Tax

The silver lining is that the IRS allows you to deduct the employer-equivalent portion of self-employment tax (half of the total, or $5,298.58 in this example) as an adjustment to gross income on your personal tax return. This deduction lowers your adjusted gross income, which reduces your federal income tax. It does not reduce the self-employment tax itself, but it does soften the overall blow.

Quarterly Estimated Payments

Self-employed workers in Texas must make quarterly estimated tax payments (federal income tax plus self-employment tax) using Form 1040-ES. The due dates are April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15 of the following year. Missing these deadlines triggers an underpayment penalty, even if you pay the full balance when filing your return.

A common approach is to calculate your expected annual liability, divide by four, and set up automatic payments through IRS Direct Pay or EFTPS. If your income is uneven throughout the year, the annualized installment method on Form 2210 can reduce or eliminate penalties for uneven payments.

Employer-Side Payroll Taxes in Texas

While this guide focuses on the employee perspective, understanding what your employer pays provides useful context. Employers in Texas pay the following on top of your salary.

The employer's share of Social Security is 6.2% of your wages up to the $168,600 wage base (2024), matching your contribution exactly. The employer's share of Medicare is 1.45% on all wages, also matching yours. Employers do not pay the 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax.

Federal Unemployment Tax (FUTA) is 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee's annual wages, but most employers receive a 5.4% credit for paying state unemployment taxes on time, reducing the effective FUTA rate to 0.6%. That is a maximum of $42 per employee per year.

Texas Unemployment Insurance (SUTA) rates for employers vary based on industry and the employer's claims history. New employers in Texas pay a default rate that the Texas Workforce Commission assigns based on industry average. Experienced employers can see rates as low as 0.25% or as high as 6.25% on the first $9,000 of each employee's annual wages.

In total, employer-side payroll tax guidees on a $75,000 salary in Texas run approximately $5,780 to $6,300, depending on the SUTA rate. That cost is invisible on your pay stub but very real in your employer's budget.

Paycheck Frequency and How It Affects Your Numbers

Texas law does not mandate a specific pay frequency. Employers can pay weekly, biweekly, semi-monthly, or monthly. The frequency affects the dollar amounts on each check but not the annual totals.

What Does Not Show Up on Your Texas Pay Stub

A few important taxes and costs are absent from your paycheck but still affect your overall financial picture as a Texas resident.

Texas property taxes average about 1.60% to 1.80% of assessed home value, among the highest in the country. On a $350,000 home, that is $5,600 to $6,300 annually. This cost does not come through payroll, but it is the primary mechanism Texas uses to fund schools, roads, and local government without an income tax.

Sales tax in Texas ranges from 6.25% (state) to 8.25% (with local additions). You pay this at the point of purchase, not through payroll. On $40,000 of annual taxable purchases, that could be $2,500 to $3,300 in sales tax.

Vehicle registration fees, gasoline taxes ($0.20 per gallon), and various excise taxes also contribute to the state's revenue without touching your pay stub.

When comparing total tax burden across states, it is important to look beyond payroll. A full analysis includes income tax, payroll taxes, sales tax, property tax, and any other state-specific fees. Several studies have found that Texas's total tax burden as a percentage of income is moderate rather than low, particularly for middle-income homeowners.

When using any paycheck estimator for Texas, keep these points in mind. First, calculators estimate withholding, not your actual tax liability. Your true tax bill depends on your full-year income, all deductions, and credits you claim on your annual return. Withholding is just the employer's best approximation based on your W-4 and each pay period's gross wages.

Second, bonuses and supplemental wages are typically withheld at a flat 22% federal rate (or 37% for amounts over $1 million), which may be higher or lower than your effective rate. If your paycheck estimator shows a surprisingly high tax on a bonus check, that flat rate is the reason.

Key Takeaways

Texas workers benefit from zero state income tax, but federal income tax and FICA still claim 18% to 25% of gross pay depending on salary level and filing status. For a $75,000 single filer with no pre-tax deductions, the take-home pay lands around $60,920 annually or $2,343 per biweekly check.

Pre-tax deductions like 401(k) contributions and health insurance premiums can reduce the effective tax rate significantly, pushing more of each dollar into savings and benefits rather than the federal treasury. The W-4 form is the primary control mechanism for adjusting withholding, and it deserves more attention than most workers give it.

Self-employed Texans face the steepest payroll tax costs, paying both sides of FICA for a combined 15.3% rate, though the deductibility of the employer-equivalent half provides partial relief.

Compared to high-tax states, a Texas worker earning $75,000 keeps roughly $3,800 to $5,900 more per year than an identical earner in California or New York City. That gap widens at higher income levels where state marginal rates climb further.
