C1V1=C2V2 Complete Guide

Dilution is one of the most common procedures in any laboratory setting. Whether you are preparing reagents for a biochemistry experiment, mixing cleaning solutions, or formulating pharmaceutical compounds, understanding dilution math is a foundational skill. The equation C1V1 = C2V2 is deceptively simple, but applying it correctly in every situation requires attention to units, technique, and the assumptions built into the formula itself.

This guide walks through the theory behind dilution, provides fully worked examples across multiple disciplines, covers serial dilutions, explains common mistakes, and gives you practice problems to test your understanding. If you need to run calculations quickly, try the dilution calculator or the molarity calculator for unit conversions.

What the C1V1 = C2V2 Equation Actually Means

The dilution equation states that the amount of solute before dilution equals the amount of solute after dilution. You are not creating or destroying any substance. You are simply adding more solvent (usually water) to reduce the concentration.

C1 x V1 = C2 x V2

In this equation, C1 represents the concentration of your starting solution (the stock solution). V1 represents the volume of stock solution you will take. C2 represents the concentration you want to end up with. V2 represents the total final volume of your diluted solution.

The math works because concentration multiplied by volume gives you the total amount of solute. If 500 mL of a 2 M sodium chloride solution contains 1 mole of NaCl (because 2 mol/L times 0.5 L = 1 mol), then any other combination of concentration and volume that also equals 1 mole is a valid dilution. For instance, 1000 mL at 1 M, or 2000 mL at 0.5 M, or 250 mL at 4 M (which would be a concentration, not a dilution).

The equation is mathematically identical to saying that the number of moles before equals the number of moles after. This is why it only works when you are adding solvent, not adding more solute. If you add solute, you have changed the total amount of substance and the equation no longer applies.

When C1V1 = C2V2 Works and When It Fails

The dilution equation works perfectly when the following conditions are met. The solute does not change chemically when diluted. The volumes are additive, meaning that mixing 10 mL of solution with 90 mL of water gives you exactly 100 mL. The concentration units on both sides of the equation are identical.

The equation fails or becomes inaccurate in these situations. When mixing generates or absorbs significant heat, the volumes may not be additive. Concentrated sulfuric acid mixed with water is the classic example. The exothermic reaction causes volume contraction, and 50 mL of acid plus 50 mL of water does not give 100 mL. When the solute dissociates or associates differently at different concentrations, the effective concentration changes in ways the equation does not account for. Strong electrolytes at high concentrations behave non-ideally. When you cross a solubility limit, the solute precipitates out and the equation no longer describes the system.

For the vast majority of laboratory dilutions with aqueous solutions below about 1 M, the equation is accurate enough for any practical purpose. Even for moderately concentrated solutions up to 6 M, the error introduced by volume non-additivity is usually less than 1%, which is within the tolerance of most volumetric glassware.

Step by Step Process for Any Dilution Problem

Every dilution problem follows the same logical sequence. Once you internalize these steps, you can solve any straightforward dilution in under a minute.

Step 1. Identify what you know. Write down C1, V1, C2, and V2. You will always know three of the four values, and you need to find the fourth.

Step 2. Check your units. Both concentration values must use the same units. Both volume values must use the same units. If C1 is in molarity, C2 must be in molarity. If V1 is in milliliters, V2 must be in milliliters. Convert before plugging into the equation.

Step 3. Rearrange the equation to solve for the unknown. The four rearrangements are:

To find V1 (volume of stock needed): V1 = (C2 x V2) / C1

To find V2 (total final volume): V2 = (C1 x V1) / C2

To find C1 (original concentration): C1 = (C2 x V2) / V1

To find C2 (final concentration): C2 = (C1 x V1) / V2

Step 4. Plug in the numbers and calculate.

Step 5. Sanity check. C2 should always be less than C1 in a dilution. V2 should always be greater than V1. If your answer violates either of these rules, you have made an error.

Step 6. Calculate the volume of solvent to add. This is V2 minus V1. You take V1 of stock and add enough solvent to bring the total volume to V2.

Worked Example Problems

Example 1. Preparing a Standard Buffer

You have a 10x PBS (phosphate buffered saline) stock solution and need to prepare 500 mL of 1x PBS for cell culture. How much stock do you need?

C1 = 10x (the stock concentration)

V1 = unknown (what we need to find)

C2 = 1x (the working concentration)

V2 = 500 mL (the total volume we need)

V1 = (C2 x V2) / C1 = (1 x 500) / 10 = 50 mL

Take 50 mL of 10x PBS stock. Add 450 mL of sterile water. Total volume = 500 mL of 1x PBS.

Notice that we used "x" as the concentration unit. This is perfectly valid because both sides use the same unit. The "x" notation is extremely common in biology for buffer concentrates, enzyme stocks, and loading dyes.

Example 2. Diluting a Molar Solution

You have a 6 M hydrochloric acid stock and need 250 mL of 0.5 M HCl for a titration. How much stock do you need?

C1 = 6 M

V1 = unknown

C2 = 0.5 M

V2 = 250 mL

V1 = (0.5 x 250) / 6 = 125 / 6 = 20.83 mL

Measure 20.83 mL of 6 M HCl. Add to a volumetric flask. Fill to 250 mL with deionized water.

Safety note for acid dilutions: always add acid to water, never water to acid. The heat generated when water contacts concentrated acid can cause the water to boil instantly and splash concentrated acid. Add the measured acid slowly to about 200 mL of water in the flask, swirl, let it cool, then top up to the 250 mL mark.

Example 3. Finding Final Concentration

A student pipettes 2 mL of a 0.4 mg/mL protein solution into 8 mL of buffer. What is the final protein concentration?

C1 = 0.4 mg/mL

V1 = 2 mL

C2 = unknown

V2 = 2 + 8 = 10 mL (the total volume after mixing)

C2 = (C1 x V1) / V2 = (0.4 x 2) / 10 = 0.8 / 10 = 0.08 mg/mL

This is a critical point that students often miss. V2 is the total final volume, not the volume of diluent you added. If you pipette 2 mL into 8 mL, the total is 10 mL, not 8 mL. Getting this wrong gives you 0.1 mg/mL instead of the correct 0.08 mg/mL, a 25% error.

Example 4. Working with Percent Solutions

You have 70% isopropyl alcohol and need to make 1 liter of 10% isopropyl alcohol for surface cleaning. How much stock do you need?

C1 = 70%

V1 = unknown

C2 = 10%

V2 = 1000 mL

V1 = (10 x 1000) / 70 = 142.86 mL

Measure 142.86 mL of 70% isopropyl alcohol. Add water to reach 1000 mL total volume.

Understanding Serial Dilutions

A serial dilution is a sequence of dilutions performed one after another, where each dilution step uses the output of the previous step as its input. Serial dilutions are essential in microbiology, immunology, pharmacology, and any field where you need to cover a wide concentration range.

The fundamental problem that serial dilutions solve is accuracy. Suppose you need to go from 1 M to 0.001 M. A single dilution step would require measuring 1 mL of stock and adding 999 mL of solvent. But if you need to go from 1 M to 0.000001 M (1 micromolar), a single step would require measuring 1 microliter into 1 liter. Measuring 1 microliter with any accuracy is extremely difficult, and the pipetting error alone could be 20% or more.

Instead, you perform six consecutive 1:10 dilutions. Each step takes 1 mL and adds 9 mL of diluent. After six steps, you have gone from 1 M to 0.000001 M, and each individual step is easy to perform accurately.

Calculating Serial Dilution Concentrations

The final concentration after n serial dilution steps is:

C_final = C_initial x (dilution factor)^n

For a 1:10 serial dilution starting at 1 M:

StepDilution FactorConcentration
Stock--1 M
11/100.1 M (100 mM)
21/1000.01 M (10 mM)
31/1,0000.001 M (1 mM)
41/10,0000.0001 M (100 uM)
51/100,0000.00001 M (10 uM)
61/1,000,0000.000001 M (1 uM)

Serial dilutions are not limited to 1:10. Common serial dilution ratios include 1:2 (twofold), 1:3 (threefold), 1:5 (fivefold), and 1:10 (tenfold). The choice depends on how many data points you need across your concentration range and how much solution you need at each step.

Two-Fold Serial Dilution Example

You are running an antibiotic susceptibility test and need seven concentrations of ampicillin in twofold dilutions starting at 256 micrograms per mL.

Prepare 2 mL of 256 ug/mL ampicillin from your stock.

Label 6 tubes, each containing 1 mL of sterile broth.

Transfer 1 mL from the 256 ug/mL tube into tube 1 (1 mL sample + 1 mL broth = 128 ug/mL).

Mix tube 1 thoroughly. Transfer 1 mL from tube 1 into tube 2 (gives 64 ug/mL).

Continue through all tubes.

Final concentrations: 256, 128, 64, 32, 16, 8, 4 ug/mL.

The volume in each tube after transfer is 1 mL (you took out 1 mL from the 2 mL, leaving 1 mL). Each tube ends with 1 mL at the correct concentration. This is a standard minimum inhibitory concentration (MIC) assay format.

Dilution Factor vs Dilution Ratio

These terms cause confusion because different fields use them differently.

In most scientific contexts, a 1:10 dilution means 1 part sample diluted to a final volume of 10 parts. You take 1 mL of sample and add 9 mL of diluent, giving 10 mL total. The dilution factor is 10. The concentration is divided by 10.

In some clinical and food science contexts, a 1:10 dilution means 1 part sample plus 10 parts diluent, giving 11 parts total. The dilution factor is 11. The concentration is divided by 11.

The difference is whether the second number represents the total volume or the amount of diluent added. When the notation is ambiguous, ask or check the protocol. A 10% error from misinterpreting the convention can invalidate experimental results.

To avoid ambiguity, some protocols use the notation "1 in 10" (meaning 1 part in a total of 10) versus "1 to 10" (meaning 1 part to 10 parts of diluent). When writing your own protocols, be explicit. Write "add 1 mL of sample to 9 mL of diluent (1:10 final dilution)" so there is zero room for misinterpretation.

Molarity vs Mass Concentration and When to Use Each

Molarity (moles per liter, M) and mass concentration (grams per liter, g/L, or milligrams per milliliter, mg/mL) are both valid ways to express concentration, but they serve different purposes.

Use molarity when you need to know the number of molecules present, which matters for chemical reactions, enzyme kinetics, binding assays, and anything where stoichiometry matters. If you are mixing two reagents that react in a 1:1 molar ratio, you need to know molarity to mix them correctly.

Use mass concentration when you care about the weight of substance present, which matters for pharmacology (drug doses in mg/kg), nutrition, and many clinical applications. If a patient needs 500 mg of a drug, you calculate with mass concentration.

To convert between them, you need the molecular weight (MW) of the solute:

Molarity = mass concentration (g/L) / molecular weight (g/mol)

For example, glucose has a MW of 180.16 g/mol. A 10 g/L glucose solution has a molarity of 10 / 180.16 = 0.0555 M, or about 55.5 mM.

Common Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Mistake 1. Confusing V2 with the volume of solvent added. V2 is the total final volume, not the amount of water you add. If V1 = 20 mL and V2 = 100 mL, you add 80 mL of solvent, not 100 mL. Adding 100 mL would give you 120 mL total, and your concentration would be lower than intended.

Mistake 2. Mismatched units. Using mL on one side and L on the other side. Using M on one side and mM on the other. Always convert to matching units before calculating. A common trap: 0.5 M is not the same as 0.5 mM. That factor of 1000 difference will ruin your experiment.

Mistake 3. Trying to concentrate instead of dilute. If C2 is greater than C1, the equation still gives you an answer, but it means you need to add more solute, not more solvent. C1V1 = C2V2 only describes dilution. If you need to increase concentration, you must either evaporate solvent or add more solute, and this equation does not apply.

Mistake 4. Not accounting for the volume of stock solution. When you add 50 mL of stock to a flask and fill to 500 mL, the total volume is 500 mL. But some students add 50 mL of stock to 500 mL of water, making 550 mL. Using a volumetric flask and filling to the line avoids this. If you are not using a volumetric flask, subtract V1 from V2 to find the amount of solvent to add.

Mistake 5. Using the wrong C1 for commercial stock solutions. Concentrated stock solutions often list concentration in different ways. Hydrochloric acid might be labeled as "37% HCl, density 1.19 g/mL." You cannot directly use 37 as C1 in a molarity-based calculation. You need to convert 37% w/w to molarity: (370 g/L x 1.19) / 36.46 g/mol = 12.08 M. That is your actual C1.

Mistake 6. Skipping the mixing step. After combining stock and solvent, the solution must be mixed thoroughly. In a volumetric flask, invert and shake at least 20 times. In a beaker, use a stir plate. Concentration gradients can persist for hours in unstirred solutions, leading to inconsistent results if you pipette from different depths.

Practice Problems

Try these problems before checking the answers. Use the dilution calculator to verify your work.

Problem 1. You have a 12 M HCl stock solution. How much do you need to prepare 2 liters of 0.6 M HCl?

Problem 2. A stock solution of bromophenol blue is 5 mg/mL. You need 20 mL of a 0.25 mg/mL working solution. What volumes of stock and water do you need?

Problem 3. You perform a 1:5 serial dilution starting from 100 ug/mL. What is the concentration after the fourth dilution?

Problem 4. A cell culture protocol calls for 10 mL of medium containing 2% fetal bovine serum. Your FBS is 100% (undiluted). How much FBS and how much base medium do you combine?

Problem 5. You accidentally made 400 mL of 2 M NaCl instead of 0.5 M. How much total volume do you need to fix it, and how much water do you add?

Answers

Answer 1. V1 = (0.6 x 2000) / 12 = 100 mL. Take 100 mL of 12 M HCl and add water to reach 2000 mL total.

Answer 2. V1 = (0.25 x 20) / 5 = 1 mL. Take 1 mL of stock, add 19 mL of water.

Answer 3. After four 1:5 dilutions: 100 x (1/5)^4 = 100 x (1/625) = 0.16 ug/mL.

Answer 4. V1 = (2 x 10) / 100 = 0.2 mL. Take 0.2 mL (200 uL) of FBS and add 9.8 mL of base medium.

Answer 5. V2 = (2 x 400) / 0.5 = 1600 mL total. Add 1200 mL of water to the existing 400 mL.

Real Laboratory Applications

Clinical Chemistry

Clinical labs dilute patient samples routinely. When a blood test result exceeds the measurement range of the instrument, the sample is diluted and rerun. If a glucose assay has a linear range up to 500 mg/dL and the patient's sample reads above that, a 1:2 dilution brings a value of 900 mg/dL down to 450 mg/dL (within range). The instrument reports 450, and the technician multiplies by the dilution factor (2) to get the true result of 900 mg/dL.

The dilution must be recorded and the multiplication factor applied correctly. Failure to account for the dilution factor is a recognized source of laboratory errors in clinical settings.

Microbiology

Bacterial culture counts use serial dilutions extensively. A sample with 10^8 colony forming units per mL (CFU/mL) cannot be plated directly because the colonies would be too dense to count. A series of 1:10 dilutions to 10^-5 or 10^-6 gives plates with 30 to 300 colonies, the countable range. The count is multiplied by the dilution factor to get the original concentration.

If a plate from the 10^-6 dilution shows 42 colonies, the original concentration is 42 x 10^6 = 4.2 x 10^7 CFU/mL. If you plated 0.1 mL instead of 1 mL, multiply by another factor of 10: 4.2 x 10^8 CFU/mL.

Molecular Biology

PCR (polymerase chain reaction) requires precise dilution of DNA templates, primers, and nucleotides. A typical PCR reaction uses DNA at 1 to 10 ng/uL, but DNA extraction yields concentrations of 50 to 500 ng/uL. The working dilution ensures consistent amplification without inhibition from excess template.

Primer stocks are typically stored at 100 uM and diluted to 10 uM working stocks, then further diluted in the reaction mix to 0.2 to 0.5 uM final concentration. Each dilution step follows C1V1 = C2V2.

Using Dilution Calculators Effectively

A dilution calculator speeds up the arithmetic but does not replace understanding. The calculator cannot tell you whether your dilution is physically feasible. If the calculator says you need 0.5 microliters of stock in 500 mL of solution, the math is correct but the measurement is impractical. You would need a serial dilution instead.

When using any dilution calculator, input the values with the correct units. Double check that you are entering concentration in the format the calculator expects. Some calculators use molarity, others use percentage, and mixing up which field is the stock versus the final solution is an easy mistake to make.

Frequently Asked Questions

What does C1V1 = C2V2 mean?

C1V1 = C2V2 is the dilution equation where C1 is the initial concentration, V1 is the initial volume (or the volume of stock solution you need to take), C2 is the final desired concentration, and V2 is the final total volume. The equation works because the amount of solute before dilution equals the amount after dilution. You are simply adding more solvent to spread the same quantity of solute across a larger volume.

Can I use C1V1 = C2V2 with any concentration units?

Yes, as long as C1 and C2 use the same units. You can use molarity (M), percent weight/volume, parts per million (ppm), milligrams per liter, or any other concentration unit. The key rule is that both sides of the equation must use identical units for concentration and identical units for volume. Mixing moles per liter on one side with grams per liter on the other will give wrong answers.

What is a serial dilution and when should I use one?

A serial dilution is a series of stepwise dilutions where each step uses the output of the previous dilution as its input. You use serial dilutions when you need to go from a very concentrated solution to a very dilute one, because a single dilution step may not be accurate enough. For example, going from 1 M to 0.000001 M in one step requires measuring 1 microliter into 1 liter, which is nearly impossible to do accurately. A serial dilution of six 1:10 steps is far more precise.

How do I calculate how much stock solution to add?

Rearrange C1V1 = C2V2 to solve for V1: V1 = (C2 x V2) / C1. For example, if you need 500 mL of 0.1 M NaCl from a 5 M stock, V1 = (0.1 x 500) / 5 = 10 mL. You would take 10 mL of the 5 M stock solution and add enough water to bring the total volume to 500 mL.

What is the difference between dilution factor and dilution ratio?

Dilution factor is the ratio of the final volume to the initial volume of the sample. A 1:10 dilution means 1 part sample plus 9 parts diluent for a total of 10 parts, giving a dilution factor of 10. Dilution ratio describes the relationship between sample and diluent. A 1:10 dilution ratio can mean 1 part sample to 10 parts diluent (total 11 parts) in some fields, or 1 part sample in 10 total parts in others. Always check the convention used in your specific discipline to avoid a 10% error.

Why does C1V1 = C2V2 not work for concentrated acids?

C1V1 = C2V2 assumes that volumes are additive, meaning 10 mL of solute plus 90 mL of solvent gives exactly 100 mL of solution. For concentrated acids like sulfuric acid, mixing generates significant heat and the volumes are not additive due to molecular interactions. A 50 mL mix of concentrated sulfuric acid and water may end up at 47 mL instead of 50 mL. For rough calculations the equation still works, but for precise analytical work with concentrated acids, you should use mass-based calculations instead.

How do I convert between molarity and percent concentration?

To convert percent w/v to molarity, use the formula M = (percent x 10) / molecular weight. For example, a 5% w/v glucose solution has a molarity of (5 x 10) / 180.16 = 0.2776 M. To convert molarity to percent w/v, use percent = (M x molecular weight) / 10. You need to know the molecular weight of the solute to make this conversion. Percent v/v and percent w/w require different conversion steps involving density.

What happens if I accidentally make my solution too concentrated?

If your solution is too concentrated, you can fix it by adding more solvent. Measure the current volume, then use C1V1 = C2V2 where C1 is the concentration you accidentally made, V1 is the current volume, and C2 is your target. Solve for V2 to find the total volume needed, then add (V2 - V1) of solvent. For example, if you made 200 mL of 0.5 M instead of 0.2 M, you need V2 = (0.5 x 200) / 0.2 = 500 mL total, so add 300 mL of solvent.

How accurate do my measurements need to be for dilutions?

Accuracy requirements depend on your application. For general laboratory work, volumetric pipettes and flasks (Class A glassware) provide accuracy within 0.02 to 0.05 mL, which is sufficient for most dilutions. For analytical chemistry and clinical diagnostics, calibrated micropipettes with accuracy within 0.5 to 1% are standard. For teaching labs and non-critical work, graduated cylinders with 1 to 2% accuracy are acceptable. The rule of thumb is that your smallest measured volume should be at least 10% of your pipette or cylinder capacity to maintain accuracy.
