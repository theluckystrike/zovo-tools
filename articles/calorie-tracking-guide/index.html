The Science and Math

Weight loss is fundamentally a math problem. If you consume fewer calories than your body burns, you lose weight. If you consume more, you gain weight. This is the law of thermodynamics applied to human metabolism, and no diet, supplement, or exercise routine can override it. Every successful weight loss approach, from keto to intermittent fasting to Weight Watchers, works by creating a calorie deficit, whether or not the person following the plan realizes it.

But the math is not as simple as "eat less, move more." Your body adapts to caloric restriction. The 3,500 calories per pound rule that has been repeated for decades is misleading. Protein requirements change during a deficit. Losing weight too fast costs you muscle, which makes maintaining the loss harder. Understanding the science behind the math is what separates successful, sustainable fat loss from the cycle of crash dieting and rebound weight gain.

This guide explains every calculation involved in setting up a calorie deficit, from estimating your basal metabolic rate to planning refeeds and knowing when to stop. The Mifflin-St Jeor Equation Explained

Before you can calculate a deficit, you need to know how many calories your body burns at rest. This is your Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR), and it represents the energy required to keep your organs functioning, maintain body temperature, and support basic cellular processes while you do nothing.

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation, published in 1990 by researchers at the University of Nevada, is the most accurate predictive equation for BMR in healthy, non-obese adults. It has been validated across multiple studies and is recommended by the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics.

Male BMR = (10 x weight in kg) + (6.25 x height in cm) - (5 x age in years) + 5 Female BMR = (10 x weight in kg) + (6.25 x height in cm) - (5 x age in years) - 161

Let us work through a specific example. A 30-year-old male who weighs 180 pounds (81.6 kg) and is 5 feet 10 inches tall (177.8 cm):

BMR = (10 x 81.6) + (6.25 x 177.8) - (5 x 30) + 5

BMR = 816 + 1,111.25 - 150 + 5

BMR = 1,782 calories per day

This person burns approximately 1,782 calories per day just by existing. This does not include any physical activity, digestion, or movement of any kind. It is the absolute minimum their body needs to maintain life functions.

For a 28-year-old female who weighs 145 pounds (65.8 kg) and is 5 feet 5 inches tall (165.1 cm):

BMR = (10 x 65.8) + (6.25 x 165.1) - (5 x 28) - 161

BMR = 658 + 1,031.9 - 140 - 161

BMR = 1,389 calories per day

The equation has an accuracy of approximately plus or minus 10% for most people. This means the true BMR for the male in our example is somewhere between 1,604 and 1,960 calories. For practical purposes, you use the calculated value as a starting point and adjust based on real-world results over 2 to 4 weeks.

From BMR to TDEE

Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE) is the total number of calories you burn in a day including all activity. TDEE is what you actually need to eat to maintain your current weight. It is calculated by multiplying BMR by an activity factor.

Activity LevelDescriptionMultiplier
SedentaryOffice job, no exercise1.2
Lightly activeLight exercise 1-3 days per week1.375
Moderately activeModerate exercise 3-5 days per week1.55
Very activeHard exercise 6-7 days per week1.725
Extremely activePhysical job plus hard daily exercise1.9

Most people overestimate their activity level. If you work a desk job and exercise 3 to 4 times per week, you are likely "lightly active" (1.375), not "moderately active." The time you spend exercising is a small fraction of your 24-hour day. Unless you have a physically demanding job or you train for more than an hour daily at high intensity, 1.375 to 1.55 is the appropriate range for most adults.

For our 30-year-old male with a BMR of 1,782 and a moderately active lifestyle:

TDEE = 1,782 x 1.55 = 2,762 calories per day

This means he needs approximately 2,762 calories per day to maintain his current weight. Any caloric intake below this number creates a deficit, and any intake above it creates a surplus.

Setting Your Deficit Size

The size of your calorie deficit determines how fast you lose weight and what that weight consists of. Bigger deficits cause faster weight loss but increase the proportion of muscle lost, increase metabolic adaptation, and are harder to sustain psychologically.

Research supports the following deficit ranges based on goals:

Deficit SizeCalories Below TDEEExpected Weekly LossBest For
Small200-300 calories0.25-0.5 lbsAlready lean (under 15% male, under 22% female), preserving maximum muscle
Moderate400-600 calories0.75-1.25 lbsAverage body fat, balanced approach
Aggressive700-1000 calories1.5-2 lbsHigher body fat (over 25% male, over 32% female), time-limited goals

A useful guideline is to target a deficit of no more than 0.5 to 1% of your body weight per week. For a 200-pound person, this is 1 to 2 pounds per week. For a 140-pound person, this is 0.7 to 1.4 pounds per week. Heavier individuals can sustain larger deficits without as much muscle loss because their body has more fat reserves to draw from.

For our 30-year-old male with a TDEE of 2,762, a moderate deficit of 500 calories puts his target intake at 2,262 calories per day. At this deficit, he should lose approximately 1 pound per week, assuming the 3,500 calorie rule is approximately valid in the short term (which it is, within the first 4 to 8 weeks).

Why the 3,500 Calorie Rule is Wrong

The 3,500 calorie rule originated from a 1958 paper by Max Wishnofsky that calculated the energy content of one pound of adipose tissue. one pound of body fat contains approximately 3,500 calories of stored energy, so a deficit of 3,500 calories should cause one pound of fat loss.

The rule is roughly accurate for short-term predictions (the first few weeks of a diet). But it becomes progressively more inaccurate over time because it ignores three critical factors.

First, as you lose weight, your body gets smaller and requires fewer calories to maintain. A person who loses 20 pounds has a lower TDEE than when they started, which means the same calorie intake that initially created a 500-calorie deficit may only create a 300-calorie deficit after 3 months.

Second, not all weight lost is fat. Some is muscle (typically 25% of weight lost during a moderate deficit without resistance training, reduced to 10-15% with proper resistance training and protein intake). Muscle has a different energy density than fat, containing about 700 calories per pound rather than 3,500.

Third, metabolic adaptation reduces energy expenditure beyond what is explained by the loss of mass alone. Your body becomes more efficient, reducing fidgeting, lowering thyroid output slightly, and reducing the energy cost of movement.

The National Institutes of Health developed the Body Weight Planner to account for these dynamic changes. It uses differential equations to model weight loss over time and produces much more accurate predictions. According to this model, a 500-calorie daily deficit that the 3,500 rule predicts would cause 52 pounds of loss in one year actually produces approximately 25 to 30 pounds of loss. The 3,500 rule overestimates long-term weight loss by roughly 50% or more.

The Components of Daily Energy Expenditure

Understanding where your calories go helps you identify the most effective levers for creating and maintaining a deficit.

Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR) accounts for 60 to 70% of total daily energy expenditure. This is the largest component and is determined primarily by body mass, height, age, and sex. You cannot meaningfully increase BMR through exercise (adding muscle helps, but the effect is modest, approximately 6 calories per pound of muscle per day at rest).

The Thermic Effect of Food (TEF) accounts for 8 to 15% of total intake. This is the energy required to digest, absorb, and process the food you eat. Different macronutrients have different thermic effects. Protein has the highest TEF at 20 to 30% (meaning 20 to 30% of protein calories are burned during digestion). Carbohydrates have a TEF of 5 to 10%. Fat has the lowest TEF at 0 to 3%. This is one reason high-protein diets are effective for fat loss: a greater percentage of calories is burned simply by digesting the food.

Non-Exercise Activity Thermogenesis (NEAT) accounts for 15 to 30% of daily expenditure in most people. NEAT includes all physical activity that is not deliberate exercise: walking, fidgeting, standing, typing, cooking, cleaning. NEAT varies enormously between individuals and is one of the primary ways the body adjusts to caloric restriction. During a deficit, NEAT drops as you unconsciously move less, sit more, and fidget less. This can amount to 200 to 400 fewer calories burned per day.

Exercise Activity Thermogenesis (EAT) accounts for 5 to 15% of daily expenditure for most people, and up to 30% for highly active individuals. A typical gym session burns 200 to 400 calories. An hour of running burns 400 to 800 calories depending on pace and body weight. Exercise is important for health and muscle preservation, but it is a relatively small lever compared to dietary changes for creating a calorie deficit.

Macronutrient Distribution During a Deficit

Total calories determine whether you lose weight. Macronutrient distribution (the ratio of protein, carbohydrates, and fat) determines what that weight consists of and how you feel during the process.

Protein Requirements

Protein is the most important macronutrient during a calorie deficit. It preserves lean muscle mass, has the highest thermic effect, and is the most satiating macronutrient (it keeps you feeling full longer than carbs or fat per calorie).

Research consistently supports protein intake of 0.7 to 1.0 grams per pound of body weight per day during a deficit (1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram). Some studies show benefits up to 1.4 grams per pound in lean, highly active individuals during aggressive deficits.

For our 180-pound male in a 500-calorie deficit (target 2,262 cal/day):

Protein: 180 g/day (at 1.0 g/lb) = 720 calories from protein

Remaining calories: 2,262 - 720 = 1,542 calories for carbs and fat

Fat (minimum 0.3 g/lb): 54 g/day = 486 calories from fat

Remaining for carbs: 1,542 - 486 = 1,056 calories = 264 g carbohydrates

Final macros: 180g protein, 264g carbs, 54g fat (2,262 total calories)

If you are significantly overweight (over 30% body fat), base your protein calculation on lean body mass or target body weight rather than current body weight. A 300-pound person does not need 300 grams of protein per day. Using a target weight of 200 pounds gives a more practical target of 140 to 200 grams.

Fat Requirements

Dietary fat is essential for hormone production (including testosterone and estrogen), absorption of fat-soluble vitamins (A, D, E, K), cell membrane integrity, and brain function. Never reduce fat intake below 0.3 grams per pound of body weight per day (0.6 grams per kilogram). Going lower risks hormonal disruption, poor recovery, and impaired cognitive function.

Most people do well with fat at 25 to 35% of total calories. During a deficit, keeping fat at the lower end of this range frees up more calories for carbohydrates, which support training performance and mood.

Carbohydrate Requirements

Carbohydrates are the most flexible macronutrient. After protein and minimum fat are set, remaining calories go to carbs. Carbs fuel high-intensity exercise, support recovery, maintain training performance, and influence mood through serotonin production. Athletes and people who train intensely benefit from keeping carbs as high as possible within their calorie budget.

Metabolic Adaptation and How to Manage It

Metabolic adaptation is the body's defense mechanism against starvation. When you eat fewer calories than you burn, your body makes several adjustments to conserve energy. These adjustments are well documented in research and include the following.

Reduced NEAT. Your spontaneous physical activity decreases. You move less, fidget less, and choose less effortful options (taking the elevator instead of stairs, sitting instead of standing). This is largely unconscious. Studies have measured NEAT reductions of 200 to 400 calories per day during sustained caloric restriction.

Reduced thermic effect of food. With less food to process, TEF decreases proportionally. If you drop from 2,800 to 2,200 calories, TEF drops by approximately 50 to 80 calories per day.

Hormonal changes. Leptin (the satiety hormone) decreases by 40 to 60% within the first week of caloric restriction, signaling the brain that energy is scarce. Thyroid hormone (T3) decreases by 10 to 15%, slightly reducing metabolic rate. Ghrelin (the hunger hormone) increases, making you feel hungrier. Cortisol may increase, promoting water retention and making the scale misleading.

Reduced exercise efficiency. Your body becomes more mechanically efficient during exercise, burning 5 to 10% fewer calories for the same workout. A run that used to burn 500 calories may only burn 450 to 475 calories after weeks of dieting.

The total impact of metabolic adaptation is typically 50 to 150 calories per day for moderate deficits sustained over 8 to 16 weeks. This is significant but not catastrophic. It means your effective deficit shrinks over time, which is why fat loss slows down even if you are eating the same amount.

Refeeds and Diet Breaks

Refeeds and diet breaks are strategic tools for managing metabolic adaptation, improving diet adherence, and sustaining fat loss over longer periods.

Refeed Days

A refeed day is a single day where you increase calorie intake to approximately maintenance level, with the extra calories coming primarily from carbohydrates. The rationale is that carbohydrate intake has the strongest effect on leptin levels. After a day of higher carb intake, leptin levels temporarily increase, which can reduce hunger and improve mood for the following days.

A practical refeed protocol for our 180-pound male:

Normal deficit day: 2,262 calories (180g protein, 264g carbs, 54g fat)

Refeed day: 2,762 calories (maintenance) (180g protein, 389g carbs, 54g fat)

The extra 500 calories come entirely from carbohydrates (125g additional carbs).

Frequency: 1 refeed day per week for those above 15% body fat. 2 refeed days per week for those under 15% body fat or in larger deficits.

Refeeds do not "reset" your metabolism. The leptin increase is temporary (24 to 48 hours). But the cumulative effect of regular refeeds on adherence and hormonal markers is meaningful over weeks and months of dieting.

Diet Breaks

A diet break is a planned period of 1 to 2 weeks at maintenance calories. The MATADOR study (Minimising Adaptive Thermogenesis And Deactivating Obesity Rebound) published in the International Journal of Obesity in 2018 provided compelling evidence for this approach. Participants who alternated 2 weeks of deficit with 2 weeks of maintenance lost more fat and had less metabolic adaptation than the continuous dieting group, even though the total number of days in a deficit was the same.

During a diet break, maintain protein intake at the same level as during the deficit. Increase carbs and fat to bring total calories up to maintenance. Continue resistance training at the same intensity and volume. Do not think of a diet break as "going off the diet." You are still controlling intake. You are eating a precise number of calories that matches your expenditure, with no deficit and no surplus.

A sensible dieting timeline might look like this:

WeeksPhaseCalories
1-6Deficit2,262/day
7-8Diet break (maintenance)2,762/day
9-14Deficit2,262/day
15-16Diet break (maintenance)2,700/day (adjusted for weight lost)

Notice that TDEE and deficit calories are recalculated after each diet break to account for the weight already lost. A body that now weighs 170 pounds has a lower TDEE than when it weighed 180 pounds.

When to Stop Cutting

Knowing when to end a calorie deficit is just as important as knowing how to start one. Dieting indefinitely is counterproductive and potentially harmful. Here are the objective criteria for ending a cut.

You have reached your target body composition. If you wanted to get to 12% body fat and you are there, stop. Continuing to diet past your goal leads to diminishing returns, increased muscle loss, and hormonal disruption.

You have been in a continuous deficit for 16 to 20 weeks (even with refeeds). Extended deficits accumulate metabolic and hormonal adaptations that take time at maintenance to reverse. A minimum of 8 to 12 weeks at maintenance calories between cutting phases allows hormones to normalize.

Your training performance has declined substantially and is not recovering. If your strength has dropped more than 10% across multiple exercises, or if you cannot complete workouts you previously handled, the deficit is likely too large or too prolonged.

You are experiencing persistent symptoms of overrestriction: constant fatigue, poor sleep quality, irritability, loss of menstrual cycle (in women), decreased libido, frequent illness, or inability to concentrate. These are signs that the deficit is causing physiological stress that outweighs the benefit of further fat loss.

Your calorie target has dropped to or below the minimum safe intake (approximately 1,200 calories for women, 1,500 for men). At these levels, it becomes extremely difficult to meet micronutrient needs, and the risk of muscle loss increases sharply.

Reverse Dieting Out of a Deficit

After ending a cut, avoid jumping immediately from deficit calories back to estimated maintenance. The sudden increase in food intake, especially carbohydrates, causes water weight gain (each gram of glycogen stored in muscle binds approximately 3 grams of water) and can trigger psychological distress when the scale jumps 3 to 5 pounds in a week, even though virtually none of it is fat.

A reverse diet adds calories gradually over 4 to 8 weeks. A practical approach adds 100 to 150 calories per week, primarily from carbohydrates, while monitoring weight and body measurements. The goal is to reach your new maintenance level (which is lower than your pre-diet maintenance because you weigh less) with minimal fat regain.

Week 1 after deficit: 2,262 + 100 = 2,362 calories

Week 2: 2,362 + 100 = 2,462 calories

Week 3: 2,462 + 100 = 2,562 calories

Week 4: 2,562 + 100 = 2,662 calories (approaching estimated new maintenance)

Stabilize at 2,650-2,700 calories and monitor weight for 2 weeks.

If weight is stable, you have found your new maintenance.

During the reverse diet, weight will increase due to water, glycogen, and increased food volume in the digestive tract. A gain of 2 to 4 pounds in the first two weeks is normal and is not fat gain. Track the trend over weeks, not daily fluctuations.

Practical Tracking Methods

You cannot manage what you do not measure. Tracking calories does not need to be obsessive, but it does need to be accurate, at least initially, to calibrate your intuition.

A food scale is the most important tool. Nutrition labels list serving sizes in grams and ounces. Eyeballing portions routinely underestimates calorie intake by 20 to 50%. A tablespoon of peanut butter "scooped casually" might be 2 tablespoons, turning 95 calories into 190. A food scale costs $10 to $15 and pays for itself in accuracy immediately.

Track for a minimum of 2 to 4 weeks when starting a deficit to learn what your portion sizes actually look like. After that period, many people can maintain their deficit by estimating portions based on the knowledge they gained during tracking. If fat loss stalls, return to precise tracking for a week to see if portion creep has closed your deficit.

Weigh yourself daily at the same time (ideally first thing in the morning after using the bathroom, before eating or drinking). Record the number but evaluate only the weekly average. Daily weight can fluctuate by 2 to 5 pounds due to water retention, food volume, sodium intake, and hormonal cycles. The weekly average eliminates this noise and shows the true trend.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many calories should I eat to lose weight?

The number depends on your Total Daily Energy Expenditure (TDEE). Calculate your TDEE using the Mifflin-St Jeor equation for BMR and multiply by your activity factor, then subtract 300 to 500 calories for moderate weight loss or 500 to 750 calories for faster loss. For example, a 180-pound, 5'10" male aged 30 with moderate activity has a TDEE of roughly 2,600 calories. Eating 2,100 to 2,300 calories per day would produce steady fat loss of about 0.5 to 1 pound per week. Never go below 1,200 calories per day for women or 1,500 for men without medical supervision.

What is the Mifflin-St Jeor equation?

The Mifflin-St Jeor equation estimates Basal Metabolic Rate (BMR), which is the number of calories your body burns at complete rest. BMR = (10 x weight in kg) + (6.25 x height in cm) - (5 x age in years) + 5. BMR = (10 x weight in kg) + (6.25 x height in cm) - (5 x age in years) - 161. This equation was published in 1990 and is considered the most accurate predictive equation for BMR in healthy adults, with an accuracy of plus or minus 10% for most people.

Is the 3,500 calorie rule for weight loss accurate?

The 3,500 calorie rule states that a deficit of 3,500 calories produces one pound of fat loss. This is approximately correct for short-term predictions but becomes increasingly inaccurate over time. The rule assumes that all weight lost is fat and that metabolic rate stays constant, neither of which is true. As you lose weight, your body burns fewer calories and metabolic adaptation further reduces energy expenditure. A more accurate model is the NIH Body Weight Planner, which accounts for these dynamic changes. In practice, the 3,500 calorie rule overestimates long-term weight loss by 50% or more.

What is metabolic adaptation and can I prevent it?

Metabolic adaptation (sometimes called adaptive thermogenesis) is the reduction in energy expenditure beyond what is explained by the loss of body mass. When you eat fewer calories, your body becomes more efficient, reducing non-exercise activity thermogenesis (fidgeting, posture maintenance, spontaneous movement), lowering thyroid hormone output slightly, and reducing the thermic effect of food. Research shows metabolic adaptation typically amounts to 50 to 100 calories per day in moderate deficits. You cannot fully prevent it, but you can reduce it by keeping the deficit moderate (no more than 25% below TDEE), maintaining high protein intake, continuing resistance training, and incorporating periodic refeeds or diet breaks.

How much protein do I need during a calorie deficit?

During a calorie deficit, protein needs increase above maintenance levels because your body is more likely to break down muscle for energy when calories are restricted. Research consistently shows that 0.7 to 1.0 grams of protein per pound of body weight per day (1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram) preserves lean mass during weight loss. For a 180-pound person, that is 126 to 180 grams of protein daily. Leaner individuals and those with larger deficits should aim for the higher end. Protein also has the highest thermic effect of any macronutrient, meaning roughly 20 to 30% of protein calories are burned during digestion.

What is a refeed day and how does it help?

A refeed day is a planned day of higher calorie intake, typically at or slightly above maintenance calories, with the extra calories coming primarily from carbohydrates. The physiological purpose is to temporarily increase leptin levels (a hormone that drops during caloric restriction and signals the brain about energy availability), replenish muscle glycogen stores, and provide a psychological break from dieting. Research suggests that one to two refeed days per week during a prolonged deficit can improve hormone profiles and diet adherence without significantly slowing fat loss. Refeeds are different from cheat days because they are planned, controlled, and focused on carbohydrates rather than unrestricted eating.

What is a diet break and when should I take one?

A diet break is a planned period of 1 to 2 weeks where you eat at maintenance calories (no deficit). The MATADOR study found that participants who alternated 2 weeks of dieting with 2 weeks of maintenance calories lost more fat and experienced less metabolic adaptation than those who dieted continuously for the same total number of deficit days. Consider a diet break after every 6 to 12 weeks of continuous dieting, when fat loss stalls despite consistent adherence, when training performance drops significantly, or when mental fatigue and cravings become difficult to manage.

When should I stop cutting and return to maintenance?

Stop cutting when you reach your target body fat percentage or weight, when you have been in a deficit for more than 16 to 20 continuous weeks, when your performance in the gym has dropped substantially and is not recovering, when you are experiencing persistent fatigue, irritability, or poor sleep, or when your calculated deficit calories are approaching the minimum safe intake (1,200 for women, 1,500 for men). After ending a cut, reverse diet by adding 100 to 200 calories per week back toward maintenance rather than jumping straight to full maintenance, which can cause rapid water weight regain and psychological distress.

Does it matter when I eat my calories or just how many?

For fat loss, total daily calorie intake matters far more than meal timing. You will lose the same amount of fat eating 2,000 calories in two meals or six meals, assuming total intake is identical. However, meal timing can affect adherence, performance, and muscle retention. Eating protein distributed across 3 to 5 meals improves muscle protein synthesis better than consuming all protein in one meal. Having carbohydrates around workouts supports training intensity. And most people find a consistent eating schedule helps them avoid overeating. The best meal timing pattern is whichever one you can stick to consistently.
