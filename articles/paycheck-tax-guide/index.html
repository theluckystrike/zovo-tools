By Michael Lip / March 20, 2026 / 22 min read

I have reviewed hundreds of pay stubs over the years, both my own and those of friends who asked me to help them understand where their money was going. The single most consistent reaction is surprise. People earning $65,000 a year expect to see roughly $65,000 worth of paychecks. Instead they see something closer to $48,000 or $50,000, and the gap between gross pay and take-home pay feels like a mystery wrapped in acronyms.

It does not have to be confusing. Paycheck taxes follow a set of rules that are entirely predictable once you understand the structure. This guide walks through every layer of tax that sits between your gross salary and the number that actually hits your bank account.

Quick Facts

  • Federal income tax uses 7 progressive brackets ranging from 10% to 37% (2025 rates)
  • FICA taxes total 7.65% for employees (6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare)
  • 9 states have no income tax on wages
  • The standard deduction for 2025 is $15,000 for single filers and $30,000 for married filing jointly
  • Pre-tax deductions like 401(k) contributions reduce your taxable income before any tax is calculated

An income tax is a tax imposed on individuals or entities (taxpayers) in respect of the income or profits earned by them. Income tax generally is computed as the product of a tax rate times the taxable income. Taxation rates may vary by type or characteristics of the taxpayer and the type of income.

How Federal Income Tax Brackets Work

The United States uses a progressive tax system. This means different portions of your income are taxed at different rates. The word "bracket" refers to a range of income that is taxed at a specific rate.

A common misconception is that moving into a higher bracket means all of your income is taxed at the higher rate. That is not how it works. Only the income within each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. The rest of your income continues to be taxed at the lower rates.

Here are the 2025 federal income tax brackets for single filers.

Let me walk through a concrete example. Say you earn $85,000 in gross wages and take the standard deduction of $15,000. Your taxable income is $70,000.

The first $11,925 is taxed at 10%, which equals $1,192.50. The next $36,550 (from $11,926 to $48,475) is taxed at 12%, which equals $4,386.00. The remaining $21,525 (from $48,476 to $70,000) is taxed at 22%, which equals $4,735.50.

Total federal income tax: $10,314. That is an effective rate of about 14.7% on taxable income, or about 12.1% of your gross $85,000 salary. Your marginal rate is 22%, but you are not paying 22% on everything.

For married couples filing jointly, the brackets are roughly double the single-filer amounts. The 10% bracket extends to $23,850, the 12% bracket runs to $96,950, and so on. This is the structural reason why marriage can reduce taxes for couples where one spouse earns significantly more than the other.

FICA Taxes Explained

FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act. Social Security and Medicare. FICA is separate from federal income tax and is calculated independently.

Social Security tax is 6.2% of your gross wages, up to the wage base limit. In 2025, that limit is $176,100. Every dollar you earn above that amount is exempt from Social Security tax. Your employer pays an additional 6.2%, so the total Social Security contribution is 12.4% of your wages.

Medicare tax is 1.45% of all wages with no cap. Your employer pays a matching 1.45%. If you earn more than $200,000 as a single filer ($250,000 for married filing jointly), you pay an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax on wages above that threshold. Your employer does not match this surtax.

Combined, the employee portion of FICA is 7.65% on wages up to $176,100 and 1.45% (plus the potential 0.9% surtax) on wages above that.

On an $85,000 salary, your FICA deductions look like this. $85,000 x 6.2% = $5,270. $85,000 x 1.45% = $1,232.50. $6,502.50. That is in addition to the $10,314 in federal income tax from the example above.

FICA taxes are regressive in practice because of the Social Security wage cap. Someone earning $176,100 and someone earning $500,000 both pay the same $10,918.20 in Social Security tax. As a percentage of total income, the higher earner pays a much smaller share.

Self-employed individuals pay both the employer and employee portions, totaling 15.3%. This is called self-employment tax. However, self-employed workers can deduct the employer-equivalent half (7.65%) when calculating adjusted gross income, partially offsetting the higher rate.

State Income Tax Overview

Federal taxes are only part of the picture. Most states impose their own income tax, and the rates and structures vary dramatically.

Among the states that do tax income, there are two general approaches. Flat-rate states charge the same percentage regardless of income. Progressive states use brackets similar to the federal system.

Notable flat-rate states include Illinois (4.95%), Michigan (4.25%), Colorado (4.4%), Indiana (3.05%), and Pennsylvania (3.07%). Notable progressive states include California (1% to 13.3%), New York (4% to 10.9%), New Jersey (1.4% to 10.75%), and Hawaii (1.4% to 11%).

California's top rate of 13.3% is the highest state income tax rate in the country. A high-earning California resident in the 37% federal bracket pays a combined marginal rate of 50.3% on their top dollars, before accounting for FICA.

Some cities also impose their own income taxes. New York City adds 3.078% to 3.876% on top of New York state taxes. Several Ohio cities levy municipal income taxes. These local taxes add another layer to paycheck deductions.

The state you live in has a significant impact on take-home pay. An $85,000 salary in Texas takes home meaningfully more than the same salary in California or New York. A Salary Calculator lets you compare net pay across different states.

Pre-Tax vs. Post-Tax Deductions

Deductions from your paycheck fall into two categories, and understanding the difference matters more than most people realize.

Pre-tax deductions are subtracted from your gross pay before federal income tax, state income tax, and (in most cases) FICA taxes are calculated. By reducing your taxable income, these deductions reduce the amount of tax you owe. Every dollar contributed pre-tax saves you money at your marginal tax rate.

Common pre-tax deductions include traditional 401(k) and 403(b) contributions (up to $23,500 in 2025, or $31,000 if age 50+), health insurance premiums paid through your employer's plan, Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions ($4,300 individual / $8,550 family in 2025), Flexible Spending Account (FSA) contributions for medical and dependent care, and commuter benefits for transit and parking.

Post-tax deductions are subtracted after taxes have been calculated. They do not reduce your current tax burden. Common post-tax deductions include Roth 401(k) contributions (you pay tax now but withdrawals in retirement are tax-free), disability insurance premiums, life insurance premiums above $50,000 in coverage, union dues, and wage garnishments.

Here is a practical example showing the difference. Say you earn $5,000 gross per pay period and you are in the 22% federal bracket with a 5% state tax rate.

If you contribute $500 pre-tax to a 401(k), your taxable income for that period drops to $4,500. $500 x 27% (22% federal + 5% state) = $135 per paycheck. Over 24 pay periods, that is $3,240 in annual tax savings while building $12,000 in retirement savings.

If instead you contribute $500 post-tax to a Roth 401(k), your taxable income stays at $5,000. You pay $135 more in taxes per check. The trade-off is that your Roth withdrawals in retirement will be completely tax-free.

Choosing between pre-tax and Roth depends on whether you expect your tax rate to be higher or lower in retirement. If you are early in your career and in a low bracket, Roth contributions often make sense. If you are in your peak earning years and in a high bracket, pre-tax contributions typically save more money.

How to Read Your Pay Stub

A pay stub is a record of your earnings and deductions for a specific pay period. Every employer is required to provide this information, though the format varies. Here is what each section means.

Gross Pay is your total earnings before any deductions. For salaried employees, this is your annual salary divided by the number of pay periods (typically 24 for semi-monthly or 26 for bi-weekly). For hourly employees, it is hours worked multiplied by your hourly rate, plus any overtime.

Federal Withholding (or FIT, FWT) is the federal income tax withheld based on your W-4 elections. This amount is calculated using IRS withholding tables and depends on your filing status, number of dependents claimed, and any additional withholding you requested.

Social Security (or OASDI, SS) is 6.2% of your gross pay, up to the annual wage base. Once your year-to-date earnings reach $176,100, this line drops to zero for the rest of the year.

Medicare (or MED, HI) is 1.45% of your gross pay. Unlike Social Security, there is no cap. If your cumulative wages exceed $200,000, you will see the Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% start appearing.

State Tax (or SIT, SWT) is your state income tax withholding, if applicable. The calculation depends on your state's rate structure and your state W-4 elections.

Local Tax covers city, county, or school district taxes where applicable. Not all employees have this line.

The deductions section lists pre-tax items (401k, health insurance, HSA) and post-tax items (Roth 401k, life insurance, garnishments). Pre-tax items appear before the tax calculations in the math, post-tax items appear after.

Year-to-date (YTD) totals track your cumulative earnings and deductions for the calendar year. These numbers are useful for verifying your W-2 at tax time and checking whether you have approached the Social Security wage base.

Net Pay (or Take-Home Pay) is the final number. Gross pay minus all taxes and deductions. This is what goes into your bank account.

If the numbers on your stub look wrong, use an ADP-style Paycheck Calculator to verify. Enter your gross pay, filing status, and deductions to see what the correct withholding should be.

Common Tax Withholding Mistakes

I see the same withholding errors repeatedly. Each one costs real money, either through over-withholding (you are giving the government an interest-free loan) or under-withholding (you face a surprise tax bill and possible penalties).

The first mistake is not updating your W-4 after major life changes. Getting married, having a child, buying a home, or having a spouse start or stop working all affect your optimal withholding. Many people fill out a W-4 on their first day of work and never touch it again for years.

The second mistake is claiming too many or too few allowances on older W-4 forms. The pre-2020 W-4 used "allowances" that each reduced withholding by a fixed amount. People would claim excessive allowances to boost their paycheck, then face a large tax bill in April. The redesigned W-4 uses dollar amounts instead of allowances, which is more transparent.

The third mistake is ignoring two-income household dynamics. When both spouses work, the combined income pushes the household into higher brackets. If each spouse fills out their W-4 as if they are the sole earner, both withhold too little, and the couple owes money at tax time. The W-4's Step 2 addresses this, but many people skip it.

The fourth mistake is not accounting for non-wage income. Investment income, rental income, side gig income, and retirement distributions are not subject to employer withholding. If these sources are significant, you need to either increase your W-4 withholding (Step 4(a)) or make quarterly estimated payments.

The fifth mistake is treating a large refund as a bonus. A $4,000 refund means you overpaid by $333 per month throughout the year. That money could have been earning interest in a high-yield savings account at 4.5%, generating $180 in interest over the year. The optimal refund is as close to zero as possible.

The W-4 Form Explained

The IRS redesigned the W-4 in 2020, and the new version is both simpler and more confusing than the old one, depending on your situation.

Step 1 asks for your personal information and filing status. Your options are Single (or Married filing separately), Married filing jointly, and Head of household. This determines which withholding table your employer uses.

Step 2 applies if you have multiple jobs or your spouse works. You have three options: use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator online (most accurate), use the Multiple Jobs Worksheet on page 3 of the W-4, or check the box in 2(c) if there are only two jobs with similar pay. Checking the 2(c) box tells the employer to use higher withholding tables. Both spouses or all job W-4s need to use the same method.

Step 3 lets you claim tax credits for dependents. Enter $2,000 per qualifying child under 17, and $500 per other dependent. These are annual amounts, and the employer prorates them across your pay periods to reduce withholding.

Step 4 has three optional entries. Line 4(a) is for other income not from jobs (interest, dividends, retirement income) that you want covered by withholding. Line 4(b) is for deductions beyond the standard deduction. If you itemize and your total deductions exceed the standard amount, enter the difference here to reduce withholding. Line 4(c) is for any additional withholding you want per pay period.

Step 5 is your signature. That is the entire form.

If you have a single job, no dependents, and take the standard deduction, you only need to fill out Step 1 and Step 5. The default withholding based on your filing status will be approximately correct.

For more complex situations, the IRS withholding estimator at irs.gov/W4App is the most reliable tool. It asks for your pay frequency, year-to-date withholding, expected annual income, deductions, and credits, then tells you exactly what to enter on your W-4.

Quarterly Estimated Taxes for Freelancers

If you work for yourself, taxes are your responsibility. No employer withholds anything. The IRS expects you to pay taxes as you earn income, not in one lump sum on April 15.

Estimated tax payments are due four times per year. The due dates for the 2025 tax year are April 15, June 16, September 15, and January 15 (of the following year). These dates do not split the year into equal quarters. Q1 covers January through March (3 months), Q2 covers April and May (2 months), Q3 covers June through August (3 months), and Q4 covers September through December (4 months).

To calculate your estimated tax, project your total income for the year, subtract business expenses and deductions, calculate federal income tax on the result, add self-employment tax (15.3% of 92.35% of net self-employment income, less the deduction for the employer-equivalent half), and divide by four.

Here is an example. You are a freelance consultant expecting $120,000 in net self-employment income for 2025, filing single with the standard deduction.

Self-employment tax: $120,000 x 92.35% x 15.3% = $16,947. The deductible half is $8,474. Your adjusted gross income is $120,000 minus $8,474 = $111,526. After the standard deduction of $15,000, taxable income is $96,526. Federal income tax on $96,526 (2025 brackets): approximately $15,507. $15,507 + $16,947 = $32,454. approximately $8,114.

You also need to make estimated payments for state income tax if your state requires them. Most states that have income tax also require quarterly estimated payments.

The safe harbor rule lets you avoid underpayment penalties if you pay at least 100% of last year's total tax liability through estimated payments (110% if your AGI exceeded $150,000). Alternatively, paying at least 90% of your current year's tax liability through estimated payments also avoids penalties.

Most freelancers set aside 25-30% of every payment they receive into a separate savings account dedicated to taxes. This prevents the quarterly shock of writing a five-figure check.

How Tax Deductions and Credits Differ

Tax deductions and tax credits both reduce what you owe, but they work differently, and credits are almost always more valuable.

A tax deduction reduces your taxable income. If you are in the 22% bracket and claim a $1,000 deduction, your tax bill drops by $220 ($1,000 x 22%). The value of a deduction depends on your marginal rate. A $1,000 deduction is worth $370 to someone in the 37% bracket but only $100 to someone in the 10% bracket.

A tax credit reduces your tax bill dollar for dollar. A $1,000 credit saves you $1,000 regardless of your bracket. Credits are much more powerful than deductions of the same dollar amount.

Refundable credits can reduce your tax bill below zero, resulting in a refund. The Earned Income Tax Credit (EITC) and the refundable portion of the Child Tax Credit are examples. Non-refundable credits can only reduce your tax bill to zero.

Common credits include the Child Tax Credit ($2,000 per qualifying child under 17), the Earned Income Tax Credit (up to $7,830 for 2025 with three or more children), the American Opportunity Tax Credit (up to $2,500 per student for the first four years of college), the Lifetime Learning Credit (up to $2,000 per return for education expenses), and the Saver's Credit (up to $1,000/$2,000 for low and moderate income retirement contributions).

Common deductions include the standard deduction ($15,000 single / $30,000 married filing jointly for 2025), state and local tax deduction (capped at $10,000), mortgage interest deduction, charitable contributions, and student loan interest (up to $2,500).

About 87% of taxpayers take the standard deduction. Itemizing only makes sense if your total deductible expenses exceed the standard deduction amount.

Paycheck Frequency and Its Impact

How often you get paid affects the math of each individual paycheck, even though your annual take-home is the same. Understanding the differences helps when budgeting.

Bi-weekly and semi-monthly are often confused. Bi-weekly means every two weeks (26 paychecks), while semi-monthly means twice per month on fixed dates, usually the 1st and 15th (24 paychecks). Bi-weekly gives you two "bonus" paychecks per year since two months will have three pay dates.

Tax withholding per check is calculated by dividing your estimated annual tax by the number of pay periods. A semi-monthly check withholds more per check than a bi-weekly check because there are fewer checks per year, even though the annual total is identical.

For budgeting, bi-weekly pay works well if you base your monthly budget on two paychecks. The two months with three paychecks become a built-in savings mechanism. Semi-monthly pay aligns better with monthly bills since dates are consistent.

How Bonuses and Supplemental Income Are Taxed

Bonuses, commissions, overtime, and other supplemental wages can be taxed differently than regular wages in terms of withholding. The actual tax you owe at year-end is the same regardless of withholding method, but the amount withheld from each check varies.

The IRS allows employers to use one of two withholding methods for supplemental income.

The Percentage Method applies a flat 22% federal withholding rate to the supplemental payment. For amounts exceeding $1 million in a calendar year, the rate jumps to 37%. This method is simpler and produces consistent withholding regardless of the size of your regular pay.

The Aggregate Method combines the supplemental payment with your regular pay for that period and calculates withholding on the combined amount as if it were a single payment. This often results in higher withholding because the combined amount may push the single-period calculation into a higher bracket.

Here is why bonuses sometimes feel heavily taxed. You receive a $10,000 bonus. Under the percentage method, $2,200 is withheld for federal tax. Under the aggregate method, if your regular bi-weekly gross is $3,269, the combined $13,269 may be projected to an annual income of $345,000, triggering withholding at a much higher rate. You might see $3,500 or more withheld from the bonus.

The excess withholding is not lost. It is reconciled on your tax return, and any over-withholding is refunded. But the paycheck shock is real, and it is the number one complaint I hear about bonus taxation.

Commissions may be treated as supplemental wages or regular wages depending on how your employer categorizes them. If treated as supplemental, the same 22% flat rate or aggregate method applies.

Year-End Tax Planning With Your Paycheck

The last few months of the year are the best time to improve your paycheck withholding. By October or November, you have enough year-to-date data to predict your final tax liability with reasonable accuracy.

Start by checking your YTD withholding against your projected total tax. If you are on track for a large refund, consider reducing withholding for the remaining paychecks by submitting a new W-4 with adjustments. If you are under-withheld, increase your W-4 withholding to catch up before year-end.

Consider maximizing pre-tax contributions before December 31. If you have not contributed the full $23,500 to your 401(k), increasing your contribution percentage for the remaining paychecks reduces your taxable income. Some employers allow lump-sum catch-up contributions.

HSA contributions can also be increased through year-end. The 2025 limits are $4,300 for self-only coverage and $8,550 for family coverage. HSA contributions are triple tax-advantaged: deductible going in, grow tax-free, and are tax-free coming out for qualified medical expenses.

If you have stock options or RSUs vesting in December, the vesting creates taxable income. Your employer will withhold taxes on the vested amount, but the default withholding may be insufficient if the vesting pushes you into a higher bracket. Planning ahead with your W-4 prevents a surprise April tax bill.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much of my paycheck goes to federal taxes?

The amount depends on your taxable income and filing status. For a single filer earning $60,000 in 2025, the effective federal income tax rate is approximately 13.1%, meaning about $7,860 goes to federal income tax. Add 7.65% for FICA (Social Security and Medicare), and total federal deductions are roughly 20.8%. Your actual amount varies based on W-4 elections, pre-tax deductions, and any tax credits you qualify for.

What is the difference between marginal and effective tax rate?

Your marginal tax rate is the rate applied to your last dollar of income. If you earn $95,000 as a single filer in 2025, your marginal rate is 22%. Your effective tax rate is the total tax divided by total income, which accounts for the lower rates on earlier portions of income. At $95,000, the effective rate is approximately 15.6%. The marginal rate determines how much tax you pay on additional income, such as a raise or bonus.

Do all states have income tax?

No. Nine states have no state income tax on wages: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. New Hampshire and Washington have limited taxes on investment income and capital gains respectively, but not on wages. Moving between states can significantly change your take-home pay.

What is FICA and why is it on my pay stub?

FICA stands for Federal Insurance Contributions Act. It funds Social Security (6.2% of your wages up to $176,100 in 2025) and Medicare (1.45% of all wages, with an additional 0.9% on wages above $200,000). Your employer pays a matching amount. FICA is separate from federal income tax and appears as its own line item on your pay stub.

How do pre-tax deductions reduce my taxes?

Pre-tax deductions are subtracted from your gross pay before taxes are calculated. A $500 per month 401(k) contribution reduces your taxable income by $6,000 per year. If you are in the 22% federal bracket and pay 5% state tax, that $6,000 deduction saves you $1,620 in taxes annually. Common pre-tax deductions include 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums, HSA contributions, and FSA contributions.

What should I put on my W-4 form?

The W-4 tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold. If you have one job with no dependents, just completing Step 1 (filing status) and signing is often sufficient. For two-income households, use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov. For dependents, enter $2,000 per qualifying child under 17 in Step 3. If you have significant non-wage income, enter it in Step 4(a).

Why is my paycheck different from what I expected based on my salary?

Your gross pay is divided by pay periods (24 for semi-monthly, 26 for bi-weekly). From each check, federal income tax, state income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and voluntary deductions are subtracted. A $60,000 salary paid bi-weekly has a gross of $2,307.69. After typical deductions, take-home might be $1,700 to $1,850 depending on your state, filing status, and elections.

Do freelancers pay more in taxes than employees?

Freelancers pay self-employment tax of 15.3% (both employer and employee portions of FICA) on net earnings, compared to 7.65% for employees. However, freelancers can deduct the employer-equivalent portion (7.65%) from adjusted gross income, and they can deduct business expenses before calculating self-employment tax. A freelancer earning $80,000 with $15,000 in deductions pays self-employment tax on $65,000.

What happens if I do not withhold enough tax during the year?

If you owe more than $1,000 at tax time, the IRS may charge an underpayment penalty. The penalty rate is the federal short-term rate plus 3 percentage points, applied quarterly to the underpaid amount. To avoid the penalty, pay at least 90% of current year tax or 100% of last year's tax (110% if AGI exceeds $150,000) through withholding or estimated payments.

How do bonuses get taxed?

Bonuses can be withheld using the flat rate method (22% federal, 37% above $1 million) or the aggregate method (combined with regular pay for higher withholding). At tax time, bonuses are taxed as ordinary income at your marginal rate. If excess tax was withheld from the bonus, you get a refund. The withholding method does not change your actual tax liability.

Is a large tax refund a good thing?

A large refund means you overpaid taxes throughout the year, giving the government an interest-free loan. A $3,000 refund is $250 per month that could have been in your paycheck earning interest. The ideal refund is close to zero. Adjust your W-4 to reduce over-withholding and increase your monthly take-home pay.
