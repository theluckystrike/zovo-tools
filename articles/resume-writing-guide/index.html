How to Build a Resume That Gets Interviews in 2026

By Michael Lip / March 20, 2026 / 23 min read

I have reviewed roughly 2,000 resumes over the past decade, both as a hiring participant at technology companies and as someone friends and colleagues routinely ask for feedback. The patterns that separate resumes that generate interviews from those that disappear into the void are remarkably consistent.

The gap is not about talent. Many highly qualified people submit resumes that get them rejected before a human being reads a single line. The resume either fails an automated filter, fails to communicate relevant value in 6 seconds of scanning, or commits one of several common formatting errors that signal carelessness.

This guide covers the structural, tactical, and strategic elements of building a resume that actually works in the current hiring environment. Every recommendation is based on how recruiters, hiring managers, and applicant tracking systems process resumes today, not ten years ago.

Quick Facts

  • Recruiters spend an average of 6-7 seconds on initial resume screening (Ladders eye-tracking study)
  • Over 98% of Fortune 500 companies use Applicant Tracking Systems (ATS)
  • Resumes tailored to specific job descriptions are 30-50% more likely to generate interviews
  • Quantified achievements are 40% more memorable than generic job descriptions
  • The average corporate job posting receives 250 applications

A resume (also spelled resumé) is a document created and used by a person to present their background, skills, and accomplishments. Resumes can be used for a variety of reasons, but most often they are used to secure new employment. A typical resume contains a summary of relevant job experience and education. The resume is usually one of the first items, along with a cover letter, that a potential employer sees regarding the job seeker.

Resume Formats and When to Use Each

There are three standard resume formats, and choosing the right one depends on your career trajectory, the role you are pursuing, and how your experience maps to the job description.

Chronological (Reverse-Chronological)

This is the most common format and the one most recruiters and ATS software expect. Work experience is listed in reverse chronological order, most recent position first. Each position includes the company name, your title, dates of employment, and bullet points describing your achievements and responsibilities.

Use chronological format when you have a steady work history with clear career progression, when you are staying in the same industry or function, or when your most recent experience is your most relevant. This format is the default choice. Use it unless you have a specific reason not to.

Functional (Skills-Based)

The functional format organizes your resume around skill categories rather than job history. Instead of listing positions chronologically, you group achievements under headings like "Project Management," "Data Analysis," or "Client Relations."

This format is sometimes recommended for career changers or people with employment gaps. In practice, I advise against it for most situations. Recruiters are skeptical of functional resumes because they obscure your career timeline. Many assume you are hiding something (gaps, job-hopping, lack of progression). ATS software also struggles with functional formats because it expects job titles, company names, and dates in a standard order.

The only scenario where functional format works well is when you genuinely have no relevant work history (fresh graduates, complete career changers) and your transferable skills are the strongest argument for your candidacy.

Combination (Hybrid)

The combination format leads with a skills summary section followed by a chronological work history. It gives you space to highlight transferable skills while still providing the timeline recruiters expect.

Use combination format when you are changing industries but have relevant transferable skills, when you have specialized technical skills that deserve prominent placement, or when you want to emphasize capabilities alongside experience. This is often the best compromise for experienced professionals who are pivoting.

What Recruiters Actually Look For

Eye-tracking studies have mapped exactly where recruiters look during that initial 6-7 second scan. The results are consistent across multiple studies. Recruiters follow an F-shaped pattern, scanning the top of the resume horizontally, then moving down the left side.

In those first seconds, they look at your current or most recent job title, the company name, the dates of employment, your previous job title and company, and your education. That is it. Five data points in six seconds. Everything else comes later, if they decide to read further.

This means the top third of your resume is the most valuable real estate. Your name, professional summary, and most recent position must be compelling, relevant, and scannable at a glance. If a recruiter cannot determine within seconds whether you are a potential fit, they move to the next resume.

After the initial scan, recruiters who continue reading look for specific achievements that demonstrate impact, keywords that match the job description, career progression (are you growing or lateral?), and red flags (gaps, demotions, very short tenures).

I have watched hiring managers sort 50 resumes into yes, maybe, and no piles in under 15 minutes. That is roughly 18 seconds per resume. The "yes" pile contains resumes where the relevant information jumped off the page. The "no" pile contains resumes that required effort to parse, regardless of the candidate's actual qualifications.

ATS Optimization

An Applicant Tracking System is software that manages the hiring pipeline. Companies use it to post jobs, collect applications, screen candidates, and track them through the interview process. The screening function is what matters for your resume.

When you apply online, the ATS parses your resume into a structured database. It extracts your name, contact information, work history, education, and skills. It then compares your parsed profile against the job requirements to generate a match score. Resumes below a certain score threshold may never be seen by a human.

Here is how to ensure your resume passes ATS screening.

Use standard section headings. The ATS looks for "Work Experience" or "Professional Experience," "Education," "Skills," and "Summary" or "Profile." Creative headings like "Where I Have Made an Impact" or "My Journey" confuse the parser.

Use a simple, single-column layout. Multi-column designs, text boxes, tables (for layout purposes), headers, footers, and sidebars can cause parsing errors. The ATS may read columns in the wrong order or skip content entirely.

Include keywords from the job description. If the posting mentions "project management," "Agile methodology," and "stakeholder communication," those phrases should appear in your resume if they are truthful. Use the exact phrasing from the job description when possible. If they say "data analysis," do not substitute "data analytics" unless you include both.

Use standard fonts and formatting. Stick with Calibri, Arial, Garamond, or Times New Roman at 10-12 point. Use standard bullet characters. Avoid images, graphics, charts, icons, and logos. These elements are invisible to most ATS software.

Save as PDF or .docx. PDF preserves formatting and is parsed well by modern ATS software. Some older systems prefer .docx. When in doubt, check if the application portal specifies a format. Name the file professionally: FirstName-LastName-Resume.pdf.

Do not stuff keywords. ATS software has evolved beyond simple keyword counting. Modern systems evaluate context. Listing "project management" 15 times in a skills section without supporting experience is flagged as keyword stuffing. Instead, use keywords naturally within your achievement descriptions.

Action Verbs That Work

Every bullet point on your resume should start with a strong action verb. The verb sets the tone for the entire bullet and signals what type of contribution you made. Weak verbs diminish strong achievements. Strong verbs elevate even modest contributions.

Here are the most effective action verbs organized by the type of contribution they signal.

CategoryVerbs
LeadershipDirected, Led, Orchestrated, Spearheaded, Championed, Mobilized, Steered
AchievementDelivered, Achieved, Exceeded, Surpassed, Earned, Captured, Won
CreationBuilt, Designed, Developed, Launched, Established, Pioneered, Introduced
ImprovementStreamlined, Optimized, Accelerated, Strengthened, Revamped, Transformed
AnalysisIdentified, Diagnosed, Evaluated, Assessed, Investigated, Mapped, Uncovered
Revenue / GrowthGenerated, Grew, Expanded, Increased, Scaled, Amplified, Doubled
Efficiency / CostReduced, Eliminated, Consolidated, Automated, Simplified, Cut
CollaborationPartnered, Coordinated, Aligned, Facilitated, Unified, Integrated

Avoid passive or vague verbs: "Responsible for," "Assisted with," "Helped," "Participated in," "Handled," "Worked on." These do not convey ownership or impact. Compare the difference:

Weak

Responsible for managing a team of 8 customer service representatives.

Strong

Led a team of 8 customer service representatives, reducing average response time from 24 hours to 4 hours and improving customer satisfaction scores by 31%.

The strong version uses an active verb, includes a specific team size, and quantifies two outcomes. It tells the reader exactly what you did and what happened as a result.

Quantifying Achievements With Numbers

Numbers are the most powerful tool on a resume. They transform vague claims into concrete evidence. A resume full of quantified achievements reads like a track record. A resume without numbers reads like a job description.

Every role has measurable outcomes. The challenge is identifying and framing them. Here are the categories of numbers you should look for in every position you have held.

Revenue and growth numbers: sales generated, revenue increased, deals closed, new accounts acquired, market share gained. If you directly influenced revenue, state the amount or percentage.

Efficiency and cost numbers: time saved, costs reduced, processes streamlined, error rates decreased. If you made something faster, cheaper, or more reliable, quantify the improvement.

Scale numbers: team size managed, budget controlled, projects delivered, users served, transactions processed. These establish the scope of your responsibility.

Achievement numbers: rankings, awards, performance percentages, goals exceeded. "Ranked #2 of 45 sales representatives" communicates more than "top performer."

Without Numbers

Improved the onboarding process for new employees.

With Numbers

Redesigned the new employee onboarding program, reducing time-to-productivity from 6 weeks to 3 weeks across 120 annual hires, saving an estimated $340,000 in training costs annually.

If you do not have exact numbers, estimate conservatively and use qualifying language: "approximately," "over," "nearly." An estimated number is infinitely more useful than no number. "Managed a budget of approximately $2M" is stronger than "Managed a large budget."

Use a Word Counter to ensure your bullet points are concise. The ideal bullet length is 1-2 lines. If a bullet exceeds 3 lines, it likely contains multiple achievements that should be split.

Building the Professional Summary

The professional summary replaces the outdated objective statement. It sits at the top of your resume, directly below your contact information, and occupies the most-read section of the entire document.

A good summary is 2-4 sentences. It includes your professional identity (title and years of experience), your area of expertise or specialization, one to two headline achievements with numbers, and the value you bring to the target role.

Example Summary for a Marketing Manager

Digital marketing manager with 7 years of experience in B2B SaaS, specializing in demand generation and content strategy. Grew organic traffic by 215% and generated $4.2M in pipeline at a Series B startup. Experienced in HubSpot, Google Analytics, and cross-functional campaign execution across sales and product teams.

Example Summary for a Software Engineer

Full-stack engineer with 5 years of experience building production applications in React, Node.js, and PostgreSQL. Led the migration of a monolithic application to microservices architecture, reducing deployment time by 80% and improving system uptime to 99.97%. Contributed to open-source projects with over 2,400 GitHub stars collectively.

Example Summary for an Operations Director

Operations director with 12 years of experience in logistics and supply chain management across manufacturing and retail. Directed a $45M annual budget and a team of 85, delivering 18% cost reduction over three years while maintaining 99.2% order fulfillment accuracy. Certified Six Sigma Black Belt with expertise in Lean methodology and ERP implementation.

Notice that each summary is specific to the person and the type of role they are targeting. Generic summaries like "Motivated professional seeking a challenging role in a dynamic company" communicate nothing and waste premium space.

Tailor your summary for each application. Pull 2-3 key requirements from the job description and ensure they appear naturally in your summary.

Common Resume Mistakes

After reviewing thousands of resumes, these are the errors I see most frequently. Each one reduces interview rates independently, and they compound when multiple mistakes are present on the same resume.

Listing responsibilities instead of achievements. "Managed social media accounts" is a responsibility. "Grew Instagram following from 12,000 to 89,000 in 14 months, generating 340 qualified leads through organic content" is an achievement. Employers know what the job involves. They want to know how well you did it.

Using the same resume for every application. The average corporate posting receives 250 applications. If your resume is not tailored to the specific role, it will be outranked by tailored resumes from equally qualified candidates. Customization does not mean rewriting from scratch. It means adjusting keywords, reordering bullet points, and aligning your summary with the job description.

Including irrelevant experience. Your summer job at a pizza restaurant 15 years ago does not belong on a resume for a financial analyst position. Every line on your resume should support your candidacy for the target role. Space spent on irrelevant history is space not available for relevant achievements.

Typos and grammatical errors. A single typo can be disqualifying, particularly for roles that involve writing, communication, or attention to detail. Read your resume out loud. Have someone else read it. Run it through a proofreading tool. Then read it again.

An unprofessional email address. partyanimal99@hotmail.com undermines a otherwise strong resume. Use a simple email: firstname.lastname@gmail.com or a personal domain.

Burying key information. If your most impressive achievement is the fourth bullet under your second-to-last job, most readers will never see it. Put your strongest, most relevant content first, in every section.

Including a photo. In the United States, resume photos are not expected and can introduce bias. In some other countries (Germany, parts of Asia), they are standard. Know the norms for the market you are applying in.

Overdesigning. Heavy graphics, unusual fonts, and creative layouts may look attractive but often fail ATS parsing and can distract from content. Clean, readable formatting with clear hierarchy serves you better than visual flair.

Industry-Specific Resume Tips

While the fundamentals apply everywhere, certain industries have specific expectations and priorities.

Technology and Software Engineering

Include a technical skills section listing programming languages, frameworks, tools, and platforms. Order them by proficiency and relevance. Include links to your GitHub profile, portfolio, or significant open-source contributions. Quantify with metrics like uptime percentages, load times improved, users served, or deployment frequency. For senior roles, emphasize architecture decisions and their business impact, not just coding ability.

Finance and Accounting

Certifications matter: CPA, CFA, Series 7/66. List them prominently. Quantify with dollar amounts: budgets managed, revenue impacted, costs reduced, portfolios sized. Demonstrate knowledge of relevant regulatory frameworks (SOX, GAAP, IFRS). For client-facing roles, include client acquisition and retention metrics.

Healthcare

Licenses and certifications are mandatory. Include your license numbers and states where you are credentialed. Quantify with patient volumes, outcomes improved, wait times reduced, or compliance scores. HIPAA awareness and patient safety metrics are valued. For research roles, include publication counts and grant amounts.

Sales

This is the most numbers-driven resume category. Include quota attainment percentages, revenue generated, deal sizes, pipeline growth, close rates, and rankings. Sales resumes without numbers are immediately weak. "Exceeded annual quota by 127%, generating $3.8M in new business across 47 enterprise accounts" is the standard expectation for bullet points.

Creative and Design

Your resume should demonstrate design sensibility while remaining ATS-compatible. Link to an online portfolio (mandatory). Quantify where possible: campaigns delivered, user engagement improvements, brand awareness metrics. Include proficiency in specific tools (Figma, Adobe Creative Suite, Sketch). For leadership roles, include team sizes and project budgets.

Education

Include certifications, endorsements, and grade levels/subjects taught. Quantify with student performance improvements, curriculum developments, test score improvements, and parent satisfaction ratings. Professional development hours and continuing education demonstrate commitment to growth.

The Skills Section

A well-organized skills section serves two purposes: it gives the ATS additional keywords to match, and it gives human readers a quick overview of your capabilities.

Organize skills into categories. For a marketing professional, this might be "Analytics" (Google Analytics, Mixpanel, Looker), "Advertising" (Google Ads, Meta Ads, LinkedIn Ads), "Content" (SEO, content strategy, copywriting), and "Tools" (HubSpot, Salesforce, Asana).

Only include skills you can discuss confidently in an interview. Listing a skill you barely know is worse than omitting it, because interview questions will expose the gap.

Prioritize hard skills over soft skills in this section. "Python," "financial modeling," and "Salesforce administration" are verifiable and specific. "Team player," "hard worker," and "excellent communicator" are subjective and better demonstrated through your achievement bullet points than listed as standalone skills.

Update this section for every application to reflect the specific skills mentioned in the job posting. If the job requires "Tableau" and you have experience with it, make sure it appears in your skills section even if you normally list a different visualization tool first.

Education and Certifications

For most professionals with 5+ years of experience, education moves to the bottom of the resume. Your work experience is more relevant and should have priority placement. For recent graduates or career changers, education may be positioned higher.

Include the degree, institution, graduation year (optional if it reveals age and you are concerned about bias), relevant coursework (for recent graduates targeting specific roles), academic honors (cum laude, Dean's List), and GPA only if it is above 3.5 and you graduated within the past 3 years.

Certifications deserve their own section or a line within education. Include the certification name, issuing organization, and date earned. In-progress certifications should be noted: "PMP Certification (expected June 2026)."

Online courses and bootcamps are increasingly accepted, particularly in technology. Include them when they are relevant to the target role and come from recognized platforms or institutions. A Google Data Analytics Certificate or an AWS Solutions Architect certification carries real weight.

Cover Letter Essentials

A cover letter is not a repeat of your resume in paragraph form. It is a targeted argument for why you are the right person for this specific role at this specific company.

The opening paragraph should name the role you are applying for and include one compelling hook: a shared connection, a notable achievement relevant to the company's needs, or a specific reason you are excited about this opportunity. Avoid generic openings.

Weak Opening

I am writing to express my interest in the Marketing Manager position at your company. I believe my skills and experience make me a strong candidate.

Strong Opening

When I saw that Meridian Health was hiring a Marketing Manager to lead their patient acquisition strategy, I recognized the exact challenge I solved at my current company, where I built a digital marketing program that grew new patient registrations by 62% in 18 months.

The body (1-2 paragraphs) should connect your most relevant experience to the company's stated needs. Pull 2-3 requirements from the job posting and address each with a specific example from your background. This is where you tell stories that do not fit in resume bullet points: the context of a challenge, your approach, and the quantified result.

The closing paragraph should restate your enthusiasm, mention your availability for an interview, and thank the reader. Keep it brief.

Total length: one page, 3-4 paragraphs, 250-400 words. A Cover Letter Generator can help you structure the content and ensure you hit the right tone and length.

Formatting and Layout Fundamentals

The visual presentation of your resume affects readability, ATS compatibility, and the impression you make. Here are the formatting rules that consistently produce the best results.

Margins: 0.5 to 1 inch on all sides. Narrower margins (0.5 inch) allow more content on one page. Wider margins (1 inch) improve readability. I default to 0.75 inch.

Font size: 10-12 point for body text. 14-16 point for your name. 11-12 point for section headings. Do not go below 10 point; it strains readability.

Font choice: Calibri, Arial, Garamond, Cambria, or Helvetica. These are clean, professional, and universally supported. Avoid decorative fonts, script fonts, or anything that requires the reader to squint.

Line spacing: 1.0 to 1.15 for body text. Add 6-12 points of space between sections. This creates visual separation without wasting space.

Section order for chronological format: Name and contact, Professional summary, Work experience, Education, Skills, Certifications (if applicable). For combination format, move Skills above Work Experience.

Contact information: Full name, city and state (full address is no longer necessary), phone number, professional email, LinkedIn URL (customized, not the default string of numbers), and portfolio URL if applicable.

Consistency: If you put a period at the end of one bullet, put a period at the end of all bullets. If you abbreviate months in one date, abbreviate them everywhere. Inconsistency signals carelessness.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long should a resume be?

One page for professionals with under 10 years of experience. Two pages for senior professionals or those with extensive relevant experience. Three pages only in academic or scientific fields where publications and grants are standard. Recruiters spend 6-7 seconds on initial screening. Concise, targeted content respects that reality.

Should I include an objective statement?

Objective statements are outdated. Replace them with a professional summary of 2-3 sentences highlighting your most relevant experience, key skills, and what you bring to the role. A good summary reads like an elevator pitch focused on the value you offer, not what you want.

What is an ATS and why does it matter?

An ATS (Applicant Tracking System) is software that collects, sorts, and filters job applications. Over 98% of Fortune 500 companies use one. It scans resumes for keywords matching the job description and filters out candidates who do not meet minimum criteria. If your resume is not ATS-compatible, it may be rejected before a human sees it.

Should I use a resume template?

Templates are fine starting points, but avoid designs with graphics, columns, text boxes, headers with key info, or unusual fonts. These break ATS parsing. Use a clean, single-column layout with standard section headings and a standard font. A Resume Builder creates ATS-friendly formats automatically.

How far back should my work history go?

10-15 years or the last 3-4 positions, whichever is more relevant. Older positions can be summarized in one line or omitted. Including very old positions can inadvertently reveal age and introduce bias. Focus on the most recent and relevant experience.

Do I need a different resume for every application?

Yes. Tailor the professional summary, adjust keywords to match the job description, reorder bullets to put the most relevant first, and emphasize skills from the posting. Tailored resumes increase interview rates by 30-50%. Keep a master resume and create targeted versions for each application.

Should I include references?

No. Do not include references or "References available upon request." Both waste space. Prepare a separate reference sheet with 3-5 professional contacts, their titles, and contact info. Provide it when requested during the interview process.

What file format should I submit?

PDF is preferred in most cases because it preserves formatting. Some older ATS software parses .docx more reliably. Follow the job posting's instructions if a format is specified. Name the file professionally: FirstName-LastName-Resume.pdf.

How do I handle employment gaps?

Be honest but strategic. Include productive gap time (caregiving, education, freelancing) as a line item. For gaps under 6 months, using years only instead of month/year often makes them invisible. For longer gaps, a brief cover letter explanation is better than leaving readers to guess. Honest gaps create less suspicion than unexplained ones.

Is a cover letter still necessary?

If the application accepts one, yes. Hiring managers consistently report that good cover letters distinguish candidates, especially for career transitions or when the resume alone does not tell the full story. Keep it to 3-4 paragraphs on one page. A Cover Letter Generator helps structure the content.

What are the biggest resume mistakes?

Using generic job descriptions instead of specific achievements, not quantifying results, submitting the same resume everywhere, including irrelevant experience, using unprofessional email addresses, having typos, using ATS-breaking formatting, and making the resume too long. Each reduces your chances individually, and they compound.

Should I include hobbies and interests?

Only if directly relevant to the role or demonstrating transferable skills. Leading a competitive robotics team adds value for an engineering role. "Reading, hiking, cooking" does not. For entry-level candidates with limited work experience, relevant extracurriculars can fill space. For experienced professionals, the space is better used for achievements.
