Wikipedia Definition

Compound interest is the addition of interest to the principal sum of a loan or deposit, or in other words, interest on principal plus interest. It is the result of reinvesting or retaining interest that would otherwise be paid out, so that interest in the next period is then earned on the principal sum plus previously accumulated interest.

1. Simple Interest vs. Compound Interest

The difference between simple and compound interest is the single most important concept in personal finance. Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus all interest earned so far.

Here is a concrete example. You invest $10,000 at 5% annual interest for 20 years:

YearSimple Interest BalanceCompound Interest BalanceDifference
0$10,000$10,000$0
5$12,500$12,763$263
10$15,000$16,289$1,289
15$17,500$20,789$3,289
20$20,000$26,533$6,533
30$25,000$43,219$18,219
40$30,000$70,400$40,400

With simple interest, you earn exactly $500 every year ($10,000 x 0.05). The growth is linear. With compound interest, you earn $500 in year one, then $525 in year two (because your balance is now $10,500), then $551.25 in year three, and so on. Each year the interest earned increases because the base amount is larger.

After 20 years, compound interest has given you an extra $6,533. After 40 years, the extra is $40,400. The longer the time period, the more dramatic the compounding effect becomes. This is why financial advisors consistently emphasize starting to invest early.

2. The Compound Interest Formula

The standard formula for compound interest is:

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)

Where:
A = Final amount (principal + interest)
P = Principal (initial investment)
r = Annual interest rate (as a decimal, so 5% = 0.05)
n = Number of compounding periods per year
t = Number of years

Breaking Down Each Variable

The principal (P) is your starting investment. The interest rate (r) is expressed as a decimal. The compounding frequency (n) determines how often interest is calculated and added to the balance:

  • Annual compounding: n = 1
  • Semi-annual compounding: n = 2
  • Quarterly compounding: n = 4
  • Monthly compounding: n = 12
  • Daily compounding: n = 365

For continuous compounding (the mathematical limit as n approaches infinity), the formula simplifies to:

A = P * e^(rt)

Where e is Euler's number (approximately 2.71828)

Worked Example

You invest $5,000 at 6% interest compounded monthly for 15 years:

A = 5000(1 + 0.06/12)^(12 * 15)
A = 5000(1 + 0.005)^(180)
A = 5000(1.005)^180
A = 5000 * 2.4541
A = $12,270.47

Your $5,000 has grown to $12,270.47. The total interest earned is $7,270.47, which is 145% of your original investment.

3. The Rule of 72

The Rule of 72 is a mental math shortcut for estimating how long it takes an investment to double. Divide 72 by the annual interest rate to get the approximate number of years.

Interest RateYears to Double (Rule of 72)Actual Years to DoubleError
2%36.035.0+2.9%
4%18.017.7+1.7%
6%12.011.9+0.8%
8%9.09.0+0.0%
10%7.27.3-1.4%
12%6.06.1-1.6%
15%4.85.0-4.0%

The rule is most accurate around 8% and becomes less precise at very high or very low rates. For rates between 2% and 12%, the error is under 2%, making it a reliable back-of-the-envelope tool.

You can also use the rule to estimate the effect of fees. A mutual fund charging 1% in annual fees on a 7% return means your effective return is 6%. At 7%, money doubles in 10.3 years. At 6%, it doubles in 12 years. That 1% fee costs you nearly 2 extra years per doubling over the life of the investment.

More frequent compounding produces higher returns, but the marginal benefit decreases rapidly. Here is $10,000 at 5% interest over 10 years at different frequencies:

This matters when comparing savings accounts. A bank offering 5% compounded daily versus 5% compounded monthly provides a negligible difference. Focus on the interest rate itself, not the compounding frequency.

5. The Power of Regular Contributions

Compound interest becomes truly powerful when combined with regular contributions. The formula for future value with regular payments is:

FV = P(1 + r/n)^(nt) + PMT * [((1 + r/n)^(nt) - 1) / (r/n)]

Where PMT = regular payment amount

Consider three scenarios, all starting with $0 and investing at 7% annual return for 30 years:

Monthly ContributionTotal ContributedFinal BalanceInterest Earned

$500$180,000$609,985$429,985

At $500/month for 30 years, you contribute $180,000 of your own money, but compound interest adds $429,985 in earnings. The interest earned is 2.4 times your total contributions. At $1,000/month, you become a millionaire, with $860,000 of the final balance coming from compounding alone.

6. The Early Start Advantage

The following comparison illustrates why starting early matters more than contributing more:

Person A starts investing $200/month at age 22, stops at age 32 (10 years, $24,000 total invested), and lets the money grow untouched until age 62. Person B starts investing $200/month at age 32 and continues until age 62 (30 years, $72,000 total invested). Both earn 7% annually.

Person A invested one-third the money and stopped 30 years earlier, yet ends up with $20,018 more at retirement. The 10 extra years of compounding time overcome the 20 extra years of contributions. This is the most compelling argument for starting to invest as early as possible, even with small amounts.

7. Compound Interest in Retirement Accounts

401(k) Plans

A 401(k) is a tax-advantaged retirement account offered by employers. Contributions reduce your taxable income, and investments grow tax-deferred until withdrawal. The 2026 contribution limit is $23,500, with an additional $7,500 catch-up contribution for those aged 50 and older.

Many employers match contributions, typically 50-100% of the first 3-6% of salary. An employer match is a guaranteed 50-100% return on your contribution, making it the single highest-return investment available. If your employer matches 50% of the first 6% and you earn $60,000, contributing 6% ($3,600/year) gets you $1,800 in free money annually.

Roth IRA

A Roth IRA accepts after-tax contributions, but all growth and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. The 2026 contribution limit is $7,000 ($8,000 for those 50+). For someone in their 20s or 30s who expects their tax rate to be higher in retirement, a Roth IRA can be more advantageous than a traditional IRA because decades of compound growth escape taxation entirely.

The Tax-Free Compounding Effect

In a taxable account earning 7% annually, if you are in the 24% federal tax bracket, your after-tax return is approximately 5.3% (assuming all gains are realized annually). In a tax-deferred account (401k, traditional IRA), you earn the full 7% until withdrawal. In a Roth IRA, you earn 7% and never pay tax on the growth.

8. Compound Interest Working Against You: Debt

Compound interest works identically on debt, but in the opposite direction. When you carry a balance on a credit card at 20% APR, you are paying interest on interest every month.

Credit Card Debt Example

A $5,000 credit card balance at 20% APR with a minimum payment of 2% of the balance (minimum $25):

  • Time to pay off: 28 years and 5 months
  • Total interest paid: $8,397
  • Total amount paid: $13,397 (nearly 2.7 times the original balance)

If instead you pay $200/month fixed:

  • Time to pay off: 2 years and 8 months
  • Total interest paid: $1,397
  • Total amount paid: $6,397

Paying $200/month instead of the minimum saves $7,000 in interest and 25 years of payments.

Student Loan Compounding

Federal student loans accrue interest daily. For unsubsidized loans, interest accrues during school and capitalizes (adds to the principal) when you enter repayment. A $30,000 loan at 6.53% (the 2025-2026 rate for undergraduate loans) accruing for 4 years of school adds approximately $7,836 in capitalized interest before you make a single payment, raising your effective principal to $37,836.

9. CAGR: Compound Annual Growth Rate

CAGR (Compound Annual Growth Rate) measures the average annual growth rate of an investment over a specified period, assuming profits are reinvested. It smooths out yearly volatility to show what the equivalent steady rate would have been.

CAGR = (Ending Value / Beginning Value)^(1/n) - 1

Where n = number of years

If you invested $10,000 and it grew to $25,000 over 10 years:

CAGR = (25000 / 10000)^(1/10) - 1
CAGR = (2.5)^(0.1) - 1
CAGR = 1.0960 - 1
CAGR = 0.096 = 9.6%

The investment grew at an average compound rate of 9.6% per year, even though individual years may have varied wildly. The S&P 500's CAGR from 1926 to 2025 is approximately 10.3%, despite including the Great Depression, 2008 financial crisis, and COVID-19 crash.

The differences are staggering. At 5%, $10,000 becomes $43,219 in 30 years. At 10%, it becomes $174,494. The 5 percentage point difference results in a 4x difference in final value. This is why investment fees (which directly reduce your return rate) matter so much over long periods.

The Fee Drag Effect

Consider two index funds tracking the same benchmark, one with a 0.03% expense ratio and one with a 1.0% expense ratio. On a $10,000 investment earning 7% gross for 30 years:

  • 0.03% fee fund: $74,908 (6.97% net return)
  • 1.0% fee fund: $57,435 (6.0% net return)
  • Cost of the higher fee: $17,473 (23% of the low-fee balance)

A 0.97% difference in annual fees costs you $17,473 over 30 years on a single $10,000 investment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods. When you earn 5% on $10,000 in year one ($500), in year two you earn 5% on $10,500 ($525). Each period builds on the last, creating an exponential growth curve rather than a linear one.

What is the compound interest formula?

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), where A is the final amount, P is principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is compounding periods per year, and t is years. For monthly compounding of $10,000 at 5% for 10 years: A = 10000(1 + 0.05/12)^(120) = $16,470.09.

How often should interest compound?

More frequent compounding is better, but the difference between monthly and daily is minimal. For $10,000 at 5% for 10 years, monthly compounding yields $16,470 and daily yields $16,487. The $17 difference is negligible. Focus on the interest rate rather than the compounding frequency when comparing accounts.

What is the Rule of 72?

Divide 72 by the annual interest rate to estimate doubling time. At 6%: 72/6 = 12 years. At 8%: 72/8 = 9 years. At 10%: 72/10 = 7.2 years. The rule is most accurate between 2-12% and provides a quick mental calculation for investment planning without needing a calculator.

How much does $10,000 grow over 30 years?

At 7% (near the inflation-adjusted S&P 500 average): $76,123. At 10% (nominal S&P 500 average): $174,494. At 5% (balanced portfolio): $43,219. Adding $200/month in contributions at 7% brings the 30-year total to approximately $283,382, with $208,259 of that coming from compounding.

What is the difference between compound and simple interest?

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. Compound interest is calculated on principal plus accumulated interest. Over 20 years at 5%, $10,000 becomes $20,000 with simple interest and $26,533 with compound interest. The gap grows larger with higher rates and longer time periods.

Does compound interest work against me with debt?

Yes. Credit card debt at 20% APR compounds monthly, meaning you pay interest on unpaid interest. A $5,000 balance with minimum payments takes 28+ years and $13,397 total to pay off. Paying a fixed $200/month reduces this to under 3 years and $6,397 total, saving over $7,000 in interest charges.

When should I start investing?

As early as possible. Due to compounding, a person investing $200/month from age 22 to 32 (then stopping) accumulates more by age 62 than someone investing $200/month from age 32 to 62. The 10 extra years of compounding outweigh 20 extra years of contributions. Even small monthly amounts benefit from early starts.
