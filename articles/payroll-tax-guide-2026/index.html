How Payroll Taxes Work: Federal, State, and FICA Explained

Payroll taxes are the single largest deduction from most Americans' paychecks, yet the mechanics behind them remain confusing for employees and employers alike. The gap between gross pay and net pay is driven by a combination of federal income tax withholding, Social Security tax, Medicare tax, and state income tax. Each of these is calculated differently, has different caps and thresholds, and follows different rules for who pays what.

This guide breaks down every component of payroll taxation for 2026, explains how each calculation works with real numbers, covers the employer side of the equation, and addresses the practical questions that come up every pay period. The Components of Payroll Tax

Every paycheck involves up to five separate tax calculations running simultaneously. Understanding each one individually is the key to understanding why your take-home pay is what it is.

Federal income tax withholding is calculated using IRS tax tables based on your W-4 filing status, your gross pay for the period, and any adjustments you have claimed. This is the progressive tax that uses brackets.

Social Security tax (officially called Old-Age, Survivors, and Disability Insurance or OASDI) is a flat 6.2% of gross wages up to the annual wage base limit. For 2026, that limit is $176,100. Every dollar you earn beyond that is exempt from Social Security tax.

Medicare tax is a flat 1.45% of all gross wages with no cap. An additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies to individual wages exceeding $200,000 per year ($250,000 for married filing jointly). This additional tax is paid by the employee only.

State income tax varies dramatically by state. Some states use flat rates, others use progressive brackets, and nine states charge no income tax on wages at all.

Local income tax applies in certain cities and counties. Notable examples include New York City (3.078% to 3.876%), Philadelphia (3.75% for residents), and various Ohio municipalities.

FICA Tax Breakdown

FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act. It funds Social Security and Medicare. FICA is not optional, cannot be reduced by deductions or credits (with limited exceptions), and applies starting from dollar one of wages.

Employee FICA rate: 6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare = 7.65%

Employer FICA rate: 6.2% Social Security + 1.45% Medicare = 7.65%

Combined FICA rate: 12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare = 15.3%

2026 Social Security wage base: $176,100

Maximum employee Social Security tax: $176,100 x 6.2% = $10,918.20

Additional Medicare tax: 0.9% on wages over $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (MFJ)

Worked Example for an $85,000 Salary

An employee earning $85,000 per year, paid biweekly (26 pay periods), has a gross pay of $3,269.23 per paycheck. Here is the FICA calculation for each paycheck:

Social Security: $3,269.23 x 6.2% = $202.69

Medicare: $3,269.23 x 1.45% = $47.40

Total employee FICA per paycheck: $250.09

Annual employee FICA: $250.09 x 26 = $6,502.34

The employer pays an identical $6,502.34, bringing the total FICA cost to $13,004.68.

Since $85,000 is below the Social Security wage base of $176,100, the full salary is subject to Social Security tax. An employee earning $200,000 would stop paying Social Security tax after paycheck 14 (roughly early July), and would see higher take-home pay for the rest of the year.

Employer vs Employee Tax Obligations

Employees see their half of FICA and their income tax withholding on every pay stub. What they do not see is the employer's matching contribution, which is a significant additional cost of employment.

Understanding FUTA

The Federal Unemployment Tax Act (FUTA) funds the federal unemployment system. It is paid entirely by employers. The statutory rate is 6.0% on the first $7,000 of each employee's annual wages, but employers in states that maintain a conforming unemployment insurance program receive a credit of up to 5.4%, reducing the effective rate to 0.6%.

All 50 states qualify for the full 5.4% credit under normal circumstances, making the effective federal rate 0.6%. The maximum annual FUTA tax per employee is $42 ($7,000 x 0.6%). However, if a state has borrowed money from the federal unemployment trust fund and has not repaid it within two years, the credit is reduced, and employers in that state pay a higher effective rate. This is called a credit reduction state.

FUTA is reported on Form 940 annually, but deposits may be required quarterly if the cumulative liability exceeds $500.

State Income Tax Overview

State income tax systems fall into four categories. Understanding which category your state falls into helps you estimate your total tax burden.

No Income Tax States

Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming levy no state income tax on wages. New Hampshire taxes interest and dividend income but not wages. Tennessee phased out its Hall tax on investment income completely as of 2021. Employees in these states keep more of their gross pay but may face higher property or sales taxes.

Flat Tax States

These states tax all income at a single rate regardless of how much you earn. Examples include Colorado (4.4%), Illinois (4.95%), Indiana (3.05%), Massachusetts (5.0%), Michigan (4.25%), North Carolina (4.5%), Pennsylvania (3.07%), and Utah (4.65%). Flat tax states are simpler to calculate: multiply taxable income by the rate.

Progressive Tax States

Most states use progressive brackets similar to the federal system. California has the highest top rate at 13.3% on income over $1 million. New York ranges from 4% to 10.9%. New Jersey ranges from 1.4% to 10.75%. The number of brackets varies from three (Arizona) to thirteen (Hawaii).

Local Income Taxes

Some states allow cities and counties to impose their own income tax. Ohio has more than 600 municipalities with local income tax. New York City adds 3.078% to 3.876%. Philadelphia charges 3.75% for residents. These local taxes are in addition to state tax and must be accounted for in payroll processing.

How the W-4 Affects Withholding

The W-4 form tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. The current W-4 (redesigned in 2020) does not use numbered allowances. Instead, it asks for your filing status, whether you have multiple jobs, dependent information, other income, and additional deductions.

The five steps on the W-4 are:

Step 1. Filing status. Choose Single, Married Filing Jointly, or Head of Household. This determines which withholding table the employer uses.

Step 2. Multiple jobs or spouse works. If you have more than one job, or you are married and your spouse also works, you need to adjust withholding to avoid owing tax at year end. The IRS provides a worksheet and an online estimator for this step.

Step 3. Claim dependents. Enter the number of qualifying children (multiplied by $2,000) and other dependents (multiplied by $500). This reduces withholding to account for the child tax credit.

Step 4a. Other income. Enter income from interest, dividends, or side work that is not subject to withholding. This increases withholding to cover the tax on that income.

Step 4b. Deductions. If you plan to itemize deductions and they exceed the standard deduction, enter the excess here. This decreases withholding.

Step 4c. Extra withholding. A flat dollar amount to withhold from each paycheck in addition to the calculated amount. Use this as a catch-all to fine-tune your withholding.

The goal is to have your total withholding match your actual tax liability as closely as possible. Overwithholding means a large refund but less money in each paycheck. Underwithholding means more money per paycheck but potentially a tax bill (and possible penalties) in April.

Pre-Tax Deductions and Their Tax Impact

Pre-tax deductions reduce your taxable income before payroll taxes are calculated. The tax savings depend on which taxes the deduction is exempt from.

Traditional 401(k) Contributions

Traditional 401(k) contributions are exempt from federal and state income tax but are still subject to FICA (Social Security and Medicare). If you contribute $500 per paycheck to a 401(k) and your marginal tax rate is 22%, you save $110 in federal income tax per paycheck. But you still pay $38.25 in FICA on that $500 (7.65%). The 2026 401(k) contribution limit is $23,500, with an additional $7,500 catch-up for those aged 50 and over.

Section 125 Cafeteria Plan Deductions

Deductions under a Section 125 plan are exempt from both income tax and FICA. These include employer-sponsored health insurance premiums, dental and vision premiums, Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions through payroll, Flexible Spending Account (FSA) contributions, and dependent care FSA contributions.

A $600 monthly health insurance premium through a Section 125 plan saves an employee $45.90 per month in FICA taxes (7.65% of $600) plus their marginal income tax rate on $600. At the 22% bracket, total monthly savings are $45.90 + $132 = $177.90. Over a year, that is $2,134.80 in tax savings from the pre-tax treatment alone.

HSA Contributions

Health Savings Account contributions through payroll deduction are exempt from federal income tax, state income tax (except in California and New Jersey), and FICA taxes. The 2026 HSA contribution limits are $4,300 for individual coverage and $8,550 for family coverage, with an additional $1,000 catch-up for those 55 and older. HSAs offer a triple tax advantage: pre-tax contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for qualified medical expenses.

How Overtime Taxation Actually Works

A persistent myth holds that overtime is "taxed at a higher rate." This is incorrect. Overtime pay is taxed as ordinary income at your normal marginal rate. There is no special overtime tax rate.

The confusion arises from how payroll systems calculate withholding. Most payroll software uses the annualization method. It takes your gross pay for the current period, multiplies it by the number of pay periods in a year, and withholds tax as if that were your annual salary.

Normal biweekly paycheck: $2,500 gross

Annualized: $2,500 x 26 = $65,000. Withholding calculated for $65,000 salary.

Overtime biweekly paycheck: $3,800 gross (includes OT)

Annualized: $3,800 x 26 = $98,800. Withholding calculated for $98,800 salary.

The $98,800 annualized figure pushes more of the income into higher brackets, so the withholding rate for that single paycheck is higher. But when you file your annual return with actual total income of $65,000 plus some overtime weeks, the math corrects itself and the excess withholding comes back as a refund.

FICA taxes on overtime are straightforward. The same 7.65% rate applies regardless of whether the pay is regular or overtime. Social Security tax stops at the wage base limit regardless of whether you reach it through regular pay or overtime.

Payroll Processing Deadlines and Deposit Schedules

Employers must deposit withheld income tax and FICA taxes according to one of two schedules determined by the IRS based on the employer's prior reporting history.

Monthly Deposit Schedule

If your total tax liability in the lookback period (July 1 through June 30 of the preceding year) was $50,000 or less, you are a monthly depositor. Deposits are due by the 15th of the month following the month in which the wages were paid. January payroll taxes are due by February 15. If the 15th falls on a weekend or holiday, the deadline moves to the next business day.

Semi-Weekly Deposit Schedule

If your total tax liability in the lookback period exceeded $50,000, you are a semi-weekly depositor. If payday falls on Wednesday, Thursday, or Friday, the deposit is due the following Wednesday. If payday falls on Saturday, Sunday, Monday, or Tuesday, the deposit is due the following Friday.

The $100,000 Next-Day Rule

If you accumulate $100,000 or more in tax liability on any single day during a deposit period, you must deposit the tax by the next business day, regardless of your normal schedule. Once triggered, you become a semi-weekly depositor for the remainder of the calendar year and the following calendar year.

Annual Filing Requirements

Form 941 (Employer's Quarterly Federal Tax Return) is due by the last day of the month following the end of each quarter: April 30, July 31, October 31, and January 31. Form W-2 must be furnished to employees and filed with the Social Security Administration by January 31 of the following year. Form 940 (FUTA annual return) is due by January 31, or February 10 if all FUTA deposits were made on time.

Calculating a Complete Paycheck

Here is a step-by-step calculation for a single filer in Texas (no state income tax) earning $75,000 per year, paid biweekly, with a $200 per paycheck 401(k) contribution and $150 per paycheck for health insurance (Section 125).

Gross pay per period: $75,000 / 26 = $2,884.62

Section 125 deduction (health insurance): -$150.00

FICA taxable wages: $2,884.62 - $150.00 = $2,734.62

Social Security tax: $2,734.62 x 6.2% = $169.55

Medicare tax: $2,734.62 x 1.45% = $39.65

401(k) deduction: -$200.00

Federal taxable wages: $2,884.62 - $150.00 - $200.00 = $2,534.62

Federal income tax withholding (using 2026 tables, single, standard deduction): approximately $282.00

State income tax (Texas): $0.00

Net pay: $2,884.62 - $150.00 - $200.00 - $169.55 - $39.65 - $282.00 = $2,043.42

Of the $2,884.62 gross pay, the employee takes home $2,043.42, a difference of $841.20 or 29.2% of gross pay. The employer additionally pays $169.55 in Social Security, $39.65 in Medicare, and approximately $1.62 in FUTA (if still under the $7,000 wage base), for a total employer cost of about $3,095.44 per paycheck.

Self-Employment Payroll Tax

Self-employed individuals pay the full 15.3% FICA tax (both the employee and employer portions) through the self-employment tax on Schedule SE. The calculation starts with net self-employment earnings, multiplies by 92.35% (to approximate the employer portion deduction), then applies the 12.4% Social Security rate and 2.9% Medicare rate.

Net self-employment income: $100,000

Taxable base: $100,000 x 92.35% = $92,350

Social Security tax: $92,350 x 12.4% = $11,451.40

Medicare tax: $92,350 x 2.9% = $2,678.15

Total self-employment tax: $14,129.55

Deductible employer-equivalent portion: $14,129.55 / 2 = $7,064.78 (deducted on Form 1040 line 15)

The deductible half of self-employment tax reduces your adjusted gross income, which in turn reduces your income tax. This partially offsets the higher FICA burden of self-employment.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the FICA tax rate for 2026?

The FICA tax rate for 2026 is 7.65% for employees and 7.65% for employers, totaling 15.3%. This breaks down into 6.2% for Social Security and 1.45% for Medicare on each side. Social Security tax applies only up to the wage base limit of $176,100 for 2026. Medicare tax has no cap, and an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies to wages over $200,000 for single filers or $250,000 for married filing jointly.

What are the federal income tax brackets for 2026?

For 2026, the federal income tax brackets for single filers are 10% on income up to $11,925, 12% from $11,926 to $48,475, 22% from $48,476 to $103,350, 24% from $103,351 to $197,300, 32% from $197,301 to $250,525, 35% from $250,526 to $626,350, and 37% on income over $626,350. For married filing jointly, each bracket threshold is roughly double the single filer amount.

What is the difference between employee and employer payroll taxes?

Employees pay 6.2% Social Security tax, 1.45% Medicare tax, and federal plus state income tax withholding. Employers match the 6.2% Social Security and 1.45% Medicare contributions, and also pay FUTA tax (6% on the first $7,000 of each employee's wages, reduced to 0.6% with the standard credit). The employer's share does not appear on the employee's pay stub but represents a real cost of employment.

What is FUTA and how much does it cost?

FUTA stands for the Federal Unemployment Tax Act. It is paid entirely by employers at a rate of 6.0% on the first $7,000 of wages paid to each employee per year. Most employers receive a credit of up to 5.4% for paying state unemployment taxes, reducing the effective FUTA rate to 0.6%. This means the maximum FUTA cost per employee is $42 per year (0.6% of $7,000). Some states that have borrowed from the federal unemployment trust fund may have a reduced credit, increasing the effective rate.

How do pre-tax deductions reduce payroll taxes?

Pre-tax deductions are subtracted from gross pay before taxes are calculated. Traditional 401(k) contributions reduce federal and state income tax but are still subject to FICA taxes. Section 125 cafeteria plan deductions, including health insurance premiums, FSA contributions, and HSA contributions through payroll, reduce both income tax and FICA taxes. A $500 monthly health insurance premium saves an employee roughly $38.25 per month in FICA taxes alone (7.65% of $500), plus their marginal income tax savings.

Do I pay taxes on overtime at a higher rate?

Overtime pay is not taxed at a special rate. It is taxed as ordinary income at your marginal tax rate. The reason overtime checks seem more heavily taxed is that the payroll system withholds at a rate based on annualizing that paycheck's amount. If your normal biweekly check is $2,000 and your overtime check is $3,500, the system withholds as if you earn $3,500 every two weeks ($91,000 annualized) rather than your actual $52,000 salary. The excess withholding is returned to you as a tax refund when you file your annual return.

What happens if I earn more than the Social Security wage base?

Once your cumulative earnings for the year exceed the Social Security wage base ($176,100 for 2026), neither you nor your employer pays the 6.2% Social Security tax on additional wages. You will see a bump in your take-home pay for the remaining paychecks of the year. Medicare tax (1.45%) continues to apply to all wages with no cap, and the additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies to wages over $200,000 for single filers.

Which states have no income tax?

Nine states have no state income tax on wages: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire (taxes investment income only), South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. However, some of these states make up for the lack of income tax through higher sales taxes, property taxes, or other fees. Washington state, for example, has no income tax but has one of the highest sales tax rates in the country at up to 10.25% combined state and local.

When are payroll tax deposits due?

The deposit schedule depends on the total tax liability reported during a lookback period. Monthly depositors must deposit payroll taxes by the 15th of the following month. Semi-weekly depositors must deposit by Wednesday if payday falls on Wednesday, Thursday, or Friday, and by Friday if payday falls on Saturday, Sunday, Monday, or Tuesday. New employers are monthly depositors by default. The IRS assigns your deposit schedule annually based on the total tax liability in the lookback period (July 1 through June 30 of the prior year). If you accumulate $100,000 or more in taxes on any day, you must deposit by the next business day regardless of your normal schedule.
