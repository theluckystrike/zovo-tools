What Happens to Your Paycheck Before You See It

I remember staring at my first real paycheck and wondering where $800 went. The gross pay said one number. The deposit in my bank account said something very different. If you have ever had that moment, this is the article I wish I had read on day one.

By Michael Lip / March 25, 2026 / 24 min read

The Anatomy of a Pay Stub

Every paycheck has two numbers that define your financial life. Gross pay is what your employer agreed to pay you. Net pay (take-home pay) is what actually hits your bank account. The gap between them is where federal taxes, state taxes, Social Security, Medicare, health insurance, retirement contributions, and sometimes a dozen other line items consume a significant percentage of your earnings.

For someone earning $85,000 per year, the gap between gross and net is typically $20,000 to $30,000 depending on state of residence, filing status, and benefit elections. That is 24% to 35% of your salary that you never touch. Understanding exactly where it goes is the first step toward making informed decisions about your money.

Your pay stub contains several sections. Earnings (gross pay, overtime, bonuses). Pre-tax deductions (401k, health insurance, HSA). Taxes (federal, state, local, FICA). Post-tax deductions (Roth 401k, garnishments, disability insurance). Net pay.

A paycheck tax calculator will estimate your take-home pay from any gross salary.

Federal Income Tax: The Marginal Bracket System

The single biggest confusion about income tax is the bracket system. I have talked to people who genuinely believe that earning one more dollar could push them into a higher bracket and reduce their overall take-home pay. That is not how it works.

The U.S. uses marginal tax brackets. Each bracket applies only to the income within that bracket's range. Your first dollars are always taxed at 10%, regardless of how much you earn in total.

Marginal vs. Effective Rate

Your marginal rate is the rate on your highest dollar of income. Your effective rate is your total tax divided by total income. These are very different numbers.

If you earn $85,000 as a single filer, your marginal rate is 22%. But your effective rate is much lower because the first $11,600 was taxed at only 10% and the next $35,550 at only 12%. I will work through the exact numbers in the full walkthrough below.

Earning one extra dollar never reduces your total take-home pay. If you earn $47,150, you are taxed at 12% on that last dollar. If you earn $47,151, only that one additional dollar is taxed at 22%. The tax on all your previous income stays the same. There is no cliff. There is no penalty for earning more.

The Standard Deduction: Income You Do Not Pay Tax On

Before your income hits the tax brackets, the standard deduction is subtracted. For 2026, the standard deduction is $14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married filing jointly.

If your gross income is $85,000 and you take the standard deduction, your taxable income is $85,000 - $14,600 = $70,400. Tax brackets apply to $70,400, not $85,000.

You can choose to itemize deductions instead of taking the standard deduction if your itemized deductions exceed the standard amount. Common itemized deductions include mortgage interest, state and local taxes (capped at $10,000), charitable contributions, and medical expenses above 7.5% of adjusted gross income. Since the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act of 2017 nearly doubled the standard deduction, roughly 87% of filers take the standard deduction because their itemized deductions do not exceed it.

FICA: The Taxes Nobody Explains

FICA stands for Federal Insurance Contributions Act. It funds Social Security and Medicare. Unlike income tax, FICA has no brackets, no standard deduction, and no way to reduce it through normal deductions. It comes straight off the top.

Social Security Tax

Rate: 6.2% of gross income. Your employer pays an additional 6.2% on your behalf (you never see this). The wage base cap for 2026 is $168,600. Income above that amount is not subject to Social Security tax.

For someone earning $85,000: $85,000 * 0.062 = $5,270 per year, or $202.69 per biweekly paycheck.

Medicare Tax

Rate: 1.45% of all gross income (no cap). Your employer pays an additional 1.45%. There is an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% on individual income above $200,000 (the employer does not match this additional portion).

For someone earning $85,000: $85,000 * 0.0145 = $1,232.50 per year, or $47.40 per biweekly paycheck.

Total FICA on $85,000

Social Security: $5,270 + Medicare: $1,232.50 = $6,502.50 per year. That is $250.10 per biweekly paycheck. Your employer pays another $6,502.50 on top, so the total FICA contribution on your behalf is $13,005.

Self-employed individuals pay both halves: 12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare = 15.3% total. This is called self-employment tax, and it is one of the biggest financial shocks for people who transition from employment to freelancing.

Where you live changes your take-home pay by thousands of dollars per year. Nine states have no income tax at all. California taxes income at rates up to 13.3%.

Pre-Tax Deductions: The Underappreciated Tax Shield

Pre-tax deductions are subtracted from your income before federal income tax is calculated. This means every dollar you put into a traditional 401k, health insurance premiums, HSA, or FSA effectively costs you less than a dollar because it reduces your tax bill.

Traditional 401k

The 2026 employee contribution limit is $23,500 ($31,000 if you are 50 or older). Contributions are pre-tax, meaning a $23,500 contribution at a 22% marginal tax rate saves $5,170 in federal income tax. Your paycheck decreases by $23,500 but your tax bill decreases by $5,170, so the net cost to your take-home pay is $18,330.

At the same time, the $23,500 is invested and compounds tax-free until withdrawal in retirement. You pay income tax when you withdraw, presumably in a lower tax bracket during retirement.

Health Insurance Premiums

Employer-sponsored health insurance premiums are almost always pre-tax. The average annual premium for single coverage in 2025 was $8,951, with the employer paying $7,034 and the employee paying $1,917. For family coverage, the total average was $25,572, with the employee paying $6,106.

These employee premiums reduce your taxable income. A $6,106 family health insurance premium at a 22% marginal rate saves $1,343 in federal taxes and roughly $468 in FICA (health insurance premiums are exempt from FICA, unlike 401k contributions which are still subject to FICA).

HSA (Health Savings Account)

If you have a high-deductible health plan (HDHP), you can contribute to an HSA. The 2026 limits are $4,300 for individual coverage and $8,550 for family coverage. HSA contributions are pre-tax, the money grows tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are also tax-free. This is the only account type that gets tax benefits at all three stages.

Unused HSA funds roll over indefinitely and can be invested like a retirement account. Some financial planners call the HSA the "stealth IRA" because after age 65, you can withdraw for any purpose (paying income tax but no penalty, like a traditional IRA).

FSA (Flexible Spending Account)

FSAs allow pre-tax contributions for healthcare ($3,200 limit in 2026) or dependent care ($5,000 limit). The major difference from an HSA is the "use it or lose it" rule: most FSA funds not spent by year-end are forfeited (employers may offer a $640 rollover or a 2.5-month grace period, but the full amount does not carry over).

For someone who knows they will have medical expenses (prescriptions, regular doctor visits, planned procedures), an FSA can save 22-37% on those expenses simply through the tax benefit. On $3,200 of medical spending at a 22% marginal rate, the FSA saves $704 in federal tax plus $245 in FICA.

Understanding the W-4

The W-4 is the form that tells your employer how much federal income tax to withhold from each paycheck. The IRS redesigned it in 2020, eliminating the old allowances system and replacing it with a system based on dollar amounts.

The goal of the W-4 is to make your total annual withholding match your actual tax liability as closely as possible. If too much is withheld, you get a refund (you gave the government an interest-free loan). If too little is withheld, you owe money at filing time (and possibly an underpayment penalty).

The Five Steps of the W-4

Step 1: Filing status (single, married filing jointly, head of household).

Step 2: Multiple jobs adjustment. If you have two jobs or your spouse works, this step prevents underwithholding by accounting for the combined income that pushes into higher brackets.

Step 3: Claim dependents. Each qualifying child under 17 reduces tax by $2,000. Each other dependent reduces tax by $500. Enter the total expected credit amount.

Step 4: Other adjustments. Line 4(a) for additional income not subject to withholding (freelance income, interest, dividends). Line 4(b) for deductions beyond the standard deduction (if you itemize). Line 4(c) for extra withholding per pay period (a flat dollar amount).

Step 5: Sign and date.

If you received a large refund last year (over $1,000), your W-4 is overwithholding. That $1,000 refund means you gave the government an extra $38.46 per biweekly paycheck that could have been in your bank account earning interest. Adjust your W-4 to bring withholding closer to your actual liability. Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator tool to determine the right settings.

Hourly Rate Reality Check

Your salary sounds like one number, but your actual hourly take-home rate is significantly less.

At $85,000 per year, the pre-tax hourly rate is $85,000 / 2,080 hours = $40.87/hour.

But after all deductions (using the Texas example with 401k, health insurance, HSA, federal tax, and FICA), net pay is $59,532. That works out to $59,532 / 2,080 = $28.62/hour in actual take-home pay.

In New York City: $52,532 / 2,080 = $25.26/hour.

Overtime, Bonuses, and Supplemental Wages

Many people are surprised when their bonus check or overtime pay has a higher tax withholding rate than their regular paycheck. The IRS requires employers to withhold federal tax on supplemental wages at a flat 22% rate (or 37% for supplemental wages above $1 million), regardless of your actual tax bracket.

This often means bonuses are overwitheld. If your actual marginal rate is 12% or your effective rate is below 22%, you will get the excess back as a refund when you file your tax return. But between receiving the bonus and filing your return, the IRS holds the difference.

Overtime is calculated at 1.5x your regular hourly rate for hours over 40 per week (for non-exempt employees). On an $85,000 salary ($40.87/hour), overtime pays $61.30/hour gross. After the 22% supplemental withholding plus FICA (7.65%), you keep about $43.19/hour of that overtime. Still worth it, but noticeably less than the $61.30 headline number.

Self-Employment Tax: The Double FICA Hit

If you freelance, contract, or run your own business, you pay self-employment tax. This is the employer and employee share of FICA combined: 12.4% for Social Security (up to the $168,600 cap) plus 2.9% for Medicare = 15.3% total.

On $85,000 of self-employment income: SE tax = $85,000 * 0.9235 * 0.153 = $12,003. (The 0.9235 multiplier exists because self-employed individuals can deduct the employer-equivalent portion; it is a quirk of the tax code.)

That $12,003 is nearly double the $6,502.50 that an employee pays. It is the most significant difference between W-2 employment and 1099 contract work. A freelancer needs to earn substantially more than an employee to achieve the same take-home pay.

Common Paycheck Mistakes and Misconceptions

Thinking a Tax Refund Is Free Money

A tax refund means you overpaid the IRS throughout the year. The average federal refund in 2025 was $3,050. That money sat in a government account earning nothing for you instead of sitting in a high-yield savings account earning 4-5% APY. On $3,050 at 4.5% for 6 months (the average time between overpayment and refund), you gave up about $69 in interest. Not catastrophic, but not zero.

Some people prefer large refunds as forced savings. That is a valid behavioral approach if you know you would spend the extra $117/paycheck. But from a pure math standpoint, the optimal strategy is to match your withholding to your liability and save the difference yourself.

Ignoring FICA Because It Is Not "Income Tax"

FICA is 7.65% of your gross income (up to the Social Security cap). On $85,000, that is $6,502.50, which is actually more than the federal income tax for many middle-income earners. Ignoring FICA when calculating your tax burden gives you an unrealistically optimistic view of your take-home pay.

Not Adjusting Withholding After Life Changes

Getting married, having a child, buying a home (mortgage interest deduction), or starting a side business all change your tax situation. If you got married and did not update your W-4 to married filing jointly, you might be overwithholding significantly because the married brackets are roughly double the single brackets.

What Your Employer Pays That You Never See

Your gross pay is not the full cost your employer pays to employ you. There are additional employer-side costs that never appear on your pay stub.

  • Employer FICA match: 7.65% of your salary ($6,502.50 on $85,000)
  • Employer health insurance share: average $7,034 for single coverage
  • 401k employer match: if 50% match on 6%, that is $2,550
  • Unemployment insurance (FUTA/SUTA): varies, roughly $200-$600
  • Workers' compensation insurance: varies by industry, 0.5-5% of payroll

For the $85,000 employee with typical benefits, the total employer cost is approximately $101,000 to $106,000. The difference between what the employer pays and what the employee takes home is staggering: the employer spends roughly $103,000 and the employee takes home roughly $59,500 (in Texas). The $43,500 gap is consumed by taxes, insurance, and retirement contributions.

Planning Your Deductions Strategically

Once you understand where every dollar goes, you can make informed decisions about which levers to pull.

Maximize 401k contributions (at least to the employer match). Contribute to an HSA if eligible (triple tax advantage). Choose the right health plan (high-deductible with HSA vs. traditional PPO). Adjust your W-4 annually (especially after major life changes). Consider Roth vs. traditional contributions based on whether you expect to be in a higher or lower tax bracket in retirement.

The paycheck tax calculator lets you model different scenarios. Try adjusting your 401k percentage and see how it changes both your take-home pay and your year-end retirement savings. The tradeoff is always smaller paychecks now versus more compounded wealth later.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much of my paycheck goes to federal income tax?

It depends on income and filing status. A single filer earning $85,000 with the standard deduction pays approximately $8,165 in federal income tax, an effective rate of about 9.6%. With pre-tax 401k contributions, the tax drops further because those contributions reduce taxable income.

What is FICA and how much is it?

FICA funds Social Security (6.2% up to $168,600) and Medicare (1.45% on all income). On $85,000, total FICA is $6,502.50. Your employer pays an equal amount. Self-employed individuals pay both halves (15.3% total).

What is the difference between marginal and effective tax rate?

Marginal is the rate on your last dollar of income. Effective is total tax divided by total income. An $85,000 earner is in the 22% marginal bracket but pays about 9.6% effective federal rate. The marginal rate only applies to income above the bracket threshold.

How do 401k contributions affect my paycheck?

Traditional 401k contributions reduce taxable income dollar for dollar. Contributing $5,100 (6% of $85,000) at a 22% marginal rate saves $1,122 in federal tax. Your paycheck goes down by $5,100 but your tax goes down by $1,122, so the net cost to take-home is $3,978.

Which states have no income tax?

Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire (dividends/interest only), South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. The annual savings on $85,000 compared to California is about $4,200, and compared to New York City is about $7,000.

What is a W-4 and how does it affect my paycheck?

The W-4 tells your employer how much federal tax to withhold. Getting a large refund means you overwitheld; owing a lot means you underwitheld. Adjust it after marriage, children, home purchase, or any significant income change. Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator for the right settings.

What is the standard deduction for 2026?

$14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married filing jointly (projected based on inflation adjustments). This is subtracted from gross income before tax brackets are applied. About 87% of filers take the standard deduction.

How do I convert salary to hourly rate?

Divide annual salary by 2,080 (52 weeks times 40 hours). $85,000 / 2,080 = $40.87 pre-tax. After all deductions, the effective take-home hourly rate drops to $25-29 depending on state. A salary to hourly calculator makes this instant.
