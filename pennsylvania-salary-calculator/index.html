How PA Taxes Work

Pennsylvania's tax system has several layers that make it unique among US states. The state imposes a flat 3.07% income tax on all taxable compensation, which is one of the lowest flat rates in the country. However, the true tax picture in PA is more involved because most municipalities levy their own local earned income tax (EIT) on top of the state rate. This local tax varies widely, from as low as 0.5% in some rural townships to 3.75% for Philadelphia residents.

For W-2 employees, your employer withholds federal income tax, PA state income tax (3.07%), local EIT, and FICA contributions from each paycheck. If you live in one municipality and work in another, the split between your resident and work-location tax rates follows PA Act 32 rules. Generally, you receive credit for taxes paid to your work location against your resident rate, so you pay the higher of the two rates, not both.

Pennsylvania defines taxable compensation broadly but with notable exceptions. Wages, salaries, commissions, bonuses, and tips are all taxable. However, PA does not tax certain types of income that other states include. Retirement distributions (after age 59.5), Social Security benefits, and certain disability payments are generally exempt from PA income tax, making the state attractive for retirees despite the local tax burden on working-age residents.

Unlike most states, Pennsylvania does not have a standard deduction for state income tax purposes. Your PA taxable income is essentially your total compensation minus a limited set of allowable deductions such as unreimbursed employee expenses, IRC Section 125 cafeteria plan contributions, and union dues.

The federal standard deduction for 2024 is $14,600 for single filers and $29,200 for married filing jointly. These amounts reduce your taxable income before the bracket calculations apply. For a single filer earning $75,000, the federal taxable income is $60,400, resulting in a federal tax of approximately $8,580.

Keep in mind that marginal brackets mean a raise never decreases your take-home pay. If you move from the 22% bracket into the 24% bracket, only the dollars above the 22% threshold ($100,525 for single filers) get taxed at 24%. Every prior dollar stays at its original, lower rate.

PA State Income Tax

Pennsylvania's flat 3.07% income tax rate is among the lowest state income tax rates in the nation. This rate has remained unchanged for many years, providing stability for tax planning. Unlike the federal system or states with graduated brackets, every dollar of PA taxable compensation is taxed at 3.07% regardless of how much you earn.

Calculating your PA state tax is straightforward: multiply your taxable compensation by 0.0307. For an employee earning $75,000 with no special deductions, the PA state tax would be $2,302.50 per year, or approximately $88.56 per biweekly paycheck.

One notable aspect of PA's tax system is that traditional 401(k) and 403(b) contributions are not subject to PA state income tax when contributed. When you eventually withdraw those funds in retirement (after age 59.5), those distributions are also generally not taxed by PA. This double benefit makes Pennsylvania particularly favorable for retirement savings compared to most other states, where withdrawals are taxed as ordinary income.

Pennsylvania is one of only nine states with a flat income tax rate. The 3.07% rate is the lowest among flat-tax states. However, when you add local EIT (typically 1-3%), the combined state and local burden can approach or exceed that of states with higher headline rates but no local taxes.

Local Earned Income Tax

The local earned income tax (EIT) is one of the defining features of Pennsylvania's tax system. Nearly every municipality in PA levies an EIT on residents' earned income, including wages, salaries, commissions, bonuses, and net profits from self-employment. The rates vary significantly by municipality, and understanding your specific rate is important for precise paycheck planning.

Under PA Act 32, local tax collection was consolidated under a handful of regional tax collection agencies. The total EIT rate for any given municipality is typically split between the municipality itself and the school district. Common EIT rate ranges include:

If you live in one municipality and work in another, the work-location tax rate is credited against your resident rate. For example, if you live in a township with a 1.5% EIT and work in a city with a 2% EIT, you would pay 2% total (the higher of the two). If you live in the 2% jurisdiction and work in the 1.5% jurisdiction, you still pay 2% because your resident rate takes priority.

Philadelphia operates its own wage tax system, separate from the Act 32 EIT system used by the rest of the state. If you live or work in Philadelphia, you are subject to the Philadelphia wage tax rather than a standard EIT. The Philadelphia resident rate is 3.75%, and the non-resident rate is 3.44%.

The local EIT is a significant expense that many people overlook when comparing salaries between PA and other states. A worker earning $75,000 in a municipality with a 1.5% EIT pays $1,125 in local taxes. In Philadelphia, the same worker pays $2,812.50 in wage tax. These amounts are on top of the 3.07% state tax and federal obligations.

Philadelphia Wage Tax

Philadelphia's wage tax deserves special attention because it is one of the highest local income taxes in the United States. The city applies its wage tax to all salaries, wages, commissions, and other compensation earned by Philadelphia residents (regardless of where they work) and non-residents who work within city limits.

For a Philadelphia resident, the combined state and local income tax burden is 6.82% (3.07% PA state plus 3.75% city wage tax). This total exceeds the flat rates in many other states, including North Carolina (4.5%), Michigan (4.05%), and Georgia (5.49%). When you factor in federal income tax and FICA, a Philadelphia resident earning $75,000 can expect to keep roughly 70-72% of gross salary.

Philadelphia has been gradually reducing its wage tax rates over recent years. The city has committed to further reductions as part of a long-term plan to make the city more competitive for workers and businesses. Check the Philadelphia Revenue Department website for the most current rates.

Remote work has complicated Philadelphia wage tax obligations. If you live in Philadelphia and work remotely for a company outside the city, you still owe the resident wage tax. If you live outside Philadelphia and your employer is in the city, but you work remotely from home, the rules depend on your employer's policies and whether they have adjusted withholding. This area has been a subject of ongoing legal and administrative clarification since 2020.

FICA Taxes Explained

FICA (Federal Insurance Contributions Act) taxes fund Social Security and Medicare programs. These payroll taxes apply uniformly across all states and are not affected by your PA state or local tax situation.

Social Security tax: Employees pay 6.2% on wages up to $168,600 (the 2024 wage base). Your employer matches this with an additional 6.2%. Once your earnings exceed $168,600 in a calendar year, Social Security tax stops. The maximum employee Social Security tax is $10,453.20.

Medicare tax: Employees pay 1.45% on all wages with no cap. Employers match this amount. If your wages exceed $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), you owe an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% on the excess. Your employer does not match the additional amount.

For a PA employee earning $75,000, FICA comes to $5,737.50 annually (Social Security: $4,650 plus Medicare: $1,087.50). Pre-tax 401(k) contributions reduce your federal and PA state taxable income but do not reduce FICA wages. Health insurance premiums paid through a Section 125 plan do reduce FICA wages.

Self-Employment Taxes in PA

Self-employed individuals in Pennsylvania face a more involved tax situation than W-2 employees. You owe federal self-employment tax (15.3%), federal income tax, PA state income tax (3.07%), and local earned income tax on your net self-employment earnings.

The self-employment tax rate is 15.3%, consisting of 12.4% for Social Security (on net earnings up to $168,600) and 2.9% for Medicare (on all net earnings). You calculate SE tax on 92.35% of your net self-employment income and can deduct half of your SE tax from gross income for income tax purposes.

Pennsylvania self-employed workers file Form PA-40 along with Schedule C (or federal equivalent) and PA Schedule SP if applicable. You must also file a local tax return with your municipality's tax collector. Quarterly estimated payments are required for both federal (Form 1040-ES) and PA state (Form PA-40ES) taxes if you expect to owe more than $8,000 in PA tax.

Self-employed individuals in Philadelphia owe the Business Income and Receipts Tax (BIRT) to the net profits tax. BIRT is assessed at 0.1415% on gross receipts and 5.99% on net income. This can substantially increase the tax burden for Philadelphia-based freelancers and contractors.

Deductions available to PA self-employed workers mirror many federal deductions: home office, vehicle expenses, health insurance premiums, business equipment, professional development, and retirement plan contributions (SEP IRA, Solo 401k). Keep detailed records, as both the PA DOR and local tax collectors may audit self-employment returns.

PA Retirement Planning

Pennsylvania is one of the most retirement-friendly states in the nation when it comes to taxes. The state does not tax most retirement income for individuals who have reached eligibility age, including Social Security benefits, 401(k) and IRA distributions, pension income, and annuities. This makes PA a standout destination for retirees, especially those coming from states that fully tax retirement income.

For current workers, pre-tax 401(k) contributions reduce both federal and PA state taxable income. However, unlike most states, PA also exempts the eventual distributions from tax. This means 401(k) savings in PA get a tax break going in and coming out at the state level, which is exceptional.

The PA state pension systems (SERS for state employees, PSERS for school employees) require employee contributions of 6.25-7.5% of salary. These contributions reduce your take-home pay during working years but provide a defined benefit pension in retirement that is generally exempt from PA income tax.

Contribution limits for 2024 retirement accounts: 401(k)/403(b) is $23,000 ($30,500 if age 50+), Traditional/Roth IRA is $7,000 ($8,000 if age 50+), and SEP IRA for self-employed is up to $69,000. these contributions while working in PA can result in significant tax savings both now and in retirement.

PA Commuter Tax Rules

Pennsylvania's commuter tax rules under Act 32 are important for workers who live in one municipality but work in another. The general principle is that you pay the higher of your resident rate or your work-location rate, not both. This credit system prevents double taxation while ensuring the higher-taxing jurisdiction collects its full rate.

Here is how the credit system works in practice:

ScenarioResident RateWork RateYou PayTo Whom
Live in suburbs, work in city1.0%2.0%2.0%2% to work city
Live in city, work in suburbs2.0%1.0%2.0%1% to work, 1% to resident
Live and work in same place1.5%1.5%1.5%1.5% to resident/work
Live in PA, work in non-tax area1.0%0%1.0%1% to resident
Live in Philly, work outside3.75%N/A3.75%3.75% to Philadelphia

Workers who commute across state lines face additional considerations. If you live in PA and work in NJ, DE, or MD, reciprocal tax agreements may apply. PA has reciprocal agreements with Indiana, Maryland, New Jersey, Ohio, Virginia, and West Virginia. Under these agreements, you only pay income tax to your state of residence, not the state where you work. Your employer should withhold PA tax rather than the work-state tax if you provide the appropriate exemption certificate.

Remote work has complicated these rules. If your employer is in Philadelphia but you work from home in the suburbs, the question of whether you owe the Philadelphia wage tax depends on your employer's policies and the specifics of your arrangement. Philadelphia has taken the position that wages earned by non-residents working from home during the pandemic may not be subject to the wage tax, but this is an evolving area. Consult a tax professional if your remote work situation crosses municipal boundaries.

If you work in multiple PA municipalities during the year (such as construction workers or traveling salespeople), the allocation of local tax can be complex. Generally, tax is owed to each municipality in proportion to the time worked there. Your employer may split withholding across multiple jurisdictions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the PA state income tax rate?Pennsylvania has a flat 3.07% state income tax on all taxable compensation. This rate has been stable for many years and applies equally to all income levels.

How do I find my local EIT rate?Visit the PA DCED website (dced.pa.gov) and search for your municipality, or check with your local tax collector (Berkheimer, Keystone Collections, or Jordan Tax Service). Your rate depends on your resident municipality.

Does PA tax 401(k) contributions?No. Traditional 401(k) contributions are exempt from PA state income tax when made, and distributions in retirement (after age 59.5) are also generally exempt. This is a significant benefit compared to most states.

What is the Philadelphia wage tax?Philadelphia imposes a wage tax of 3.75% on residents and 3.44% on non-residents who work in the city. This replaces the local EIT used by the rest of the state and is significantly higher than most municipal EIT rates.

Does PA tax Social Security?No. Pennsylvania fully exempts Social Security benefits from state income tax, regardless of your total income. Social Security is also exempt from local earned income tax.

Do I have to pay both resident and work-location local taxes?No. Under PA Act 32, you receive credit for the tax paid to your work location against your resident tax obligation. You pay the higher of the two rates, not both combined.

When are PA state taxes due?PA state income tax returns (Form PA-40) are due April 15, the same as federal returns. Quarterly estimated payments are due April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15.

How does PA compare to NJ for taxes?PA has a lower state rate (3.07% vs NJ's graduated rates up to 10.75%) but adds local EIT. For mid-range salaries ($50K-$100K), suburban PA and NJ are roughly comparable. PA wins for retirees due to the exemption of retirement income.
