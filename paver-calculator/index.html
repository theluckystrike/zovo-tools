Paver Patterns Guide

The pattern you choose affects the structural integrity, waste factor, and appearance of your installation. Here are the most popular options:

Herringbone (90°)

The strongest pattern for driveways and high-traffic areas. Pavers interlock at 90-degree angles, creating excellent resistance to lateral shifting. Requires about 10-15% waste for edge cuts.

Driveways High Traffic

Running Bond

A staggered brick pattern where each row is offset by half. Simple to install with minimal waste (5-8%). Works well for walkways and patios. Sometimes called a "stretcher bond."

Walkways Easy Install

Basketweave

Pairs of pavers alternate between horizontal and vertical orientation, creating a woven appearance. Best for decorative patios. Not recommended for driveways as it can shift under heavy loads.

Patios Decorative

Stack Bond

Pavers are aligned in a grid with no offset. Creates a clean, modern look. Weakest structurally because joints line up. Best for patios and light-use areas only.

Modern Look Light Use

Base Preparation Guide

A solid base is the single most important factor determining how long your paver installation lasts. Skipping or shortcutting base preparation is the number one cause of paver failure. Here is the process:

Step-by-Step Base Preparation

  1. Excavate. Dig out the area to the required depth: paver thickness + 1" bedding sand + gravel base depth (4" for patios, 6-8" for driveways). For most patios, that means excavating about 7-8 inches total.
  2. Grade for drainage. Slope the subgrade away from structures at a minimum of 1/4 inch per foot (2% grade). This slope carries through all layers to the finished surface.
  3. Compact the subgrade. Use a plate compactor on the native soil. If the soil is soft clay, consider laying landscape fabric before adding gravel.
  4. Add gravel in lifts. Spread 2 inches of crushed stone (3/4" minus / road base) at a time. Compact each lift before adding the next. This builds a dense, stable foundation.
  5. Screed the bedding sand. Spread 1 inch of coarse concrete sand (C-33 or ASTM C33) over the compacted gravel. Use screed rails and a straight board to achieve a perfectly level surface.
  6. Lay pavers. Place pavers on the screeded sand without disturbing it. Work from one corner outward, kneeling on already-placed pavers rather than the sand.

For freeze-thaw climates (USDA zones 3-6), consider increasing the gravel base by 2 inches to improve drainage and reduce frost heave risk.

Sand and Gravel Requirements

Calculating sand and gravel accurately prevents costly extra deliveries or leftover material sitting in your driveway. Here is how the math works:

Bedding Sand

The standard bedding sand layer is 1 inch deep. To calculate tons needed:

Tons = (Area in sq ft × depth in ft × 100 lbs per cubic ft) / 2000

At 1 inch depth: roughly 0.00417 tons per square foot, or about 1 ton per 240 square feet.

Gravel Base

Compacted crushed stone weighs approximately 130 lbs per cubic foot. To calculate tons needed:

Tons = (Area in sq ft × depth in ft × 130) / 2000

At 4" depth: roughly 0.0217 tons per square foot, or about 1 ton per 46 square feet.

At 6" depth: roughly 0.0325 tons per square foot, or about 1 ton per 31 square feet.

Always order 5-10% extra material to account for uneven ground, compaction loss, and minor calculation differences.

Edge Restraint Essentials

Edge restraints keep pavers from migrating outward over time. Without them, joints widen, sand washes out, and the surface becomes uneven. Every paver installation needs edge restraint along any exposed edge.

  • Plastic paver edging ($0.50-1.50/ft) is the most common choice. Flexible, easy to curve, and secured with 10" galvanized spikes every 12 inches.
  • Aluminum L-channel ($1.50-3.00/ft) is more rigid and durable. Better for straight edges and commercial applications.
  • Concrete curb ($3-8/ft installed) is the most permanent option. Often required for commercial driveways.
  • Natural stone or existing hardscape can serve as a restraint if it is stable and does not move.

To calculate total edge restraint: measure the perimeter of your paver area. For a 15 × 20 foot patio, that is 2(15 + 20) = 70 linear feet. Subtract any edges where pavers abut a house foundation, existing concrete, or other fixed structure.

Polymeric Sand Guide

Polymeric sand fills the joints between pavers and hardens into a firm, flexible fill that resists weeds, ants, and erosion. It is a significant upgrade over regular joint sand.

  • Coverage: One 50-lb bag covers approximately 25-75 sq ft depending on paver size and joint width. Larger pavers with narrow joints use less sand per square foot.
  • Application: Sweep sand into joints, vibrate with a plate compactor, sweep again, then mist with water to activate the polymers. Avoid heavy rain for 24 hours after application.
  • Cost: $20-30 per 50-lb bag. More expensive than regular sand but lasts years longer and eliminates weed maintenance.
  • When to skip it: If pavers are set on a flexible sand-only base without edge restraints (temporary installations), regular sand works fine.

Drainage Considerations

Water management is critical for long-lasting paver installations. Poor drainage leads to erosion, base failure, and ice damage in cold climates.

Surface Drainage

  • Maintain a minimum 2% slope (1/4" per foot) away from buildings.
  • For patios against a house, slope away from the foundation.
  • For driveways, slope toward the street or a drainage swale.
  • Crown long walkways slightly in the center to shed water to both sides.

Subsurface Drainage

  • In clay soils, install a 4" perforated drain pipe at the base of the gravel layer along the low side.
  • Wrap drain pipe in filter fabric to prevent clogging.
  • Connect to a daylight outlet, dry well, or storm drain (check local codes).
  • Permeable pavers with open joints allow water to infiltrate directly, reducing runoff. Some municipalities offer stormwater credits for permeable paver installations.

If your area experiences heavy rain or you are paving near a structure with a basement, invest in proper subsurface drainage. The cost of a drain pipe during installation is a fraction of repairing water damage later.

Installation Tips

After years of seeing paver installations done right and wrong, here are the tips that make the biggest difference:

  • Start from a fixed edge. Begin laying pavers against a building wall, existing concrete, or a string line. Work outward so cuts happen at the less visible edges.
  • Place, do not slide. Drop each paver straight down onto the sand bed. Sliding pavers pushes sand and creates an uneven surface.
  • Use spacers or maintain consistent joints. Even 1/16" variation in joint width becomes visible over a large area. Some pavers have built-in spacing lugs.
  • Check lines every 4-5 rows. Stretch a string line and adjust before small errors compound into large misalignment.
  • Cut with a wet saw. A diamond-blade wet saw gives clean, precise cuts. Rent one for the day. It is far superior to a chisel or dry angle grinder for visible edges.
  • Compact after laying. Run a plate compactor with a rubber pad over the finished surface to seat pavers into the sand and level minor height differences.
  • Apply polymeric sand last. Fill joints after compaction, vibrate again to settle sand, then sweep and mist.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

These errors cause most paver installation failures. Knowing what not to do is just as important as knowing the correct process.

  • Skimping on base depth. A 2-inch gravel base will settle and crack pavers within a year or two. Follow the recommended depths: 4" minimum for patios, 6-8" for driveways.
  • Not compacting in lifts. Dumping all the gravel at once and compacting the top does not work. The bottom stays loose. Compact every 2-inch layer.
  • Using the wrong sand. Play sand, mason sand, and beach sand are too fine for bedding. Use concrete sand (C-33) for the base layer. Polymeric sand goes in the joints only.
  • Forgetting slope. A perfectly flat paver patio will pond water. You need that 1/4" per foot minimum slope for drainage.
  • Skipping edge restraints. Even a patio against a house needs edge restraints on the three open sides. Without them, the entire installation gradually spreads apart.
  • Not ordering enough material. Running 20 pavers short means a second delivery fee or a trip to the store. The 10% waste factor exists for a reason.
  • Installing on organic soil. If you are building over a former garden bed or area with lots of organic matter, dig deeper. Organic soil decomposes and settles unevenly.

Maintenance and Longevity

Properly installed pavers require very little maintenance, but some periodic care keeps them looking great for decades:

  • Annual joint sand check. Inspect joints each spring. Top up polymeric sand if it has washed out or settled.
  • Power wash every 2-3 years. Use a fan tip at moderate pressure (1500-2000 PSI). High pressure or turbo nozzles can dislodge joint sand and damage paver surfaces.
  • Seal every 3-5 years (optional). Paver sealers enhance color and protect against stains. Use a breathable, non-film-forming sealer for best results.
  • Weed and moss removal. If weeds appear in joints, pull them and re-apply polymeric sand in that area. Moss can be treated with a diluted oxygen bleach solution.
  • Individual paver replacement. One of the greatest advantages of pavers over poured concrete is the ability to lift and replace individual pavers without affecting the surrounding area.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many pavers do I need for a 12x12 patio?

A 12x12 foot patio is 144 square feet. With 12x12 inch pavers (1 per sq ft) and 10% waste, you need about 159 pavers. With 4x8 inch pavers (~4.5 per sq ft), you need roughly 713 pavers.

How long does a paver installation take?

For a DIY homeowner, a 200-300 sq ft patio typically takes a full weekend for base preparation plus a day for laying pavers. A professional crew can complete the same project in 1-2 days. Driveways take longer due to deeper excavation and larger areas.

Do pavers increase home value?

Yes. According to the National Association of Realtors, a well-designed paver patio can return 50-75% of its cost at resale. Paver driveways often return even more because they are the first thing buyers see. The aesthetic and functional upgrade over basic concrete is a strong selling point.

Pavers vs. poured concrete: which is better?

Pavers win on durability, repairability, and aesthetics. They flex with ground movement instead of cracking, and individual pavers can be replaced. Poured concrete is cheaper initially but cracks are permanent and costly to fix. Over a 20-year period, pavers typically cost less because maintenance and repairs are simpler.
