Project Tips

How to Calculate the Number of Pavers You Need

Calculating pavers for a project involves three steps: measuring the area to be paved, determining the coverage per paver (including joints), and adding a waste factor for cuts and breakage. Getting these numbers right before ordering saves you from the frustration of running short during installation or the waste of having pallets of leftover pavers.

Start by measuring the area in square feet. For rectangular areas, multiply length by width. For L-shaped or irregular areas, break the space into rectangles, calculate each one separately, and add them together. For curved areas, use the closest rectangular approximation and add 10-15% extra pavers for the additional cutting required along the curves.

Next, calculate how many pavers cover one square foot. Divide 144 (the number of square inches in a square foot) by the area of one paver in square inches. A standard 4x8-inch brick paver covers 32 square inches, so you need 144 divided by 32 = 4.5 pavers per square foot. A 12x12-inch paver covers 144 square inches, so you need exactly 1 paver per square foot.

Joint spacing reduces the number of pavers slightly because each paver is surrounded by a gap. For standard 3/16-inch joints, the reduction is about 2-3%. Some calculators ignore joints because the waste factor more than compensates. This calculator includes joints in the calculation for precision, then adds the appropriate waste factor on top.

Understanding Waste Factors

Every paver project requires cutting pavers to fit at the borders, around obstacles, and at pattern transitions. The waste factor accounts for material lost to these cuts plus an allowance for breakage during installation. Different patterns produce different amounts of waste because of the cutting required at the edges.

Paver Types and Their Characteristics

Concrete Pavers

Concrete pavers are manufactured from Portland cement, aggregates, and iron oxide pigments pressed into molds under high pressure. They are the most common residential paver choice because of their wide availability, consistent dimensions, and reasonable cost. Modern concrete pavers come in an enormous range of colors, shapes, and textures, including products that closely mimic natural stone at a fraction of the cost.

Standard concrete pavers have a compressive strength of 8,000 PSI or higher, which exceeds the strength of most poured concrete driveways. They absorb less than 5% moisture by weight, making them resistant to freeze-thaw damage. The interlocking nature of a properly installed paver surface also allows for slight movement without cracking, unlike rigid poured concrete that develops cracks over time.

The main drawback of concrete pavers is that they can fade in color over years of UV exposure. High-quality pavers use through-body color (pigment mixed throughout the concrete) rather than surface-applied color, which maintains appearance much longer. Applying a paver sealer every 2-3 years also helps preserve color and protect against staining.

Clay Brick Pavers

Clay brick pavers are made from natural clay fired in a kiln at temperatures exceeding 2,000 degrees Fahrenheit. The firing process vitrifies the clay, creating an extremely hard, dense product with permanent color that does not fade in sunlight. Clay pavers have been used for paving since ancient Rome, and many brick streets from the 1800s remain in service today.

Clay brick pavers develop a warm, aged patina over time that many homeowners find attractive. They are available in natural earth tones ranging from light tan to deep red to nearly black, depending on the clay composition and firing temperature. The color goes all the way through the paver, so chips and scratches do not show a different color underneath.

The higher cost of clay pavers ($5-$10 per square foot vs $3-$6 for concrete) is offset by their extreme durability and timeless appearance. I recommend clay brick pavers for any project where long-term aesthetics matter more than initial cost, particularly front walkways, courtyards, and traditional home exteriors.

Natural Stone Pavers

Natural stone pavers include granite, bluestone, limestone, sandstone, slate, and quartzite. Each stone has unique characteristics in terms of color, texture, hardness, and weather resistance. Natural stone creates the most distinctive and high-end paver installations, but it costs significantly more than manufactured products and requires more skill to install because of the natural variation in thickness and dimensions.

Granite is the hardest and most durable natural stone paver. It resists scratching, staining, and weathering better than any other paving material. Bluestone is popular in the northeastern United States for its attractive blue-gray color and relatively flat, consistent surface. Limestone and sandstone are softer and more porous, making them better suited for warm, dry climates where freeze-thaw is not a concern.

Porcelain Pavers

Porcelain pavers are a relatively new option in the outdoor paving market. They are made from refined clay fired at very high temperatures, producing a dense, non-porous product with virtually zero water absorption. This makes them highly resistant to staining, frost damage, and fading. Porcelain pavers are available in realistic stone, wood, and concrete textures that are nearly indistinguishable from the natural materials they imitate.

The main advantage of porcelain pavers is their extremely low maintenance. Because they absorb almost no moisture, they resist mold, mildew, and algae growth. Stains from food, grease, and wine wipe off easily. The disadvantage is cost ($7-$15 per square foot) and the fact that they are thinner (typically 3/4 inch) than standard pavers, requiring a more precisely prepared base.

Base Preparation for Pavers

The base is the single most important factor in a paver installation's long-term performance. A paver surface is only as good as the base it sits on. Inadequate base preparation leads to settling, shifting, unevenness, and premature failure, regardless of how expensive the pavers are or how carefully they are laid.

Excavation

The total excavation depth equals the paver thickness plus the bedding sand thickness (1 inch) plus the compacted base thickness (4 inches for residential, 8 inches for driveways). For standard 2-3/8-inch pavers on a residential patio, the excavation depth is 2-3/8 + 1 + 4 = approximately 7-3/8 inches, or roughly 8 inches to provide a slight margin.

Excavate the entire area to a uniform depth, removing all organic material including grass, roots, and topsoil. Organic material decomposes over time, creating voids that cause settling. If you encounter soft spots or fill material during excavation, remove the loose material and replace it with compacted base material to create a uniform, stable subgrade.

Compaction

After excavation, compact the exposed subgrade with a plate compactor or hand tamper before placing any base material. This step is particularly important in areas where the soil has been previously disturbed (near new construction, over utility trenches, or on filled land). The subgrade should be firm enough that you do not leave visible footprints when walking on it.

Place the crushed stone base material in lifts (layers) no more than 2 inches thick, compacting each lift before adding the next. Attempting to compact a 4-inch layer in a single pass does not achieve full compaction at the bottom. Two 2-inch lifts, each compacted separately, produce a much firmer, more uniform base than one 4-inch lift.

Base Material Selection

The standard base material for paver installations is crushed stone aggregate with a gradation that includes both coarse and fine particles. Common trade names include 3/4-inch minus, road base, ABC stone, Class 5 aggregate, and dense grade aggregate. The fine particles fill the voids between the larger stones, and compaction locks everything together into a dense, stable layer.

Do not use pea gravel, river rock, or recycled concrete as base material. These materials do not compact properly because they lack the angular particle shapes and fines that create interlocking compaction. They shift under load and do not provide a stable foundation for pavers.

Bedding Sand Layer

After the base is compacted, spread a 1-inch layer of coarse concrete sand (also called washed concrete sand or ASTM C33 sand) over the base. This bedding sand layer provides a smooth, level surface for setting the pavers and allows minor adjustments to individual paver heights during installation.

Screed the sand to a uniform 1-inch thickness using pipes or conduit as guides. Place two screed pipes on the compacted base, fill between them with sand, and drag a straight board across the pipes to create a flat, even surface. Remove the pipes and fill the channels with sand. Do not compact the bedding sand before laying pavers; the pavers themselves get pushed down into the sand during final compaction.

Paver Installation Process

Laying the Pavers

Start laying pavers from a straight edge or corner, working outward. Place each paver flat onto the bedding sand with a slight downward press, butted firmly against the adjacent pavers with the desired joint spacing. Do not slide pavers into position because this disturbs the bedding sand and creates an uneven surface. Drop each paver straight down into place.

Work from on top of the already-laid pavers whenever possible, standing on the paved surface rather than the bedding sand. Walking on the screeded sand before pavers are placed creates depressions that result in uneven paver heights. As you lay each row, check for alignment with a string line and correct any drift before it compounds over multiple rows.

Cutting Pavers

A wet-cutting masonry saw (also called a brick saw or paver splitter) produces the cleanest cuts with the least waste. For straight cuts, a diamond blade on a circular saw or angle grinder also works well. Always wear safety glasses, hearing protection, and a dust mask when cutting pavers. The dust from cutting concrete and stone pavers is harmful to your lungs.

When cutting border pavers, measure each piece individually rather than cutting multiple pieces to the same dimension. Even on a rectangular project, the border distance varies slightly due to cumulative joint width variations. Cutting each border paver to fit its specific location produces much tighter, more professional-looking borders.

Final Compaction and Sanding

After all pavers are laid and border pavers are cut and placed, run a plate compactor over the entire surface. Use a rubber pad on the compactor plate to prevent scuffing the paver faces. The compaction seats the pavers firmly into the bedding sand and levels any slight height differences between adjacent pavers.

Spread polymeric sand over the surface and sweep it into all joints with a push broom. Make multiple passes to ensure the joints are filled completely. Remove all excess sand from the paver surfaces before activating the polymeric sand with water, because any polymeric sand left on the surface will leave a haze that is very difficult to remove after it hardens.

Mist the surface gently with water per the polymeric sand manufacturer's instructions. The water activates the polymer binders, which bond the sand particles together and create a firm, flexible joint fill. Allow 24 hours for the polymeric sand to cure before allowing foot traffic, and 48-72 hours before vehicle traffic on driveways.

Edge Restraint Systems

Edge restraint holds the perimeter pavers in place and prevents the entire paver field from migrating outward over time. Without edge restraint, the joint sand gradually washes out at the borders, pavers spread apart, and the surface becomes unstable. Edge restraint is not optional; it is a required component of every paver installation.

Plastic paver edging is the most common residential edge restraint. It is a low-profile L-shaped strip that pins against the outside face of the border pavers and anchors into the compacted base with 10-inch galvanized spikes. Install the edging tightly against the pavers and drive spikes every 12-18 inches along the length, plus one spike at each joint between edging sections.

Aluminum edging is a more durable alternative that works well for curved borders because it bends easily to follow gentle radii. Concrete borders (either poured in place or precast curbing) provide the strongest edge restraint and work well for driveways where vehicles may track across the border. Existing structures like house foundations, retaining walls, and concrete sidewalks also serve as edge restraint where pavers butt against them.

Paver Pattern Guide

Running Bond

Running bond is the most popular paver pattern for residential projects. The pavers are laid in rows with each row offset by half a paver length from the row above and below, creating the familiar staggered brick pattern. This offset distributes load across multiple pavers and provides good interlock resistance to lateral shifting.

Running bond works with any rectangular paver size and is the easiest pattern to install. The only cuts required are half-pavers at the beginning or end of alternating rows. For a cleaner look, start with full pavers at the most visible edge of the project and put the half-paver cuts at the less visible back edge.

Herringbone

Herringbone is the strongest interlocking pattern and the required pattern for any paver surface that will carry vehicle traffic. The pavers are set at alternating 45 or 90 degree angles, creating a zigzag pattern where each paver is locked in place by its neighbors on all four sides. This interlocking prevents individual pavers from rotating or shifting under wheel loads.

The 45-degree herringbone (diagonal to the border) is the most common orientation for driveways and parking areas. The 90-degree herringbone (parallel to the border) is also effective and sometimes preferred because it creates a slightly different visual appearance. Both orientations provide equal structural performance.

Herringbone requires more cutting than running bond because every border paver must be cut at an angle. This increases installation time and material waste. Budget an extra 10% over the calculated paver count for herringbone patterns, compared to 5% for running bond.

Basketweave

Basketweave arranges pairs of pavers in alternating horizontal and vertical orientations, creating a woven appearance. This pattern only works with rectangular pavers that have a 2:1 length-to-width ratio (like 4x8 inches), because the pairs must form a square. Each pair of pavers creates a module that aligns with the next pair in the opposite direction.

Basketweave is a classic pattern with a strong traditional aesthetic. It is relatively easy to install because the pairs repeat in a simple grid. However, the straight joint lines in basketweave provide less interlock than running bond or herringbone, making it less suitable for driveways.

Stack Bond

Stack bond (also called jack-on-jack or grid pattern) aligns all pavers in straight rows and columns with no offset. This creates a modern, geometric look that works well with larger format pavers (12x12 or 16x16). The continuous straight lines emphasize the paver geometry and create strong visual direction.

The downside of stack bond is minimal interlock. The straight joint lines create natural fault lines where the surface can shift. Stack bond is acceptable for patios and walkways with proper edge restraint, but not recommended for driveways or areas with vehicle traffic.

Sealing and Protecting Pavers

Paver sealer is a clear or slightly tinted coating that penetrates or sits on the paver surface to protect against staining, fading, and moisture damage. Sealing is optional but recommended for most paver installations, especially in areas where food spills, oil drips, or freeze-thaw exposure are concerns.

Water-based acrylic sealers are the most common type. They are easy to apply, dry quickly, and provide a natural or slight wet-look finish. Solvent-based acrylic sealers produce a more pronounced wet look with deeper color enhancement. Penetrating sealers absorb into the paver without changing the surface appearance, providing stain protection without any visible coating.

Apply sealer to clean, dry pavers after the installation has been complete for at least 30 days. This waiting period allows efflorescence (white mineral deposits) to work its way out of new concrete pavers. Trying to seal over efflorescence traps the minerals under the sealer, creating a permanent white haze. After the initial application, reseal every 2-4 years depending on traffic and weather exposure.

Paver Maintenance

Regular maintenance keeps a paver surface looking good and functioning properly for decades. Sweep or blow off leaves and debris regularly to prevent organic staining. Remove weeds as soon as they appear in joints by pulling them or applying a targeted herbicide. Replenish polymeric sand in any joints where it has settled or washed out, as bare joints are the entry point for weeds and ants.

For general cleaning, a garden hose and stiff brush handle most dirt and light staining. For oil stains, apply an absorbent material (cat litter works well) immediately and let it sit overnight before sweeping up. Follow with a degreaser if the stain persists. For mold and algae on shaded surfaces, a mixture of one part bleach to ten parts water applied with a pump sprayer kills the growth without damaging the pavers.

If individual pavers settle, crack, or stain beyond repair, they can be removed and replaced without disturbing the surrounding pavers. Pop out the damaged paver with a flat pry bar, add or remove bedding sand to adjust the height, and set the replacement paver in place. This repairability is one of the greatest advantages of pavers over poured concrete. A poured concrete surface with a crack or stain requires an ugly patch or a complete tear-out; a paver surface just needs one replacement unit.

Freeze-Thaw Considerations

In northern climates, freeze-thaw cycles are the primary threat to paver installations. Water that enters the base and bedding layers can freeze and expand, causing individual pavers to heave upward. The key to preventing frost damage is ensuring excellent drainage through and under the paver system.

The compacted aggregate base drains water quickly because of its high permeability. The bedding sand also drains well. As long as these layers can shed water faster than it accumulates, frost heave is minimal. Problems occur when the subgrade soil is clay that holds water like a bowl, allowing the base to become saturated during spring thaw. In these situations, installing a drainage layer (4 inches of open-graded gravel) under the standard base helps water escape before it freezes.

Permeable paver systems, which use open-graded base material and wide joints filled with aggregate instead of polymeric sand, handle freeze-thaw exceptionally well because water passes through the surface rather than running off and collecting at the edges. These systems are increasingly popular in northern states and are often required by stormwater management regulations in new construction.

Permeable Pavers

Permeable paver systems allow rainwater to pass through the paver surface and into the ground below, reducing stormwater runoff and helping recharge groundwater. They work by incorporating wider joints (1/4 to 1/2 inch) filled with small aggregate instead of polymeric sand, and using open-graded (no fines) base material that acts as an underground reservoir.

Many municipalities now require or incentivize permeable paving for new construction and renovations. Some offer credits against stormwater utility fees for property owners who install permeable surfaces. The technical requirements for permeable paver installations differ from conventional systems, including deeper base depths (12-18 inches), open-graded aggregate throughout, and geotextile fabric separating the base from the native soil.

The infiltration rate of a permeable paver system depends on the native soil permeability. Sandy soils can infiltrate water at rates exceeding 5 inches per hour, while clay soils may only handle 0.1 inches per hour. In clay soils, the base acts primarily as a detention basin that slowly releases water over hours or days, rather than immediately infiltrating it into the ground.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many pavers come on a pallet?

Paver pallet quantities vary by manufacturer and paver size. Standard 4x8-inch concrete pavers typically come about 486 per pallet (covering approximately 108 sq ft). 12x12-inch pavers come about 80-120 per pallet (80-120 sq ft). Check with your supplier for exact pallet counts and coverage, as these vary between brands. Buying full pallets is almost always cheaper per unit than buying loose pavers.

Can I install pavers on a slope?

Yes, pavers can be installed on slopes up to about 8% (approximately 1 inch rise per foot). For slopes steeper than about 4%, use a herringbone pattern for maximum interlock and consider using mechanical edge restraint at the downhill border. On slopes steeper than 8%, terracing with retaining walls is a better approach than trying to pave the slope directly.

How much excavation is needed for a paver patio?

For a standard patio with 2-3/8-inch pavers, 1-inch bedding sand, and 4-inch compacted base, excavate 7-8 inches deep. For driveways, the 8-inch base increases total excavation to 11-12 inches. Always dig a few inches beyond the paver border to allow room for edge restraint installation.

What is the cheapest paver option?

Standard concrete pavers in common colors (gray, charcoal, tan) are the most affordable option at $2-$4 per square foot. Buying from big-box home improvement stores during seasonal sales can reduce the price further. Reclaimed or seconds-quality pavers from manufacturers can cost as little as $1 per square foot, though selection is limited and dimensional consistency may vary.

How long does it take to install a paver patio?

A 200 square foot paver patio takes a competent DIYer 2-4 full days of labor. Day 1 for excavation and base preparation. Day 2 for base compaction, screed rails, and sand screeding. Days 3-4 for laying pavers, cutting borders, compacting, and installing polymeric sand. A professional crew of 3-4 workers can complete the same project in 1-2 days.

Do pavers increase home value?

A well-designed paver patio or driveway typically returns 50-80% of its cost in increased home value. The actual return depends on the quality of materials, professional appearance of the installation, and how the outdoor living space integrates with the home. In markets where outdoor living is highly valued (Southern states, California, Pacific Northwest), the return can exceed 80%.

Can I lay pavers directly on dirt?

No. Pavers laid directly on dirt will settle unevenly, shift, and become hazardous within one season. The compacted aggregate base and bedding sand are required structural layers that distribute loads, provide drainage, and maintain a level surface. Skipping the base to save money results in a project that looks terrible and needs to be redone within 1-2 years.

How do I prevent weeds between pavers?

Polymeric sand in the joints is the first line of defense against weeds. When properly installed, the hardened sand prevents seeds from germinating in the joints. Reapply polymeric sand whenever you notice joints that have settled or washed out. For additional protection, a pre-emergent herbicide applied in spring prevents weed seeds from sprouting. Pull any weeds that do appear immediately, before they establish deep roots.

What equipment do I need to install pavers?

For a DIY paver installation, you need a plate compactor (rent for about $60-$90 per day), shovel, wheelbarrow, garden rake, string line and stakes, level, tape measure, rubber mallet, masonry saw or angle grinder with diamond blade (for cuts), push broom, and screed rails (3/4-inch conduit pipes work well). Safety equipment includes glasses, hearing protection, gloves, and a dust mask for cutting.

How far apart should paver joints be?

Standard paver joints are 3/16 inch (about 5 mm) wide. This is narrow enough to provide a tight, clean appearance while allowing room for polymeric sand to fill and lock the joints. Wider joints (1/4 to 3/8 inch) are used with natural stone and rustic installations. For permeable paver systems, joints are 1/4 to 1/2 inch wide, filled with aggregate instead of polymeric sand to allow water infiltration.

Can pavers be used around a pool?

Pavers are an excellent choice for pool decks. They provide a slip-resistant surface when wet, stay cooler than poured concrete in direct sunlight, and can be easily removed and replaced if pool plumbing needs to be accessed. Choose pavers with a textured surface for maximum traction, and seal them to resist chlorine and salt water staining. Avoid dark-colored pavers around pools in hot climates, as they absorb more heat and become uncomfortable for bare feet.
