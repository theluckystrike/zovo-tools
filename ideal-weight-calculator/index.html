How the Formulas Work

Each of these four formulas was developed in a different era and context, which is why they can produce different results. I've found that looking at the range across all four gives a more realistic picture than relying on any single number.

Devine Formula (1974)

Dr. B.J. Devine originally created this formula for calculating drug dosages, not as a weight management tool. It became widely adopted in clinical settings because of its simplicity. For males, the baseline is 50 kg at 5 feet, adding 2.3 kg per inch over 5 feet. For females, the baseline is 45.5 kg at 5 feet, adding 2.3 kg per inch. You'll find this formula referenced in pharmacology textbooks and Wikipedia's article on ideal body weight.

Robinson Formula (1983)

Robinson and colleagues refined Devine's work using updated actuarial data. The male baseline is 52 kg at 5 feet, adding 1.9 kg per inch over 5 feet. For females, it starts at 49 kg with 1.7 kg per inch. This formula tends to produce slightly lower estimates for taller individuals.

Miller Formula (1983)

Miller's formula also emerged in 1983 but uses different coefficients. Males start at 56.2 kg at 5 feet, adding 1.41 kg per inch. Females start at 53.1 kg, adding 1.36 kg per inch. This formula generally gives higher results, especially for shorter people.

Hamwi Formula (1964)

Dr. G.J. Hamwi developed what many consider the simplest approach. Males get 48 kg at 5 feet plus 2.7 kg per inch over 5 feet. Females get 45.5 kg plus 2.2 kg per inch. It was designed as a quick clinical estimation tool and it's still used in many hospital settings today.

Understanding BMI at Ideal Weight

Body Mass Index is a separate measurement, but it's useful to see where your ideal weight falls on the BMI scale. A BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is considered normal by the World Health Organization. Most ideal weight formulas target a BMI around 21 to 23, which sits comfortably in the middle of the normal range.

That said, BMI doesn't account for muscle mass, bone density, or body composition. An athlete with significant muscle mass might have a BMI above 25 while being perfectly healthy. That is one reason why I've included the frame size adjustment in this calculator, as it provides a more personalized estimate.

I've tested this calculator against clinical reference tables and the results consistently fall within the normal BMI range for the given height. Our testing methodology involved cross-referencing results with published medical tables from multiple sources.

How Frame Size Affects Ideal Weight

Frame size is an often overlooked factor in weight assessment. A person with a larger skeletal frame naturally weighs more than someone of the same height with a smaller frame. The adjustment factors used in clinical practice are typically plus or minus 10 percent from the medium-frame baseline.

To determine your frame size, you can measure your wrist circumference relative to your height. For women under 5 foot 2 inches, a wrist under 5.5 inches indicates a small frame, 5.5 to 5.75 inches medium, and over 5.75 inches large. These reference values come from the Metropolitan Life Insurance height-weight tables, which have been discussed extensively on Stack Overflow's health informatics discussions.

You won't find a single "correct" ideal weight because human bodies aren't standardized. The frame size adjustment helps bridge the gap between formula-based estimates and individual body structure. Health professionals I've talked to generally agree that a range is more useful than a single number.

Practical Interpretation

When you get your results, look at the spread across all four formulas. If all four cluster tightly together, there is strong consensus for that weight range. If they spread widely, it means your height falls in a zone where the formulas diverge, and you should focus on the median value rather than any individual formula.

Limitations of Ideal Weight Formulas

These formulas were developed decades ago using data that may not represent today's diverse population. They don't account for age, ethnicity, muscle mass, or individual body composition. They were designed as quick clinical references, not definitive health assessments.

I can't stress enough that these calculators shouldn't replace professional medical advice. If you are making health decisions about weight management, consult a healthcare provider who can evaluate your complete health picture. The formulas also don't work well for very short or very tall individuals, as the linear scaling breaks down at the extremes.

There has been ongoing discussion in the medical community about whether these formulas are still appropriate. Some researchers argue that waist-to-hip ratio is a better predictor of health outcomes than ideal body weight. For a deeper dive into this debate, check the Hacker News discussion on BMI alternatives.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which ideal weight formula is the most accurate?
There isn't a single "most accurate" formula. Each was developed using different datasets and assumptions. The Devine formula is most widely used in clinical pharmacology, while Robinson's 1983 update is often considered the best general-purpose estimate. I recommend looking at the range across all four formulas rather than relying on just one.
Why do the four formulas give different results?
The formulas were created at different times using different population datasets. Devine's 1974 formula was designed for drug dosing, Hamwi's 1964 formula for quick clinical estimates, and Robinson and Miller both published in 1983 using updated actuarial data. The differences reflect varying assumptions about body composition and health.
Can I use this calculator if I'm very tall or very short?
These formulas work best for heights between about 5 feet and 6 feet 4 inches. Outside that range, the linear scaling can produce results that don't match clinical expectations. If you are significantly above or below average height, I'd suggest consulting with a healthcare provider for a personalized assessment.
Does ideal body weight account for muscle mass?
No, none of these formulas account for body composition. A muscular athlete and a sedentary person of the same height would get the same result. The frame size adjustment helps somewhat, but for athletes or bodybuilders, these formulas aren't the right tool. Body composition analysis (like DEXA scanning) is more appropriate in those cases.
How does frame size affect my ideal weight?
Frame size adjusts the baseline by approximately 10 percent. A small frame reduces the ideal weight by 10 percent, while a large frame increases it by 10 percent. This adjustment is based on the Metropolitan Life Insurance tables and reflects the fact that skeletal structure varies significantly between individuals.
Should I trust these formulas for weight loss goals?
These formulas can provide a general target range, but they shouldn't be your only guide. Weight management is complex and involves factors like activity level, metabolic rate, and overall health status. Use these results as a starting point for discussion with a healthcare provider, not as a definitive goal.
Is my data stored anywhere?
No. All calculations run entirely in your browser. No personal data is sent to any server. The only thing stored locally is a simple visit counter, which doesn't include any health information. You can verify this by checking the network tab in your browser's developer tools.
