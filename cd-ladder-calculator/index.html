Definition

A certificate of deposit (CD) is a time deposit offered by banks and credit unions that pays a fixed interest rate for a specified term, ranging from a few months to several years. CDs typically offer higher interest rates than savings accounts in exchange for the depositor agreeing to leave the funds untouched until the maturity date. Early withdrawal results in a penalty, usually equal to several months of interest.

Complete Guide to CD Ladder Strategy

I have used CD ladders as part of my own savings strategy for years, and I consider them one of the most underrated tools for conservative savers who want to maximize returns without taking on market risk. A CD ladder solves the fundamental tension in CD investing: longer terms earn higher rates, but locking money away for 3 to 5 years creates a liquidity problem. By staggering maturity dates, you get regular access to a portion of your funds while keeping the majority invested at competitive longer-term rates.

The concept is straightforward. Instead of putting $25,000 into a single 5-year CD, you split it five ways: $5,000 in a 1-year CD, $5,000 in a 2-year CD, $5,000 in a 3-year CD, $5,000 in a 4-year CD, and $5,000 in a 5-year CD. After the first year, the 1-year CD matures, and you reinvest it into a new 5-year CD. After the second year, the original 2-year CD matures, and you again reinvest into a 5-year CD. Within 4 years, every CD in your ladder is a 5-year CD (earning the highest available rate), but one matures every year, providing annual liquidity.

The Math Behind CD Ladder Returns

CD interest is calculated using the annual percentage yield (APY), which accounts for compounding. The formula for the value at maturity is: Principal x (1 + APY/n)^(n x t), where n is the number of compounding periods per year and t is the term in years. Most banks compound interest daily or monthly, and the difference between the two is small but not zero.

For example, $10,000 invested at 4.75 percent APY for 3 years with monthly compounding grows to $10,000 x (1 + 0.0475/12)^(12 x 3) = $11,525.43. The same amount with daily compounding grows to $10,000 x (1 + 0.0475/365)^(365 x 3) = $11,527.08, a difference of $1.65. The APY rate already accounts for the stated compounding frequency, so when comparing CDs, the APY is the true comparison metric regardless of how often each bank compounds.

The blended rate of a CD ladder is the weighted average APY across all rungs, weighted by the dollar amount and term length. A simple average of the individual APYs does not accurately reflect the ladder's effective return because longer-term CDs earn interest for more periods. The true blended APY takes into account both the deposit amount and the duration of each rung.

CD Ladder Strategies for Different Goals

The classic 5-rung annual ladder with 1, 2, 3, 4, and 5-year terms is the most common, but it is not the only option. Different financial situations call for different ladder structures.

A short-term ladder uses 3, 6, 9, and 12-month CDs. This works well when you need frequent access to funds or when the yield curve is flat (short-term and long-term rates are similar). In the current rate environment where short-term CD rates are competitive with longer terms, a short-term ladder sacrifices very little yield while providing quarterly access to maturing funds. I use this approach for funds that I might need within the next year.

A barbell ladder concentrates money at the shortest and longest terms, skipping the middle. For example: 40 percent in 6-month CDs and 60 percent in 5-year CDs. This maximizes the yield on the long-term portion while keeping a substantial amount accessible in the short term. The trade-off is that you have no maturities in years 2 through 4, creating a liquidity gap. This strategy works well when long-term rates are significantly higher than short-term rates, making the yield premium worth the reduced flexibility.

A bullet ladder concentrates all maturities around a target date. If you know you will need the money in 3 years (for a home down payment, for instance), you might put equal amounts in 1-year, 2-year, and 3-year CDs. As each matures, you park the proceeds in a high-yield savings account until the target date. This approach uses CDs to lock in rates while ensuring all funds are available when needed.

CD Rate History and Current Trends

CD rates have gone through dramatic cycles over the past 40 years. In the early 1980s, 5-year CD rates exceeded 15 percent as the Federal Reserve fought double-digit inflation. Rates declined steadily through the 1990s and 2000s, hitting rock bottom near 0.15 percent for 1-year CDs during the 2020-2021 period when the Fed held rates near zero to support the economy during the pandemic.

The 2022-2023 rate hiking cycle brought CD rates back to levels not seen since before the 2008 financial crisis. As of March 2026, the best nationally available CD rates are approximately: 6-month CDs at 4.25 to 4.50 percent, 1-year CDs at 4.50 to 4.75 percent, 2-year CDs at 4.25 to 4.50 percent, 3-year CDs at 4.25 to 4.50 percent, and 5-year CDs at 4.00 to 4.50 percent. The yield curve is relatively flat, meaning longer terms are not offering a significant premium over shorter terms right now.

When the yield curve is flat, shorter-term ladders become more attractive because you get comparable rates with better liquidity. When the yield curve is steep (long-term rates significantly higher than short-term rates), longer-term ladders capture more of the yield premium. The current flat curve suggests the market expects rates to remain relatively stable or decline modestly over the next few years.

Choosing Where to Buy CDs

CD rates vary significantly across institutions, and shopping around is one of the simplest ways to increase your returns. Online banks and credit unions consistently offer the highest CD rates, often 0.50 to 1.50 percent higher than large national banks. This spread alone can mean hundreds or thousands of dollars in additional interest over the life of a CD ladder.

I recommend checking at least five sources when building a CD ladder: your existing bank (for convenience), two online banks (Ally, Marcus, Discover, Synchrony, and Bread Financial are consistently competitive), a local credit union (many have excellent rates for members), and a brokerage platform like Fidelity, Schwab, or Vanguard (for brokered CDs from multiple issuers). Rate comparison sites like Bankrate, NerdWallet, and DepositAccounts aggregate current rates from hundreds of institutions.

Beyond the rate, consider the early withdrawal penalty, the minimum deposit, the compounding frequency, and the grace period at maturity. A bank offering 0.10 percent higher APY but with a 12-month early withdrawal penalty versus 6 months at a competitor may not be the better deal if there is any chance you will need to break the CD early.

Tax Efficiency and CD Ladders

CD interest is taxed as ordinary income in the year it is earned, not the year the CD matures. For multi-year CDs, the bank reports accrued interest annually on Form 1099-INT, and you owe taxes on that interest even though you have not received the cash. This phantom income creates a small tax drag on your returns.

To calculate the after-tax return, multiply the APY by (1 minus your marginal tax rate). At a 22 percent federal rate and 5 percent state rate, a 4.75 percent CD yields an after-tax return of 4.75 x (1 - 0.27) = 3.47 percent. In a high-tax state like California (13.3 percent top rate), the after-tax return drops further.

Holding CDs in a tax-advantaged account eliminates this tax drag. Traditional IRA CDs defer taxes until withdrawal, and Roth IRA CDs eliminate taxes entirely if you meet the holding period requirements. Many banks and brokerage firms offer IRA CDs specifically designed for retirement accounts. The trade-off is that IRA contributions have annual limits ($7,000 for 2026, $8,000 if over age 50), and early withdrawals from IRAs before age 59.5 incur a 10 percent penalty plus taxes.

CD Ladders vs. Treasury Securities

Treasury securities (T-bills, T-notes, and Treasury bonds) are the main alternative to CDs for low-risk interest income. Treasuries are backed by the full faith and credit of the US government, making them slightly safer than FDIC-insured CDs (which are backed by an insurance fund that could theoretically be depleted in a catastrophic banking crisis, though this has never happened).

Treasuries have a significant tax advantage: the interest is exempt from state and local income taxes. In a state with a 5 percent income tax rate, a Treasury yielding 4.50 percent has the same after-state-tax return as a CD yielding 4.74 percent. In a high-tax state like California or New York, the state tax savings make Treasuries even more attractive relative to CDs.

Treasuries are also more liquid than CDs because they trade on a secondary market. You can sell a Treasury at any time without an early withdrawal penalty, though you may receive more or less than face value depending on current interest rates. If rates have risen since you bought the Treasury, it will sell at a discount (a loss), and if rates have fallen, it will sell at a premium (a gain).

I build ladders using a mix of CDs and Treasuries: CDs when they offer a higher APY (which is common for 1 to 3 year terms), and Treasuries when the state tax benefit makes them more competitive (especially for longer terms in high-tax states).

Reinvestment Strategy When CDs Mature

The reinvestment decision when a rung matures is the ongoing management component of a CD ladder. In the standard approach, you reinvest each maturing CD into a new CD at the longest term in your ladder. If you started with 1 through 5-year terms, every maturing CD gets reinvested into a new 5-year CD. This is the autopilot approach, and it works well in most rate environments.

However, if rates have changed significantly since you built the ladder, you might adjust. If rates have dropped substantially, you might prefer to reinvest in a shorter-term CD rather than locking in a low 5-year rate, anticipating that rates may recover. If rates have risen, you might reinvest in a longer-term CD to lock in the higher rate. These judgment calls add complexity and introduce the risk of being wrong about future rate movements.

I generally recommend the standard approach of reinvesting at the longest term unless the rate environment has changed dramatically (more than 1.5 percent shift since the original purchase). Trying to time interest rate movements is similar to trying to time the stock market: occasionally successful, but more often counterproductive.

Practical Example: Building a $50,000 CD Ladder

Here is a real-world example of how I would build a CD ladder today with $50,000 and current March 2026 rates.

Rung 1: $10,000 in a 6-month CD at 4.40 percent APY (Discover Bank). Matures September 2026. Interest earned: approximately $219.

Rung 2: $10,000 in a 1-year CD at 4.60 percent APY (Marcus by Goldman Sachs). Matures March 2027. Interest earned: approximately $460.

Rung 3: $10,000 in a 2-year CD at 4.35 percent APY (Ally Bank). Matures March 2028. Interest earned: approximately $889.

Rung 4: $10,000 in a 3-year CD at 4.30 percent APY (Bread Financial). Matures March 2029. Interest earned: approximately $1,350.

Rung 5: $10,000 in a 5-year CD at 4.25 percent APY (Synchrony Bank). Matures March 2031. Interest earned: approximately $2,318.

Total interest earned across all five rungs: approximately $5,236 on $50,000 invested. The blended APY across the ladder is approximately 4.38 percent, accounting for the different terms and rates. If I had put the entire $50,000 in a single 5-year CD at 4.25 percent, the interest would be approximately $11,590 over 5 years, but I would have no liquidity for the entire period. The ladder sacrifices about $980 in total interest over 5 years (compared to a single 5-year CD) in exchange for having $10,000 coming available every 6 to 12 months.

Common CD Ladder Mistakes

The most common mistake is not shopping for rates. The difference between 4.00 percent and 4.75 percent on a $10,000 CD over 3 years is about $230, which is real money left on the table by accepting your existing bank's rate without comparison shopping.

Second, ignoring the early withdrawal penalty structure. If you might need funds before maturity, the penalty structure matters. A bank with a 3-month penalty versus a 12-month penalty is much more forgiving of unexpected withdrawals. For the shortest rung of a ladder, consider a no-penalty CD that allows withdrawal at any time after the first week.

Third, setting it and forgetting it without monitoring rates. CD rates change as the Federal Reserve adjusts monetary policy. If rates rise significantly, your maturing rungs should be reinvested at the new higher rates. If you autopilot reinvestments without checking current rates, you might miss better opportunities at competing banks.

Fourth, putting too much money in CDs relative to your emergency fund needs. A CD ladder provides scheduled liquidity, but it does not provide instant liquidity. Keep 3 to 6 months of expenses in a high-yield savings account or money market fund before investing additional savings in CDs. The CD ladder is for funds beyond the emergency reserve.

CD Ladders for Retirees

CD ladders are particularly popular among retirees who want predictable income with zero market risk. A retiree with $200,000 in a 5-rung CD ladder earning an average of 4.5 percent generates about $9,000 per year in interest income. With $40,000 maturing each year, the retiree can take the interest as income and reinvest the principal into a new 5-year rung, or take some principal for living expenses if needed.

For retirees using CDs as an income source, matching the ladder maturity schedule to anticipated income needs is important. If you expect major expenses in year 3 (a home renovation, a new car), weight the ladder so that a larger rung matures that year. This targeted maturity approach is more efficient than breaking a CD early and paying the penalty.

I recommend that retirees hold their CD ladders inside IRA accounts when possible. The tax deferral on Traditional IRA CDs allows the interest to compound without annual tax drag, and Roth IRA CDs generate completely tax-free interest income. Given that CD interest is taxed at ordinary income rates (which can be as high as 37 percent at the federal level), the tax savings from IRA placement are substantial over a multi-decade retirement.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the best CD ladder structure for 2026?+
Given the relatively flat yield curve in early 2026, a short-to-medium ladder works well. I suggest a 5-rung structure with 6-month, 1-year, 18-month, 2-year, and 3-year terms. The rate difference between 3-year and 5-year CDs is small right now, so you give up little yield by staying shorter and maintaining more frequent access. As rates change, you can adjust the structure when each rung matures.
How much money do I need to start a CD ladder?+
You can start a CD ladder with as little as $2,500 to $5,000 if using banks with low minimums ($500 per CD). Many online banks have no minimums at all, allowing you to start even smaller. However, a CD ladder becomes more practical with $10,000 or more, as the interest earned on very small deposits may not justify the effort of managing multiple accounts. For a 5-rung ladder, I suggest at least $2,000 per rung ($10,000 total) to make the returns meaningful.
Should I break a CD to take advantage of higher rates?+
Sometimes. If rates have risen significantly (more than 1 percent) since you purchased a CD, calculate whether the interest earned at the new higher rate over the remaining term exceeds the early withdrawal penalty. For example, if breaking a CD costs 6 months of interest at 3 percent but lets you reinvest at 5 percent for the remaining 2 years, the math favors breaking. Run the numbers for your specific situation using the penalty amount and the rate differential.
Are online bank CDs safe?+
Yes, as long as the bank is FDIC-insured. Online banks like Ally, Marcus, Discover, and Synchrony are all FDIC-insured, and your deposits are protected up to $250,000 per depositor per institution. The higher rates at online banks reflect their lower overhead costs (no physical branches), not higher risk. Verify FDIC membership using the FDIC BankFind tool at fdic.gov before opening any account.
What is a bump-up CD and should I use one in my ladder?+
A bump-up CD (also called a step-up or raise-your-rate CD) allows you to increase your rate once or twice during the term if rates rise. The initial rate is typically 0.10 to 0.30 percent lower than a standard CD of the same term. In a rising rate environment, the bump-up feature has value. In a falling or stable rate environment, the standard CD wins because its initial rate is higher. For the longest rung of a ladder, a bump-up CD provides insurance against missing out on future rate increases.
How often should I review and adjust my CD ladder?+
Review your ladder each time a rung matures, which is the natural decision point for reinvestment. Also review whenever the Federal Reserve changes interest rates, as this directly affects available CD rates. You do not need to make changes at every review. Simply compare current rates to what you are earning and decide whether your reinvestment strategy needs adjustment. An annual portfolio review that includes your CD ladder alongside other investments is sufficient for most people.
Can I build a CD ladder inside a brokerage account?+
Yes, and this is my preferred method for larger ladders. Fidelity, Schwab, and Vanguard all offer brokered CDs from multiple banks through a single brokerage account. The advantages include easy comparison of rates from dozens of issuers, FDIC coverage from each issuing bank (up to $250,000 each), the ability to sell on the secondary market before maturity, and consolidated tax reporting on a single 1099. The main disadvantage is that brokered CD rates are occasionally slightly lower than the best direct-to-bank rates.
What happens if a bank fails while I have CDs there?+
If an FDIC-insured bank fails, the FDIC protects your deposits up to $250,000 per depositor per institution. Typically, another bank acquires the failed bank and honors existing CD terms. If no buyer is found, the FDIC pays depositors directly, usually within a few business days. You receive your principal plus accrued interest up to the date of the failure. Since the creation of the FDIC in 1933, no depositor has ever lost a single cent of FDIC-insured funds.
Is a CD ladder better than I bonds?+
Series I savings bonds offer inflation protection that CDs do not, as their rate adjusts every 6 months based on the Consumer Price Index. However, I bonds have a $10,000 per person annual purchase limit, a 1-year lockup period, and a 3-month interest penalty if redeemed before 5 years. CDs have no purchase limit and offer predictable fixed returns. For most savers, I bonds and CDs serve complementary roles: I bonds for inflation protection on the first $10,000, and CD ladders for larger amounts where predictability is valued.
Do credit unions offer better CD rates than banks?+
Credit unions frequently offer rates that match or beat the best online banks, and occasionally offer promotional rates that are the highest available nationally. Credit union CDs are called share certificates and are insured by the NCUA up to $250,000, equivalent to FDIC insurance. The catch is that you must be eligible for membership, which may require living in a certain area, working for a specific employer, or making a small donation to a qualifying organization. Check credit union rate lists at depositaccounts.com for the best current offers.

Community Questions

QI have $100,000 to invest in CDs. Should I split it across multiple banks for FDIC coverage?
A

With $100,000 you are well within the $250,000 FDIC limit at any single bank, so splitting is not necessary for insurance purposes. However, splitting across 2 to 3 banks lets you cherry-pick the best rate at each term length, since no single bank consistently offers the top rate across all terms. A practical approach: pick the best rate for each rung regardless of which bank offers it.

QRates seem to be peaking. Should I lock in long-term CDs now before they drop?
A

If you believe rates have peaked, locking in longer-term CDs makes sense. A CD ladder lets you do both: invest some money at current long-term rates (protecting against rate drops) while keeping shorter-term rungs available for reinvestment if rates stay high or rise further. This is exactly the scenario where a ladder outperforms an all-or-nothing approach.

QMy bank is offering a 13-month CD at 5.00% APY as a promotional rate. Is this a good deal?
A

A 5.00% APY on a 13-month CD is excellent in the current rate environment where most 1-year CDs are in the 4.50 to 4.75% range. If the early withdrawal penalty is reasonable (3 months or less of interest), this could serve as a strong rung in your ladder. Promotional rates are often the best deals available, just verify the rate is the APY (not the nominal rate) and confirm the penalty terms before committing.
