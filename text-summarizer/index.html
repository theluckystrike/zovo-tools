How Extractive Summarization Works

Extractive summarization is a technique that identifies the most important sentences in a document and pulls them out to form a summary. Unlike abstractive methods that generate new text, extractive methods preserve the original wording exactly. The summary is a subset of the original sentences, selected by importance.

This approach dates back to the 1950s, when Hans Peter Luhn at IBM proposed using word frequency as a proxy for sentence importance. The core idea remains sound: sentences containing frequently used meaningful words are likely to be about the main topics of the text.

Modern extractive summarization adds several refinements. Position-based scoring recognizes that introductory and concluding sentences often carry key information. Length normalization prevents very long sentences from dominating purely because they contain more words. Stop word filtering ensures that common function words like "the," "is," and "of" don't inflate importance scores.

The Scoring Algorithm Explained

This tool implements a multi-factor scoring system. Here is how each sentence in your text gets evaluated:

Term frequency (TF): After removing stop words, the tool counts how often each word appears across the entire document. Each sentence's score starts as the sum of its words' frequencies, normalized by the total word count. This captures what the text is primarily about.

Position weighting: The first 15% and last 10% of sentences get a score boost. In most writing, the opening sentences introduce the topic and the closing sentences wrap up key points. This heuristic works especially well with news articles and academic papers.

Length penalty: Sentences that are extremely short (under 5 words) or very long (over 40 words) are penalized. Very short sentences often lack substance, while extremely long ones may be run-on constructions that are hard to read out of context.

Title keyword boost: If you provide a title or topic, the tool identifies keywords from it and gives bonus weight to sentences containing those terms. This helps focus the summary on the intended subject, especially useful for long documents that cover multiple subtopics.

Final selection: Sentences are ranked by their combined score, and the top N% are selected (based on your slider setting). The selected sentences are then re-ordered to match their original position in the text, preserving the logical flow.

When to Use Extractive Summarization

Research and study: When reading academic papers or lengthy reports, an extractive summary gives you the key points without any risk of misrepresentation. Every sentence comes directly from the source.

Content curation: If you need to preview or vet articles before reading them in full, a quick extractive summary shows you the substance without the filler.

Meeting notes and emails: Long email threads and meeting transcripts often bury important decisions in walls of text. Extractive summarization surfaces the most information-dense sentences.

Writing feedback: Running your own writing through an extractive summarizer shows you which sentences the algorithm considers most important. If the summary misses your main point, your emphasis may need work.

Getting Better Summaries

Provide a title. The optional title field significantly improves results. It tells the algorithm which terms matter most, so the summary focuses on sentences relevant to your topic of interest.

Use the right summary length. For dense, technical text, a longer summary (35-50%) preserves more nuance. For straightforward articles, 15-25% usually captures the gist effectively.

Clean your input. Remove headers, footers, navigation text, image captions, and other boilerplate before summarizing. These fragments can confuse the frequency analysis and dilute your results.

Longer inputs work better. Extractive summarization needs enough text to identify patterns. A paragraph won't give meaningful results. Aim for at least 300-500 words of input for useful output.

Limitations of This Approach

Extractive summarization has clear boundaries. It cannot paraphrase, generalize, or combine ideas from multiple sentences. The summary is always a subset of original sentences, which means it can occasionally feel disjointed — two selected sentences might refer to concepts explained in sentences that were not selected.

The term frequency approach works best with expository text — articles, reports, essays, documentation. It performs less well with narrative fiction, poetry, dialogue-heavy text, or highly technical material where jargon frequency doesn't map to importance.

This tool processes English text. The built-in stop word list, abbreviation handling, and sentence tokenization are optimized for English. Other languages may produce less accurate results.

Extractive vs. Abstractive Summarization

Abstractive summarization, used by large language models, generates entirely new sentences that capture the meaning of the source text. It can paraphrase, combine ideas, and produce more fluid summaries. However, it can also introduce errors — sometimes called hallucinations — where the summary states something the original text never said.

Extractive summarization avoids this problem entirely. Every sentence in the output exists verbatim in the input. For applications where accuracy and traceability matter — legal documents, medical texts, academic research — extractive methods offer a reliability advantage.

The two approaches are not mutually exclusive. Many production summarization systems use extractive methods as a first pass to identify important content, then apply abstractive techniques to polish the output. This tool focuses on the extractive step, giving you a faithful representation of the source material's key sentences.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is extractive text summarization?

Extractive summarization selects the most important sentences from the original text and presents them as a summary. Unlike abstractive summarization, it does not generate new sentences — every sentence in the summary appears verbatim in the source text.

How does the scoring algorithm work?

Each sentence receives a score based on multiple factors: term frequency of its words (with stop words removed), its position in the text (first and last sentences are weighted higher), sentence length (very short or very long sentences are penalized), and the presence of title keywords if a title is provided.

Does this tool use any AI or external APIs?

No. This tool uses a pure algorithmic approach built from scratch in JavaScript. There are no API calls, no machine learning models, and no data transmission. Everything runs locally in your browser.

Is my text stored or sent anywhere?

No. All processing happens entirely in your browser. Your text is never uploaded to any server, stored in any database, or transmitted over the network.

How accurate is extractive summarization compared to AI summarization?

Extractive summarization produces reliable summaries because it uses actual sentences from the text, so there is no risk of hallucination or factual errors. However, the summaries may read less fluently than those produced by large language models, since sentences are selected rather than generated.
