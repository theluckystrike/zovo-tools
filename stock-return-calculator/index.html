Summary

Understanding Stock Returns

Stock returns come from two sources: capital gains (the increase in share price) and dividends (cash payments made by the company to shareholders). Total return combines both components to give the complete picture of your investment performance.

Capital Gains

Capital gains occur when you sell a stock for more than you paid. If you bought shares at $50 and sold at $72, your capital gain per share is $22. Capital losses occur when the sell price is lower than the buy price. Short-term capital gains (held less than one year) are typically taxed at higher rates than long-term gains.

Dividend Returns

Dividends are regular cash payments made by companies to shareholders from their profits. Dividend-paying stocks provide income even when the stock price does not appreciate. Historically, dividends have contributed a substantial portion of total stock market returns. The average S&P 500 dividend yield has been around 2-3% annually.

Annualized Return

Annualized return converts a total return earned over any period into an equivalent annual rate. This allows you to fairly compare investments held for different lengths of time. A 44% total return over 3 years is equivalent to approximately 12.9% per year on an annualized basis, accounting for compounding.

Impact of Fees

Trading commissions and fees reduce your net return. While many brokerages now offer commission-free trading for stocks, other fees like fund expense ratios, advisory fees, and account maintenance charges can add up. Even small fees compound over time and significantly impact long-term returns.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is total stock return calculated?

Total return includes both capital gains and dividends. The formula is: ((Sell Price - Buy Price + Dividends Per Share) / Buy Price) x 100. This gives the complete picture of investment performance, not just the price change.

What is annualized return?

Annualized return converts total return to an equivalent yearly rate. It uses the formula ((1 + Total Return)^(1/Years) - 1) x 100, accounting for compound growth. This makes it possible to compare investments held for different durations.

Should I include dividends in my return calculation?

Yes. Dividends are a significant part of total return, especially for long-term investments. Historically, dividends have contributed roughly 40% of the S&P 500 total return. Excluding them understates actual performance.

What is a good annual return for stocks?

The historical average annual return of the S&P 500 is roughly 10% before inflation, about 7% after. Individual stock returns vary widely. Consistently beating the market average over long periods is considered excellent performance.
