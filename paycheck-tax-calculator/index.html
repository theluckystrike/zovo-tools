How Federal Income Tax Brackets Work

The United States uses a progressive income tax system, meaning different portions of your income are taxed at different rates. Many people assume that earning enough to enter a higher tax bracket means all their income gets taxed at that higher rate. That is a common misconception. In reality, only the income within each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate.

For 2024/2025, there are seven federal income tax brackets ranging from 10% to 37%. When you earn income, the first portion fills the lowest bracket and gets taxed at the lowest rate. As your income increases, successive portions fill higher brackets.

Consider a single filer who earns $85,000. Before applying tax brackets, the standard deduction of $14,600 is subtracted, leaving $70,400 in taxable income. The tax calculation works as follows. The first $11,600 is taxed at 10%, producing $1,160 in tax. The next $35,550 (from $11,601 to $47,150) is taxed at 12%, producing $4,266. The remaining $23,250 (from $47,151 to $70,400) is taxed at 22%, producing $5,115. The total federal income tax comes to $10,541, which represents an effective tax rate of roughly 12.4% on the full $85,000 gross salary, even though the marginal (highest) bracket is 22%.

For married couples filing jointly, the bracket thresholds are roughly double the single filer thresholds. This means a couple with a combined income of $150,000 will often pay less total tax than two single filers each earning $75,000, because more of their combined income stays in lower brackets. Head of Household filers, typically single parents or those supporting dependents, receive wider brackets than Single filers but narrower than Married Filing Jointly.

Understanding this progressive structure is essential for smart financial planning. When you hear someone say they are "in the 22% bracket," they are referring to their marginal rate. Their effective rate, which represents the true overall percentage they pay, is always lower. State-by-State Tax Overview

Beyond federal income tax, most states impose their own income tax that further reduces your take-home pay. State income tax systems vary dramatically. Some states use a flat tax rate applied equally to all income levels, while others use their own progressive bracket systems with multiple rates.

Nine states impose no income tax on earned wages. If you live in Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, or Wyoming, your paycheck avoids state income tax entirely. New Hampshire historically taxed interest and dividend income but has been phasing out that tax. For workers in these states, take-home pay can be meaningfully higher compared to colleagues earning the same salary in a high-tax state.

The state you live in can create a substantial difference in annual take-home pay. Someone earning $100,000 in Texas keeps roughly $4,950 more per year compared to someone earning the same salary in California, purely from the state tax difference. That works out to about $190 more per bi-weekly paycheck. This calculator applies approximate state tax rates when you select your state, giving you a realistic picture of your actual take-home pay.

FICA Taxes Explained

FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act, and it funds two critical programs: Social Security and Medicare. Unlike federal income tax, FICA taxes are not based on tax brackets. They apply as flat percentages with specific caps and thresholds.

Social Security tax is 6.2% of your gross earnings, applied to the first $168,600 of income for 2024. Once your cumulative earnings for the year reach $168,600, you stop paying Social Security tax on additional income. This means the maximum Social Security tax you can pay as an employee is $10,453.20 per year. Your employer pays an additional 6.2%, but that comes from their budget and does not reduce your paycheck.

Medicare tax is 1.45% of all your gross earnings, with no income cap. Unlike Social Security, you pay Medicare tax on every dollar you earn. The Affordable Care Act introduced an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% on earnings exceeding $200,000 for single filers or $250,000 for married filing jointly. This additional tax only applies to the employee and is not matched by the employer.

For a concrete example, consider someone earning $250,000 as a single filer. Their Social Security tax is 6.2% of $168,600, totaling $10,453.20. Their regular Medicare tax is 1.45% of $250,000, totaling $3,625. Their Additional Medicare tax is 0.9% of $50,000 (the amount over $200,000), totaling $450. Their total FICA contribution for the year is $14,528.20, which works out to roughly $559 per bi-weekly paycheck.

FICA taxes are calculated on your gross pay before the standard deduction is applied. Pre-tax 401(k) contributions reduce your income for federal and state tax purposes but do not reduce FICA taxes. This is an important distinction that means contributing to a 401(k) does not lower your Social Security and Medicare obligations.

How Pre-Tax Deductions Save You Money

Pre-tax deductions are subtracted from your gross income before federal and state income taxes are calculated. By lowering your taxable income, these deductions reduce the amount of tax you owe, giving you an immediate tax benefit with every paycheck.

The most common pre-tax deductions are traditional 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums, Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions, and Flexible Spending Account (FSA) contributions. Each of these comes out of your paycheck before your employer calculates withholding taxes.

Consider a single filer earning $80,000 per year who contributes $6,000 annually to a traditional 401(k) and pays $3,600 in health insurance premiums. Without these deductions, their taxable income (after the standard deduction) would be $65,400. With the deductions, their taxable income drops to $55,800. The federal tax savings from this $9,600 reduction depends on their marginal bracket. At the 22% rate, they save approximately $2,112 in federal taxes. If they live in a state with a 5% tax rate, they save an additional $480 in state taxes. The total annual tax savings come to roughly $2,592, or about $100 per bi-weekly paycheck.

Health Savings Accounts offer a triple tax advantage that makes them especially. Contributions are pre-tax, the money grows tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are also tax-free. For 2024, the HSA contribution limit is $4,150 for individuals and $8,300 for families. If you are 55 or older, you can contribute an additional $1,000 catch-up amount.

Flexible Spending Accounts work similarly but with a key difference: FSA funds generally must be used within the plan year or you lose them (the "use it or lose it" rule). Some employers offer a grace period of up to 2.5 months or allow you to carry over up to $640 into the next year. The 2024 FSA contribution limit is $3,200 for healthcare FSAs.

that pre-tax deductions lower your federal and state income tax but do not reduce FICA taxes. Social Security and Medicare taxes are calculated on your gross pay regardless of pre-tax deductions. This means you still pay the full 7.65% FICA on your entire salary even with significant pre-tax deductions.

401(k) Contribution Strategies and Limits

Your 401(k) is one of the most impactful tools for both building retirement savings and reducing your current tax burden. For 2024, the employee contribution limit for 401(k), 403(b), and most 457 plans is $23,000. Workers aged 50 and older can make an additional catch-up contribution of $7,500, bringing their total to $30,500.

If your employer offers matching contributions, contributing enough to capture the full match should be your first priority. Employer matches are essentially free money. A common match formula is 50% of your contributions up to 6% of your salary. For someone earning $80,000, contributing 6% ($4,800) would yield a $2,400 employer match, giving you $7,200 total going into your retirement account.

The choice between traditional (pre-tax) 401(k) and Roth (post-tax) 401(k) depends on your current versus expected future tax situation. Traditional 401(k) contributions reduce your taxable income today, lowering your current tax bill. You will pay income tax when you withdraw the money in retirement. Roth 401(k) contributions are made with after-tax dollars, meaning no current tax break, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free.

If you are currently in a high tax bracket and expect to be in a lower bracket during retirement, the traditional 401(k) typically makes more sense. If you are early in your career and in a lower bracket, the Roth 401(k) may be advantageous since you are paying taxes now at a lower rate and avoiding taxes later when your rate might be higher.

Many financial planners recommend splitting contributions between traditional and Roth 401(k) to create tax diversification in retirement. This gives you both taxable and tax-free income sources, allowing flexibility in managing your tax bill during retirement years. This calculator lets you model both traditional and Roth contributions to see how each affects your current take-home pay.

Beyond the employee contribution limit, the total 401(k) annual contribution limit including employer contributions is $69,000 for 2024 ($76,500 for those 50 and older). This total limit is rarely reached by most workers but can be relevant for highly compensated employees whose employers offer generous profit-sharing or matching formulas.

Understanding Your Paycheck Stub

Every paycheck you receive should include a pay stub (also called an earnings statement) that itemizes your gross pay, deductions, and net pay. Understanding each line item helps you verify accuracy and make informed decisions about your benefits and withholding.

The gross pay section shows your total earnings for the pay period. For salaried employees, this is your annual salary divided by the number of pay periods. For hourly employees, it reflects your hours worked multiplied by your hourly rate, plus any overtime at 1.5x the regular rate.

The deductions section typically breaks down into statutory deductions (federal tax, state tax, Social Security, Medicare) and voluntary deductions (401k, health insurance, dental, vision, HSA, FSA, life insurance, disability insurance, and others). Each deduction should match what you elected during open enrollment or what is required by law.

Common paycheck errors to watch for include incorrect filing status being used for withholding, deductions being applied at the wrong amount, overtime hours not being compensated at the correct rate, and benefit changes not taking effect when expected. If you notice a discrepancy between this calculator's estimate and your actual paycheck, check your W-4 withholding elections and benefit enrollment details with your HR department.

Salary vs Hourly Compensation

Whether you receive a salary or hourly wage affects your paycheck in important ways beyond just the amount. Salaried employees typically receive the same gross pay each period regardless of the number of hours worked. Hourly employees see their gross pay fluctuate based on actual hours.

To convert between the two, divide an annual salary by 2,080 (the number of working hours in a year assuming 40 hours per week for 52 weeks). A $75,000 salary equals approximately $36.06 per hour. Conversely, multiply an hourly rate by 2,080 to get the annual equivalent. A $25 per hour wage equals $52,000 per year at full-time hours.

For hourly workers, overtime significantly impacts take-home pay. Under the Fair Labor Standards Act, non-exempt employees must receive at least 1.5 times their regular rate for hours exceeding 40 in a workweek. That extra income pushes more of your earnings into higher tax brackets, which means the tax rate on overtime dollars may be higher than your regular pay. This calculator uses your base hourly rate and regular hours to estimate standard take-home pay, but keep in mind that overtime weeks will look different.

Pay frequency also matters for budgeting purposes. Bi-weekly pay (every two weeks, 26 paychecks per year) is the most common frequency in the United States. Semi-monthly pay (twice per month, 24 paychecks per year) results in slightly larger checks. Weekly pay provides more frequent but smaller checks. Monthly pay means fewer, larger deposits. Your annual take-home pay total remains the same regardless of frequency, but per-paycheck amounts will differ.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate my take-home pay from my salary

Start with your gross annual salary. Subtract pre-tax deductions like 401(k) and health insurance. Subtract the standard deduction for your filing status ($14,600 single, $29,200 married, $21,900 HOH). Apply federal tax brackets progressively to the remaining taxable income. Calculate FICA taxes (6.2% Social Security on the first $168,600 plus 1.45% Medicare on all income). Subtract your state income tax. Subtract post-tax deductions. The result is your annual take-home pay. Divide by your number of pay periods for the per-paycheck amount.

What percentage of my paycheck goes to taxes

For most American workers, between 20% and 35% of gross pay goes to combined taxes and FICA. Someone earning $60,000 in Texas (no state tax) with no deductions can expect about 22-24% going to taxes. The same salary in California might see 27-30% going to taxes due to the additional state tax. Higher earners face higher percentages because more income falls into upper federal brackets. The calculator above gives you the exact percentage for your specific situation.

How much is a $100,000 salary after taxes

A single filer earning $100,000 with no pre-tax deductions will pay approximately $14,260 in federal income tax, $7,650 in FICA taxes, and a variable amount in state tax depending on location. In a no-tax state like Texas, take-home pay is roughly $78,090 per year or about $3,003 per bi-weekly paycheck. In a state like California with an effective rate around 5.5%, take-home pay drops to approximately $72,590 or $2,792 bi-weekly.

Does contributing to a 401(k) really lower my taxes

Yes, traditional 401(k) contributions directly reduce your federal and state taxable income. If you are in the 22% federal bracket and contribute $500 per month ($6,000 per year), you save approximately $1,320 in federal taxes annually. If your state tax rate is 5%, that is another $300 saved. Your total annual tax savings from a $6,000 contribution comes to about $1,620, meaning the effective out-of-pocket cost of that $6,000 contribution is only $4,380. You will pay taxes on 401(k) withdrawals during retirement.

What is the difference between marginal and effective tax rate

Your marginal tax rate is the rate applied to your last (highest) dollar of income. Your effective tax rate is the overall percentage of your total income paid in taxes. Because the U.S. uses progressive brackets, your effective rate is always lower than your marginal rate. For example, a single filer with $80,000 in taxable income has a marginal rate of 22% but an effective federal rate of about 13.7%. The marginal rate matters for decisions about additional income, while the effective rate reflects your overall tax burden.

Why is my actual paycheck different from this calculator's estimate

Several factors can cause differences. Your employer may withhold taxes based on your W-4 elections, which can differ from your actual tax liability. You may have additional deductions like dental insurance, vision insurance, life insurance, disability insurance, union dues, or garnishments that are not included here. Local city or county taxes exist in some areas. Also, your employer may use slightly different withholding tables. This calculator provides a close estimate based on standard tax formulas and is best used for planning purposes.

Should I adjust my W-4 withholding

If you consistently receive large tax refunds (over $1,000), you may be having too much withheld from each paycheck. Reducing your withholding puts more money in your pocket throughout the year. Conversely, if you owed a large amount at tax time, increasing withholding prevents a surprise bill. Life changes like marriage, having a child, buying a home, or starting a side job all warrant reviewing your W-4. The IRS provides a Tax Withholding Estimator at irs.gov that can help you fine-tune your withholding.

How does overtime affect my paycheck taxes

Overtime pay is taxed the same way as regular pay. It does not get taxed at a special higher rate. Because overtime increases your total earnings, it may push more of your income into a higher federal tax bracket. Your employer's withholding system may withhold at a higher rate on the overtime portion because it projects your annual earnings based on that larger paycheck. The actual tax you owe is determined by your total annual income when you file your return.

What is the Social Security wage base and why does it matter

The Social Security wage base for 2024 is $168,600. This is the maximum amount of earnings subject to the 6.2% Social Security tax. Once your cumulative earnings for the year exceed this threshold, you stop paying Social Security tax on additional income. For high earners, this means paychecks later in the year may be noticeably larger because the Social Security deduction disappears. The wage base is adjusted annually for inflation.

Is this calculator suitable for self-employed individuals

This calculator is primarily for W-2 employees. Self-employed individuals face a different tax situation because they pay both the employee and employer portions of FICA (totaling 15.3%) and may also owe self-employment tax. Self-employed workers can deduct half of their self-employment tax as an above-the-line deduction. If you are self-employed, this calculator can still give a rough estimate, but you should account for the additional FICA burden and potential quarterly estimated tax payments.

How do I my take-home pay

Several strategies can help. First, make sure your W-4 withholding is accurate so you are not overpaying throughout the year. Second, contribute to pre-tax accounts like a traditional 401(k) and HSA to reduce taxable income. Third, ensure you are taking advantage of all employer-sponsored benefits that come out pre-tax. Fourth, if you have flexibility in where you live, consider states with no income tax. Fifth, review your benefit elections annually during open enrollment to confirm you are not paying for coverage you do not need.

How often should I recalculate my paycheck estimate

You should recalculate whenever significant changes occur. These include annual tax bracket adjustments (which happen every year due to inflation indexing), salary raises or promotions, changes in filing status (marriage, divorce), changes in deduction amounts (increasing or decreasing 401k contributions), moving to a different state, or changes in benefit elections during open enrollment. Checking at the start of each calendar year is a good habit.

A payroll tax is a tax that an employer withholds and pays on behalf of their employees. The tax is usually calculated as a percentage of the salaries that employers pay their staff. Payroll taxes generally fall into two categories: deductions from an employee's wages and taxes paid by the employer based on the employee's wages. In the United States, the largest payroll taxes are the 12.4% Social Security tax and the 2.9% Medicare tax (combined as FICA), split equally between employers and employees.
