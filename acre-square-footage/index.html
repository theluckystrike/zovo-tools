History of the Acre

The acre is one of the oldest units of land measurement still in everyday use. Its origins stretch back to medieval England, and its definition has been shaped by farming, legal codes, and international standardization over the centuries.

~1200 AD
The word "acre" comes from the Old English "aecer," meaning open field. It was originally defined as the amount of land a yoke of oxen could plow in a single day under typical soil conditions.
1300s
English statute law standardized the acre as a strip of land 1 furlong (660 feet) long and 1 chain (66 feet) wide. The product 660 times 66 gives us the familiar 43,560 square feet.
1593
The English Parliament formally codified the acre under the Weights and Measures Act, permanently tying it to the statute mile and rod system.
1795
France introduced the metric system, with the hectare (10,000 square meters) as its primary unit of land measurement. Over the following two centuries, most nations transitioned away from the acre.
1959
The international yard and pound agreement defined the yard as exactly 0.9144 meters, which fixed the acre at precisely 4,046.8564224 square meters.
Present Day
The acre remains the standard land measurement unit in the United States, the United Kingdom, and several former British colonies. It is used daily in real estate, farming, and forestry.

How to Measure Land

Whether you are buying a residential lot, planning a garden, or surveying a farm, knowing how to measure land accurately matters. Here are the most common methods used today.

GPS survey. Professional surveyors use GPS receivers that pinpoint boundary corners to within centimeters. This is the gold standard for legal property boundaries and is required for most real estate closings.

Measuring wheel. For smaller parcels, a measuring wheel lets you walk the perimeter and record the distance traveled. Multiply length by width for rectangular lots, or break irregular shapes into triangles and rectangles.

Satellite imagery. Services like Google Earth Pro include a polygon tool that lets you trace property boundaries and calculate enclosed area. This method gives rough estimates suitable for planning but not for legal descriptions.

County plat maps. Every county recorder's office maintains plat maps that show the exact dimensions and acreage of every recorded parcel. These are the most authoritative source outside of a fresh survey.

Smartphone apps. Modern GPS apps can track your path as you walk a boundary and estimate the enclosed acreage. Accuracy is typically within 2 to 5 percent, which works well for informal estimates of open land.

Acres in Real Estate

Acreage is one of the first numbers you see in any land listing. Understanding how acre measurements translate to actual usable space is critical whether you are buying a home, investing in land, or developing a property.

Residential zoning. Most municipalities set minimum lot sizes in their zoning codes. A typical R-1 zone might require 0.25 to 0.5 acres per dwelling unit. Knowing the exact square footage of your parcel tells you whether you can subdivide, add an accessory dwelling unit, or build a larger structure.

Price per acre. Raw land is almost always priced on a per-acre basis. In rural areas of the Midwest, agricultural land may sell for $3,000 to $8,000 per acre. In suburban areas near major cities, the same acre could cost $50,000 to $200,000 or more. Converting to price per square foot makes it easier to compare land to finished lots.

Setback calculations. Building setbacks are measured in feet from property lines. If you have a 0.25-acre lot (about 104 x 105 feet) with 25-foot front and rear setbacks and 10-foot side setbacks, your buildable footprint is roughly 54 x 85 feet, or 4,590 square feet. That is less than half the total lot area.

Lot coverage limits. Many codes cap lot coverage at 30 to 50 percent of the parcel. On a quarter-acre lot (10,890 sq ft), a 40% coverage limit means your building footprint cannot exceed 4,356 square feet. Converting acres to square feet lets you check compliance quickly.

Flood zone mapping. FEMA flood maps show zones in terms of geographic boundaries, but lenders evaluate flood risk based on the percentage of your parcel that falls within a flood zone. Knowing your exact acreage helps you calculate that percentage and understand your insurance requirements.

Acres in Farming and Agriculture

The acre has been tied to farming since its origin, and it remains the standard unit for agricultural land in the United States. Here is how farmers and ranchers use acreage measurements in practice.

Crop yield. Agricultural productivity is measured in bushels per acre. The US national average corn yield is approximately 183 bushels per acre. Converting between acres and hectares is necessary when comparing US yields to international data, which is reported in metric tons per hectare.

Irrigation planning. An acre-foot is the volume of water needed to cover one acre to a depth of one foot, which equals 325,851 gallons. This unit is standard for water rights and irrigation allocations throughout the western United States.

Grazing capacity. The USDA measures grazing land in Animal Unit Months per acre. In good rangeland, one acre might support one cow-calf pair for two months. In arid land, you might need 20 or more acres per AUM. Knowing exact acreage is necessary for sustainable stocking rates.

Fertilizer application. Fertilizer rates are calculated per acre. A standard recommendation of 150 pounds of nitrogen per acre means you need to know your exact field size to order the right amount.

Government programs. USDA programs like crop insurance, Conservation Reserve Program payments, and farm subsidies are all calculated on a per-acre basis. Accurate acreage reporting is required for enrollment and compliance.

Step-by-Step Conversion Guide

If you need to do acre-to-square-foot conversions by hand, or just want to understand the math behind this calculator, follow these steps.

Acres to square feet. Multiply the number of acres by 43,560.

2.5 acres x 43,560 = 108,900 sq ft

Square feet to acres. Divide the square footage by 43,560.

75,000 sq ft / 43,560 = 1.722 acres

Acres to hectares. Multiply the number of acres by 0.404686.

10 acres x 0.404686 = 4.047 hectares

Hectares to acres. Multiply hectares by 2.47105.

3 hectares x 2.47105 = 7.413 acres

Square feet to square meters. Multiply square feet by 0.092903. For example, 43,560 square feet (one acre) times 0.092903 equals 4,046.86 square meters.

Irregular lots. For non-rectangular parcels, break the shape into triangles and rectangles. Calculate the area of each piece in square feet, add them together, then divide by 43,560 to get acres.
