Wikipedia Definition

Compound interest is the addition of interest to the principal sum of a loan or deposit, or in other words, interest on principal plus interest. It is the result of reinvesting or retaining interest that would otherwise be paid out, so that interest in the next period is then earned on the principal sum plus previously accumulated interest.

1. Simple Interest vs. Compound Interest

The difference between simple and compound interest is the single most important concept in personal finance. Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal. Compound interest is calculated on the principal plus all interest earned so far.

Here is a concrete example. You invest $10,000 at 5% annual interest:

YearSimple Interest BalanceCompound Interest BalanceDifference
0$10,000$10,000$0
5$12,500$12,763$263
10$15,000$16,289$1,289
15$17,500$20,789$3,289
20$20,000$26,533$6,533
30$25,000$43,219$18,219
40$30,000$70,400$40,400

With simple interest, you earn exactly $500 every year. With compound interest, you earn $500 in year one, then $525 in year two (because your balance is now $10,500), then $551.25 in year three, and so on. The longer the time period, the more dramatic the compounding effect.

2. The Compound Interest Formula

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt)

Where:
A = Final amount (principal + interest)
P = Principal (initial investment)
r = Annual interest rate (decimal: 5% = 0.05)
n = Compounding periods per year
t = Number of years

Compounding Frequencies

  • Annual: n = 1
  • Semi-annual: n = 2
  • Quarterly: n = 4
  • Monthly: n = 12
  • Daily: n = 365
  • Continuous: A = P * e^(rt)

Worked Example

You invest $5,000 at 6% compounded monthly for 15 years:

A = 5000(1 + 0.06/12)^(12 * 15)
A = 5000(1.005)^180
A = 5000 * 2.4541
A = $12,270.47

Your $5,000 grew to $12,270.47. Total interest earned: $7,270.47 (145% of your original investment).

3. The Rule of 72

Divide 72 by the annual interest rate to estimate doubling time.

The rule also estimates fee impact: a 1% fee on a 7% return means 6% effective. At 7%, money doubles in 10.3 years. At 6%, it doubles in 12 years. That 1% fee costs you nearly 2 extra years per doubling.

6. The Early Start Advantage

Person A invests $200/month from age 22-32 (10 years, $24,000 total), then stops. Person B invests $200/month from age 32-62 (30 years, $72,000 total). Both earn 7%.

7. Compound Interest in Retirement Accounts

401(k) Plans

Tax-advantaged retirement accounts with 2026 contribution limit of $23,500 ($31,000 if 50+). Employer matches of 50-100% on the first 3-6% of salary provide a guaranteed return that amplifies compounding.

Roth IRA

After-tax contributions with tax-free growth and withdrawals. 2026 limit: $7,000 ($8,000 if 50+). For someone in their 20s-30s expecting higher future tax rates, a Roth lets decades of compound growth escape taxation entirely.

8. Compound Interest Working Against You: Debt

Credit Card Debt Example

$5,000 balance at 20% APR with minimum payment (2% of balance, minimum $25):

  • Time to pay off: 28 years and 5 months
  • Total interest paid: $8,397
  • Total amount paid: $13,397

With a fixed $200/month payment instead:

  • Time to pay off: 2 years and 8 months
  • Total interest paid: $1,397
  • Savings: $7,000 in interest, 25 years of payments

Student Loans

Federal student loans accrue interest daily. A $30,000 unsubsidized loan at 6.53% accruing for 4 years of school adds approximately $7,836 in capitalized interest before your first payment, raising the principal to $37,836.

9. CAGR: Compound Annual Growth Rate

CAGR = (Ending Value / Beginning Value)^(1/n) - 1

If $10,000 grew to $25,000 over 10 years: CAGR = (2.5)^(0.1) - 1 = 9.6%. The S&P 500's CAGR from 1926 to 2025 is approximately 10.3%, despite including the Great Depression, 2008 crisis, and COVID-19 crash.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is compound interest?

Compound interest is interest calculated on both the initial principal and accumulated interest. When you earn 5% on $10,000 in year one ($500), in year two you earn 5% on $10,500 ($525). Each period builds on the last, creating exponential growth.

What is the compound interest formula?

A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt), where A is the final amount, P is principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is compounding periods per year, and t is years. For $10,000 at 5% compounded monthly for 10 years: A = 10000(1 + 0.05/12)^(120) = $16,470.09.

How often should interest compound?

More frequent is better, but the difference between monthly and daily is minimal. For $10,000 at 5% for 10 years, monthly yields $16,470 and daily yields $16,487. Focus on the interest rate rather than compounding frequency.

What is the Rule of 72?

Divide 72 by the annual rate to estimate doubling time. At 6%: 12 years. At 8%: 9 years. At 10%: 7.2 years. Most accurate between 2-12% rates.

How much does $10,000 grow over 30 years?

At 7% (inflation-adjusted S&P 500 average): $76,123. At 10% (nominal): $174,494. At 5%: $43,219. Adding $200/month at 7% brings the total to approximately $283,382.

What is the difference between compound and simple interest?

Simple interest: calculated on principal only. Compound interest: calculated on principal plus accumulated interest. Over 20 years at 5%, $10,000 becomes $20,000 (simple) vs $26,533 (compound). The gap widens with time.

Does compound interest work against me with debt?

Yes. Credit card debt at 20% APR compounds monthly. A $5,000 balance with minimum payments takes 28+ years and costs $13,397 total. Fixed $200/month payments: under 3 years, $6,397 total. Saves $7,000.

When should I start investing?

As early as possible. Investing $200/month from age 22-32 (then stopping) yields more at 62 than investing $200/month from 32-62. The extra compounding time outweighs the extra contributions.
