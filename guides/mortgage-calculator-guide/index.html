What Makes Up a Mortgage Payment

Your monthly mortgage payment is more than just repaying the money you borrowed. The total payment, commonly abbreviated PITI, consists of four components: Principal, Interest, Taxes, and Insurance. Understanding each piece helps you predict your real costs and make informed decisions about how much home you can afford.

Principal

Principal is the portion of your payment that reduces the loan balance. On a new 30-year mortgage, a surprisingly small fraction of your early payments goes toward principal. Over time, as the balance shrinks, the principal portion grows. This shift is the core mechanism of loan amortization, and it explains why paying extra toward principal in the early years can dramatically reduce total interest paid.

Interest

Interest is the cost of borrowing money, expressed as an annual percentage rate (APR). Your lender calculates monthly interest by dividing the annual rate by 12 and multiplying by the outstanding balance. On a $300,000 loan at 6.5%, the first month's interest is $300,000 x 0.065 / 12 = $1,625. That means only $271 of a $1,896 monthly payment reduces the principal in month one.

Property Taxes

Property taxes are assessed by local governments based on the assessed value of your home. Rates vary significantly by location, from under 0.3% in Hawaii to over 2% in New Jersey and Illinois. On a $350,000 home in a 1.2% tax area, you would pay $4,200 per year, or $350 per month. Most lenders collect tax payments as part of your monthly payment and hold them in an escrow account.

Homeowners Insurance

Homeowners insurance protects against damage, theft, and liability. Lenders require it as a condition of the mortgage. Average annual premiums vary by state and coverage level, but $1,200 to $2,400 per year ($100 to $200 per month) is typical for standard coverage. Homes in flood zones, hurricane-prone areas, or wildfire regions may face significantly higher premiums.

The Mortgage Payment Formula

The standard formula for calculating a fixed-rate mortgage payment is:

M = P [ r(1+r)^n ] / [ (1+r)^n - 1 ]

Where:
  M = Monthly payment (principal + interest only)
  P = Principal loan amount
  r = Monthly interest rate (annual rate / 12)
  n = Total number of payments (years x 12)

Worked example for a $300,000 loan at 6.5% over 30 years:

P = 300,000
r = 0.065 / 12 = 0.005417
n = 30 x 12 = 360

M = 300,000 [ 0.005417(1.005417)^360 ] / [ (1.005417)^360 - 1 ]
M = 300,000 [ 0.005417 x 6.9913 ] / [ 6.9913 - 1 ]
M = 300,000 [ 0.03788 ] / [ 5.9913 ]
M = 300,000 x 0.006321
M = $1,896.20

Understanding Amortization

Amortization describes how each monthly payment is split between interest and principal over the life of the loan. The split changes every month. Early payments are heavily weighted toward interest; later payments are heavily weighted toward principal. This is not a lender trick; it is simply how compound interest on a declining balance works mathematically.

Fixed-Rate vs. Adjustable-Rate Mortgages

Fixed-Rate Mortgages

A fixed-rate mortgage locks your interest rate for the entire loan term. The rate you agree to at closing is the rate you pay in year 1 and year 30. This predictability makes budgeting straightforward. The most common terms are 30 years and 15 years, though 10-year and 20-year options exist.

The trade-off is that fixed rates are typically higher than the introductory rate on an ARM. In rising rate environments, however, a fixed rate protects you from future increases.

Adjustable-Rate Mortgages (ARMs)

An ARM starts with a fixed rate for an initial period and then adjusts periodically based on a market index. A "5/1 ARM" means the rate is fixed for 5 years and adjusts every 1 year after that. A "7/6 ARM" means fixed for 7 years with adjustments every 6 months.

ARMs have caps that limit how much the rate can change at each adjustment and over the life of the loan. A typical cap structure is 2/2/5: maximum 2% increase at the first adjustment, 2% per subsequent adjustment, and 5% over the life of the loan.

How Much Home Can You Afford

The 28/36 Rule

The 28/36 rule is a lending guideline (not a law) that suggests your monthly housing costs should not exceed 28% of your gross monthly income, and your total monthly debt payments (housing plus car loans, student loans, credit cards) should not exceed 36%.

Down Payment Strategies

Conventional wisdom says to put 20% down, and there are good reasons: you avoid PMI, you start with equity, and your monthly payment is lower. On a $350,000 home, 20% means $70,000 up front. That is not realistic for many first-time buyers.

Alternatives include FHA loans (3.5% down, but with mandatory mortgage insurance for the life of the loan unless you refinance), conventional 97 loans (3% down with PMI until you reach 20% equity), VA loans (0% down for eligible military service members), and USDA loans (0% down for eligible rural properties).

A 10% down payment on a $350,000 home means $35,000 up front and a $315,000 loan. At 6.5% over 30 years, the monthly P&I is $1,991 instead of $1,770 with 20% down. The PMI adds roughly $130 to $260 per month until equity reaches 20%.

One of the most eye-opening aspects of mortgage math is the total interest paid over the loan's life. On a $300,000 loan at 6.5% for 30 years, you will pay approximately $382,633 in interest alone, making the total cost $682,633. You pay more in interest than the original loan amount.

Extra Payments and Early Payoff

Making extra payments toward principal is one of the most powerful strategies available to homeowners. Because interest is calculated on the remaining balance each month, reducing that balance faster means less interest accrues.

Strategies for Extra Payments

One extra payment per year: Instead of 12 monthly payments, make 13. On a $300,000 loan at 6.5%, this shaves roughly 4.5 years off the loan and saves about $72,000 in interest.

Biweekly payments: Pay half your monthly payment every two weeks. Because there are 52 weeks in a year, this results in 26 half-payments (13 full payments) instead of 12. The effect is similar to one extra annual payment.

Rounding up: Round your $1,896 payment to $2,000. The extra $104 per month applied to principal saves approximately $45,000 in interest and reduces the loan term by about 3.5 years.

Lump-sum payments: Apply bonuses, tax refunds, or windfalls directly to principal. A one-time $10,000 extra payment in year 2 of a $300,000 loan at 6.5% saves roughly $24,000 in total interest over the remaining term.

Before making extra payments, confirm with your lender that there is no prepayment penalty and that extra funds are applied to principal (not to future payments). Some loan servicers require you to specify that additional amounts should reduce principal.

Refinancing: When and Why

Refinancing replaces your existing mortgage with a new one, ideally at a lower rate. The general rule of thumb is that refinancing makes sense when you can reduce your rate by at least 0.75% to 1%, plan to stay in the home long enough to recoup closing costs, and the closing costs (typically 2-3% of the loan) are reasonable relative to your savings.

Break-even calculation: If refinancing saves you $200 per month and costs $6,000 in closing, the break-even point is 30 months. If you plan to stay longer than 30 months, the refinance pays for itself.

Cash-out refinancing lets you borrow against your home equity, receiving the difference as cash. This can fund home improvements, debt consolidation, or other expenses, but it increases your loan balance and resets your amortization clock.

Private Mortgage Insurance (PMI) Explained

PMI is required on conventional loans when the down payment is below 20%. It protects the lender against default, not the borrower. PMI costs typically range from 0.5% to 1.5% of the loan amount per year, paid monthly.

On a $300,000 loan with a 10% down payment ($270,000 financed), PMI at 0.8% costs $2,160 per year, or $180 per month. This is added to your PITI payment.

You can request PMI removal when your equity reaches 20% of the original appraised value. The Homeowners Protection Act requires automatic cancellation at 22% equity. Some borrowers accelerate PMI removal by making extra principal payments or by requesting a new appraisal if their home has appreciated significantly.

FHA loans have their own mortgage insurance (MIP), which cannot be removed without refinancing into a conventional loan (for FHA loans originated after June 2013 with less than 10% down).

Tax Benefits of Homeownership

Mortgage interest is deductible on federal income taxes for loans up to $750,000 (for mortgages taken out after December 15, 2017). Property taxes are deductible up to a combined $10,000 state and local tax (SALT) cap. However, these deductions only help if your itemized deductions exceed the standard deduction ($15,000 for single filers and $30,000 for married filing jointly in 2026).

For many homeowners, especially those with smaller mortgages or in low-tax states, the standard deduction is larger than itemized deductions. This means the mortgage interest deduction provides no actual tax benefit. Run the numbers with a tax professional before counting on this as a reason to buy.

First-Time Buyer Programs and Assistance

Many states, counties, and cities offer down payment assistance programs for first-time buyers. These programs may provide grants (free money), forgivable loans (forgiven after you live in the home for a set period), or low-interest second loans.

Federal programs include FHA loans (lower credit requirements, 3.5% minimum down), VA loans (0% down for veterans and active military), USDA loans (0% down for eligible rural areas), and Good Neighbor Next Door (50% discount for teachers, law enforcement, firefighters, and EMTs in revitalization areas).

Check your state housing finance agency's website for local programs. Many have income limits and purchase price caps, but the savings can be substantial.

Frequently Asked Questions

What credit score do I need to get a mortgage? Conventional loans typically require a minimum 620 FICO score, though better rates start at 740+. FHA loans accept scores as low as 580 with 3.5% down, or 500 with 10% down. VA and USDA loans have no official minimum but lenders commonly require 620+.

Can I get a mortgage while self-employed? Yes, but expect additional documentation. Lenders typically want two years of tax returns, profit and loss statements, and bank statements. Self-employed income is averaged, so fluctuating earnings can reduce your qualifying amount. Some lenders offer bank statement loans that use deposits instead of tax returns, but these carry higher rates.

What is the difference between pre-qualification and pre-approval? Pre-qualification is a rough estimate based on self-reported information, usually done online in minutes with no credit pull. Pre-approval involves a full credit check, income verification, and asset documentation, resulting in a conditional commitment letter. Sellers take pre-approval much more seriously than pre-qualification.

How long does it take to close on a house? The average closing timeline is 30 to 45 days from accepted offer to closing. Cash purchases can close in as little as 7 to 14 days. Delays often come from appraisal issues, inspection negotiations, title problems, or lender processing backlogs.

Should I lock my rate or float? Rate locks guarantee your rate for a set period (typically 30 to 60 days). If rates are rising or you are close to closing, locking protects you from increases. If rates are falling, floating allows you to benefit from decreases. Rate locks sometimes have fees, especially for longer lock periods.

What is an escrow account? An escrow account is managed by your mortgage servicer to pay property taxes and homeowners insurance on your behalf. Each month, a portion of your payment goes into escrow. The servicer pays your tax and insurance bills when they come due. This protects the lender by ensuring these obligations are met. Escrow accounts are required for most loans with less than 20% down and optional for others.
