What Is a French Drain and How Does It Work

A French drain is a gravel-filled trench containing a perforated pipe that redirects groundwater away from an area where it causes problems. The concept is simple: water follows the path of least resistance, and a trench filled with coarse gravel surrounded by native soil provides an easy pathway for water to flow through. The perforated pipe at the bottom of the trench collects the water and carries it by gravity to an outlet point.

French drains are named after Henry French, a Massachusetts farmer and lawyer who popularized the technique in his 1859 book on farm drainage. The basic principle has not changed in over 160 years, though modern materials like plastic pipe and geotextile fabric have dramatically improved the longevity and performance of these systems.

The drain works because gravel has much higher permeability than the surrounding soil. When the water table rises or surface water infiltrates downward, it encounters the gravel fill of the French drain and flows into it preferentially. The water moves through the voids between the gravel particles, enters the perforated pipe through its holes, and flows along the pipe to the outlet. The pipe provides a clear channel that carries the water faster than it could move through gravel alone, especially over longer distances.

Common Applications

French drains solve a wide range of water management problems in residential and commercial settings. Each application has slightly different design requirements, but the basic principle remains the same.

Yard and Landscape Drainage

Standing water in a yard after rain indicates poor surface drainage or a high water table. A French drain installed along the low area collects the excess water and carries it to a lower point on the property or to a dry well. For yard drainage, a trench depth of 18-24 inches and a width of 12 inches is typically sufficient. The drain can follow the contour of the problem area, curving as needed to intercept water across a broad zone.

I have installed French drains in backyards that were unusable for weeks after rain events. In most cases, a single 40-80 foot drain along the lowest contour of the yard, discharging to daylight at a slope on the property boundary, eliminated the standing water problem entirely. The key is intercepting water at its lowest collection point before it has a chance to pool.

Foundation Waterproofing

A French drain installed at the base of a foundation footing (called a footing drain) intercepts groundwater before it can build up hydrostatic pressure against the basement wall. This is one of the most effective basement waterproofing strategies and is standard practice in new construction.

Foundation drains are deeper than yard drains, typically reaching the bottom of the footing (24-48 inches below grade). The drain pipe sits on a bed of gravel alongside the footing, and the entire trench is filled with gravel up to within a few inches of grade. A waterproof membrane on the foundation wall works together with the drain to keep the basement dry. The drain carries collected water to a sump pit (which pumps it out) or to a daylight outlet downhill from the building.

Retaining Wall Drainage

Water pressure behind a retaining wall is the leading cause of wall failure. A French drain installed behind the wall, at the base of the retained soil, relieves this pressure by collecting water and routing it to weep holes or outlet pipes that pass through the wall. Without proper drainage, hydrostatic pressure can exceed the wall's design capacity and cause it to bow, crack, or collapse.

Retaining wall drains typically use a wider trench (18-24 inches) filled entirely with gravel, with the perforated pipe at the bottom. The gravel extends from the drain up to within 6-12 inches of grade, covered by a layer of soil and sod on top. The filter fabric wraps the entire gravel column to prevent soil migration into the drainage layer.

Driveway and Hardscape Edge Drains

French drains along the edges of driveways, patios, and other hardscape features intercept water that runs off the impervious surface. These drains prevent erosion at the hardscape edge and keep water from undermining the base material. They are especially common where a driveway meets a lawn area and surface runoff would otherwise create a ditch over time.

Gravel Selection for French Drains

The gravel in a French drain serves two purposes: it provides structural support around the pipe and creates a highly permeable zone that attracts and channels water. The right gravel makes the difference between a French drain that works for decades and one that fails in a few years.

Never use unwashed gravel or crusher run (gravel with stone dust mixed in) for French drains. The fine particles fill the voids between the larger stones, dramatically reducing permeability and eventually clogging the drain. Always specify "washed" gravel when ordering.

Calculating Gravel Volume

The gravel volume for a French drain equals the trench volume minus the space occupied by the pipe. The trench volume is straightforward: length times width times depth, converted to cubic yards (divide by 27). The pipe displacement for a 4-inch diameter pipe is about 0.087 cubic feet per linear foot, which subtracts a relatively small amount from the total.

In practice, order about 10% more gravel than the calculated amount to account for settling, spillage, and the irregular shape of the trench bottom. Gravel settles as it is placed and compacted, so you need slightly more than the theoretical volume to fill the trench to grade.

Converting cubic yards to tons depends on the gravel type. Crushed stone and most drainage gravel weigh approximately 1.4 tons per cubic yard. River rock is slightly heavier at about 1.5 tons per cubic yard. Pea gravel weighs about 1.4 tons per cubic yard. Your gravel supplier sells by the ton, so converting from cubic yards to tons is necessary for ordering.

Pipe Selection and Installation

Corrugated vs Rigid PVC Pipe

The two main pipe options for French drains are corrugated polyethylene (the black adaptable pipe with ridges) and rigid PVC (the smooth white or green pipe with drilled holes). Each has advantages and disadvantages that affect cost, performance, and longevity.

Corrugated pipe is less expensive, easier to handle, and can curve around obstacles without fittings. It comes in long coils, so there are fewer joints to connect. However, the ridged interior surface traps sediment, the corrugations reduce flow capacity compared to smooth pipe of the same diameter, and the thinner walls are more susceptible to crushing under soil pressure or vehicle loads.

Rigid PVC pipe costs more and requires fittings at every direction change, but the smooth interior surface resists sediment buildup, maintains full flow capacity, and the thicker walls resist crushing. PVC pipe also holds grade (slope) better than corrugated pipe, which can develop sags between support points. For foundation drains and any drain under a driveway or other load-bearing surface, I strongly recommend rigid PVC.

Pipe Placement in the Trench

The pipe sits on a bed of gravel at least 2 inches thick at the bottom of the trench. This gravel bed provides uniform support and allows water to enter the pipe from below. After placing the pipe, fill gravel around and over it to within 2-3 inches of the finished grade. The top 2-3 inches can be topsoil and sod, or more gravel if the drain will be visible.

For perforated pipe, the hole orientation has been debated for decades. For most applications, place the holes facing downward. Water enters the drain from the surrounding saturated soil, rises through the gravel bed, and enters the pipe from below. With holes facing down, the pipe fills from the bottom and acts like a channel, carrying water efficiently to the outlet. Placing holes up causes the pipe to act more like a collection trough, which fills unevenly and is more prone to sediment accumulation.

Filter Fabric and Geotextile

Filter fabric (geotextile) is a permeable textile material that allows water to pass through while blocking soil particles. In French drain construction, the fabric lines the trench before the gravel is placed, wraps over the top of the gravel after filling, and prevents the surrounding soil from migrating into the drainage layer over time.

Without filter fabric, fine soil particles gradually fill the voids between gravel particles, a process called siltation. As the voids fill, the gravel loses its permeability and the drain loses its ability to collect and move water. In clay soils, this process can clog an unprotected French drain in 5-8 years. In sandy soils, it takes longer but still happens eventually.

Types of Geotextile

Non-woven geotextile is the standard choice for French drains. It has a felt-like texture with randomly oriented fibers that create millions of tiny pores. These pores are small enough to block soil particles but large enough to allow water to flow through freely. Non-woven geotextile is available in rolls of various widths and lengths, typically 3 to 6 feet wide.

Woven geotextile is stronger but has a more uniform pore structure that can clog more readily in fine-grained soils. It is better suited for separation and reinforcement applications than for filtration. For French drains, use non-woven fabric unless your engineer specifies otherwise.

Proper Fabric Installation

Line the entire trench with filter fabric before placing any gravel. The fabric should extend up the sides of the trench and fold over the top of the gravel with at least 6 inches of overlap. At joints between fabric sections, overlap at least 12 inches in the direction of water flow (upstream fabric on top of downstream fabric).

Do not wrap the fabric tightly around the pipe like a sock. The fabric should wrap the entire gravel mass, not just the pipe. Wrapping only the pipe reduces the infiltration area to the small surface of the pipe, while wrapping the gravel column allows water to enter the drain across the full trench surface.

Slope and Grade Requirements

A French drain must slope continuously from the high point to the outlet. Without adequate slope, water pools in low sections of the pipe, sediment settles out, and the drain eventually loses capacity. The minimum slope is 1% (1/8 inch per foot, or 1 foot of drop per 100 feet of run). A slope of 2% (1/4 inch per foot) is preferred and provides significantly better flow performance.

Before digging, establish the grade by driving stakes at each end of the drain and running a string line at the desired slope. Mark the trench depth at multiple points along the line to guide excavation. Check the bottom of the trench with a level and a straight board before placing the gravel bed.

For a 50-foot drain with a 1% slope, the total drop is 6 inches. At 2% slope, the drop is 12 inches. If the available grade on your property provides less than 1% slope over the drain length, consider a sump pit with a pump at the low end to move collected water to a higher outlet point.

Outlet Options

Daylight Outlet

A daylight outlet is the simplest and most dependable termination for a French drain. The pipe exits the ground on a slope and discharges water onto the surface. The outlet should be at least 10 feet from any building foundation and should direct water away from structures and property boundaries. Install a rodent guard (galvanized mesh screen) over the pipe opening to prevent animals from nesting inside the drain.

Dry Well

A dry well is an underground chamber filled with gravel or a prefabricated plastic chamber that receives water from the French drain and allows it to percolate slowly into the surrounding soil. Dry wells are used when there is no available slope for a daylight outlet. Size the dry well to hold the expected water volume between drainage events, typically 50-100 gallons per 1,000 square feet of drainage area.

Pop-Up Emitter

Pop-up emitters are spring-loaded caps that open when water pressure builds in the pipe, releasing water at the surface. They close when the flow stops, keeping debris and animals out. Pop-ups work well for moderate water volumes and provide a neat appearance, but they can freeze shut in cold climates and may not handle high-volume flows during heavy rain.

Common French Drain Mistakes

The single most common mistake in French drain installation is insufficient slope. A drain with no slope or reverse slope will not drain, and standing water in the pipe leads to sediment buildup and eventual failure. Always verify slope with a level at multiple points during and after excavation.

Using the wrong gravel ranks second. Crusher run, bank gravel, and unwashed stone all contain fines that clog the drain. Even if the gravel looks clean, the dust generated during crushing and handling fills the voids between particles. Always specify washed stone and inspect the delivery before using it.

Third is omitting the filter fabric. Some installers skip the fabric to save time and cost, but this shortcut dramatically shortens the drain's lifespan. The fabric costs a few dollars per linear foot and takes only minutes to install. It is the cheapest insurance for a long-lasting French drain.

Fourth is inadequate outlet design. The outlet must be able to discharge water as fast as the drain collects it. A small pop-up emitter on a drain that collects water from a large area will back up during heavy rain. Size the outlet to match the drain capacity, and make sure the discharge area can handle the water volume without creating erosion or flooding problems downstream.

Fifth is digging the trench too narrow. A 6-inch wide trench with a 4-inch pipe leaves almost no room for gravel around the pipe. The gravel provides the infiltration capacity that makes the drain work. For a 4-inch pipe, the trench should be at least 12 inches wide, and wider is better for high water table conditions.

Maintenance and Troubleshooting

A properly installed French drain needs very little maintenance. Check the outlet periodically to make sure it is clear of debris, vegetation, and sediment. If you have catch basins, clean them once or twice a year by removing accumulated debris from the basin grate and chamber.

If a French drain starts performing poorly (water drains slower than it used to), the most likely cause is sediment accumulation in the pipe. You can flush the pipe with a garden hose from the upstream end, or for more thorough cleaning, use a pressure washer or plumber's snake inserted from the outlet end. For drains with cleanout access points (installed every 100 feet or at direction changes), flushing is much easier.

Roots from nearby trees and shrubs can invade French drain pipes through the perforations. If you see root growth at the outlet, consider chemical root treatment or mechanical root cutting to restore flow. For new installations near large trees, use solid (non-perforated) pipe for the sections within the tree's root zone, transitioning to perforated pipe once past the roots.

Permits and Regulations

Most jurisdictions do not require a permit for a standard French drain that discharges onto your own property. However, connecting to a storm sewer, discharging onto neighboring property, or modifying drainage patterns that affect adjacent properties may require permits and could violate local ordinances.

Before digging, call 811 (or your local equivalent) to have underground utilities marked. French drain trenches are deep enough to encounter gas lines, water lines, cable, and electrical conduit. Hitting a utility line during excavation can be dangerous and expensive. Utility locating is free and required by law in most states before any excavation.

Frequently Asked Questions

How far from the foundation should a French drain be?

A footing drain sits directly alongside the foundation footing, touching or within a few inches of the footing itself. A perimeter drain for general yard drainage should be at least 3 feet from the foundation to avoid disturbing the foundation backfill. If you are draining a large area away from the house, the French drain can be 10 feet or more from the building, placed at the lowest point of the area to be drained.

Can a French drain go uphill?

No. A French drain relies on gravity to move water through the pipe to the outlet. The pipe must slope downhill from the water collection area to the discharge point. If the outlet is higher than the collection area, you need a sump pit with a pump to lift the water to the outlet elevation.

How wide should a French drain trench be?

For a 4-inch pipe, the minimum trench width is 12 inches, which provides 4 inches of gravel on each side of the pipe. For a 6-inch pipe, use at least 14-16 inches. Wider trenches (18-24 inches) provide more infiltration capacity and are recommended for high water table conditions or clay soils where water moves slowly through the native soil.

Do I need a French drain or a surface drain?

If water is pooling on the surface after rain (surface runoff problem), a surface drain (catch basin with grate) collects water faster. If the ground stays wet for days after rain (groundwater problem), a French drain addresses the subsurface water. Many drainage solutions combine both: surface catch basins to collect runoff quickly, connected to a French drain that also intercepts groundwater below the surface.

Can I use sand instead of gravel in a French drain?

No. Sand is too fine to provide the void space needed for water collection and flow. Sand-filled trenches have poor permeability compared to gravel and will clog much faster with silt from the surrounding soil. Always use gravel with a minimum particle size of 3/4 inch for French drain fill.

How long does it take to install a French drain?

A 50-foot French drain at 18 inches deep takes 1-2 full days of manual labor for one person. Renting a trenching machine reduces the excavation time to 1-2 hours for the same length, with another 3-4 hours for pipe laying, gravel filling, and backfilling. Professional crews with equipment can install 50-100 feet of French drain in a single day.

What do I do with the excavated soil?

The excavated soil volume is surprisingly large. A 50-foot trench that is 12 inches wide and 18 inches deep produces about 2.8 cubic yards of soil, which fills about 3-4 wheelbarrow loads. You can spread it in low areas of the yard, use it to regrade slopes, or haul it away. Budget for soil disposal if you have no use for the material on your property.

Can I install a French drain under a driveway?

Yes, but use rigid PVC pipe (not corrugated) and specify a trench depth that places the pipe below the driveway base material. The pipe must handle the load of vehicles above it without crushing. Schedule 40 PVC is adequate for most residential driveways. For heavy vehicle traffic, use Schedule 80 PVC or encase the pipe in a concrete saddle.

Does a French drain need a cleanout?

Cleanouts are not strictly required for short runs (under 50 feet), but they are highly recommended for longer drains and any drain with direction changes. A cleanout is simply a vertical pipe (usually a tee fitting) that connects to the drain pipe and extends to the surface with a removable cap. Install cleanouts every 100 feet and at every significant change in direction.

Will a French drain lower my water table?

A French drain lowers the water table locally, in the immediate vicinity of the trench. The effect extends roughly 1-3 feet horizontally from each side of the trench, depending on soil permeability. To lower the water table across a larger area, multiple parallel French drains spaced 10-15 feet apart may be needed. This pattern is sometimes called a herringbone drainage system.
