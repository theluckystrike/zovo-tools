The Texas No Income Tax Advantage

Texas is one of nine U.S. states that does not collect a state income tax. The others are Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Washington, and Wyoming. For Texas workers, this means a larger paycheck compared to employees in most other states doing the same work at the same salary.

I built this estimator specifically for Texas because the calculation is cleaner than most states. Without a state tax component, your take-home pay depends on just three variables beyond your gross salary. Federal income tax, FICA payroll taxes, and any voluntary pre-tax deductions you elect. The simplicity is part of what draws companies and workers to Texas.

The economic impact is real. On a $75,000 salary, a Texas worker keeps roughly $3,000 to $4,500 more per year than someone earning the same amount in states like California, New York, or New Jersey. Over a 30-year career, that difference compounds into a significant sum, especially if invested. At a 7% average annual return, that extra $4,000 per year becomes over $400,000 after three decades.

FICA Taxes in Texas

FICA payroll taxes are the second largest deduction from most Texas paychecks. These federal taxes fund Social Security and Medicare and apply regardless of state.

Social Security tax is 6.2% of your gross wages up to the wage base limit of $168,600 for 2026. Once your year-to-date earnings exceed this cap, Social Security deductions stop for the rest of the year. If you earn exactly $168,600, your annual Social Security contribution is $10,453.20.

Medicare tax is 1.45% of all gross wages with no earnings cap. There is an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax on wages above $200,000 for single filers and $250,000 for married couples filing jointly. This surtax is relatively new (introduced in 2013 as part of the Affordable Care Act) and catches many high earners by surprise on their first large-income year.

Together, a Texas worker earning $80,000 pays $4,960 in Social Security tax and $1,160 in Medicare tax, totaling $6,120 in FICA for the year. That is about $235 per biweekly paycheck.

Pre-Tax Deductions That Boost Your Paycheck

While Texas already gives you a head start by skipping state tax, pre-tax deductions can further increase your take-home pay by reducing the income subject to federal tax and FICA.

401(k) and 403(b) Contributions

The 2026 employee contribution limit for 401(k) plans is $23,500, with a $7,500 catch-up for employees 50 and older. Every dollar contributed reduces your federal taxable income. At a 22% federal rate, a $10,000 annual 401(k) contribution saves you $2,200 in federal taxes and also reduces your FICA obligation if you are below the Social Security wage base.

Employer-Sponsored Health Insurance

Most employer health insurance premiums are paid with pre-tax dollars. The average employee contribution for single coverage is about $1,400 per year and about $6,100 for family coverage. These amounts come out before federal taxes and FICA are calculated.

Health Savings Accounts

HSA contributions are triple tax-advantaged. They reduce your taxable income, grow tax-free, and can be withdrawn tax-free for qualified medical expenses. The 2026 limits are $4,300 for individual and $8,550 for family HDHP coverage. HSAs also reduce your FICA taxable wages, which is unusual among deduction types.

Dependent Care FSA

If you have children under 13, a dependent care FSA lets you set aside up to $5,000 pre-tax for childcare expenses. This directly lowers both your federal and FICA taxable income.

Property Tax and Sales Tax in Texas

Texas compensates for the lack of income tax through two main revenue sources that directly affect residents.

Property Tax

Texas has some of the highest property tax rates in the country. The average effective property tax rate is about 1.68%, compared to the national average of roughly 1.07%. On a $350,000 home, that translates to about $5,880 annually in property taxes. In some Houston suburbs and Dallas-area school districts, effective rates can exceed 2.5%.

This is the tradeoff that many people overlook. A household earning $100,000 with a $350,000 home pays $5,880 in property taxes but saves $4,000 to $5,000 by not paying state income tax. The net benefit depends heavily on your home value relative to your income. Renters in Texas get the income tax benefit without directly paying property taxes, though landlords pass some of that cost through in rental rates.

Sales Tax

The Texas state sales tax rate is 6.25%, and local jurisdictions can add up to 2%, bringing the combined rate to a maximum of 8.25%. Most major metro areas charge the full 8.25%. Groceries are exempt from Texas sales tax, which helps lower-income households. However, clothing, electronics, and most other goods are fully taxable.

For a household spending $40,000 annually on taxable goods (beyond groceries and exempt items), the 8.25% sales tax adds $3,300 in annual costs. This is a regressive tax, meaning it hits lower-income households proportionally harder than higher earners.

Remote Workers and Texas Tax Rules

The rise of remote work has made Texas an increasingly attractive base for employees of companies headquartered in other states. If you live in Texas and work remotely for a California or New York employer, you generally owe no state income tax at all. Texas will not tax you because it taxes nobody. And most states (with some exceptions) follow the rule that you pay income tax in your state of residence, not your employer's state.

The notable exceptions are the "convenience of the employer" states. New York is the most aggressive, claiming that if your remote work is for your own convenience rather than a business necessity, you still owe New York state income tax. Connecticut, Delaware, Nebraska, and Pennsylvania have similar rules. If your employer is in one of these states, consult a tax professional before assuming you owe zero state tax from Texas.

For freelancers and independent contractors based in Texas, the situation is straightforward. You pay no state income tax on self-employment income. Your obligations are limited to federal income tax, self-employment tax (15.3% covering both employee and employer FICA shares), and any applicable city or local franchise taxes.

Military Pay in Texas

Texas has a significant military presence with bases including Fort Cavazos (formerly Fort Hood), Joint Base San Antonio, Fort Bliss, and Naval Air Station Corpus Christi. For active-duty service members stationed in Texas, the no-income-tax advantage applies fully to their military pay if Texas is their state of legal residence.

Military retirement pay is also exempt from Texas state tax because Texas has no income tax. This makes Texas one of the most tax-friendly states for military retirees. Combined with the relatively low cost of living in many Texas cities (particularly San Antonio, El Paso, and the Killeen-Temple area near Fort Cavazos), military retirees can stretch their pensions significantly further than in high-tax states.

The Veterans Administration disability compensation is tax-free at both the federal and state level regardless of where you live. However, concurrent receipt of military retirement and VA disability involves a complex offset calculation that affects the taxable portion of your retirement pay. If you receive both, I recommend consulting a tax professional who specializes in military benefits.

Self-Employment in Texas

Self-employed individuals in Texas benefit significantly from the no-income-tax policy. While self-employment income is subject to federal self-employment tax (15.3% on the first $168,600 and 2.9% on amounts above that), there is no state income tax on any amount of self-employment income.

The self-employment tax rate of 15.3% combines both the employee and employer portions of FICA. As a W-2 employee, you pay 7.65% and your employer pays the matching 7.65%. As a self-employed individual, you pay both halves. However, the IRS allows you to deduct the employer-equivalent portion (7.65%) from your gross income when calculating federal income tax, which partially offsets the higher rate.

Texas does have a franchise tax (also called the margin tax) that applies to businesses with annual revenue above $2.47 million. For most sole proprietors and small LLCs, this threshold is far above their revenue levels, so the franchise tax does not apply. Businesses that exceed the threshold pay either 0.375% (wholesale or retail) or 0.75% (all other businesses) on their taxable margin.

Freelancers and independent contractors in Texas should make quarterly estimated federal tax payments (Form 1040-ES) to avoid underpayment penalties. Since there is no state income tax, there are no state estimated payments required. This simplifies the quarterly payment process compared to states where you must file estimated payments with both the IRS and the state.

Total Benefits Package Considerations

When comparing job offers in Texas versus other states, salary is only part of the equation. Texas employers in competitive markets like Austin tech and Houston energy often structure benefits packages differently than employers in high-tax states.

Health insurance is a major factor. Texas has among the highest uninsured rates in the country, partly because the state did not expand Medicaid under the Affordable Care Act. Employer-sponsored coverage is therefore especially valuable. A job offering $80,000 with full family health coverage (worth $15,000 to $25,000 annually) may be more valuable than a $90,000 position where you pay $6,000 per year in employee premiums.

Retirement contributions deserve attention. A 401(k) match is essentially free money. A 6% match on an $80,000 salary adds $4,800 per year to your retirement. In Texas, your 401(k) contributions reduce your federal tax bill (there is no state tax to reduce), so the tax savings are smaller per dollar contributed than in a state with income tax. This does not make 401(k) contributions less valuable. The match and tax-deferred growth still make it one of the best savings vehicles available.

Paid time off varies significantly by employer in Texas. The state does not mandate paid vacation, sick leave, or family leave. Some cities like Austin and Dallas have passed local paid sick leave ordinances, though enforcement and legal status have been contested. When comparing offers, calculate the value of PTO by dividing annual salary by 260 (working days) to find your daily rate, then multiply by the difference in PTO days between offers.

Texas Payroll Calendar and Planning

Texas law requires employers to pay employees at least twice per month for most workers. Exempt employees (salaried workers who meet FLSA exemption criteria) can be paid monthly. Understanding pay timing helps with budget planning and cash flow management.

Biweekly pay (every two weeks) is the most common frequency in Texas. This creates 26 pay periods per year, with two months having three paychecks. For a worker earning $75,000, a biweekly paycheck before deductions is $2,884.62. The two triple-paycheck months provide an automatic savings opportunity if you budget based on two paychecks per month.

Semi-monthly pay (1st and 15th, or 15th and last day) creates 24 pay periods per year. Each paycheck is $3,125 for a $75,000 salary. This schedule aligns well with monthly bills since you know exactly when each paycheck arrives, but the gap between checks varies from 13 to 18 days depending on the month.

Weekly pay is common in construction, retail, and hourly wage positions. The 52 smaller paychecks provide the most consistent cash flow but create more payroll processing overhead for employers. Four months per year will have five paychecks instead of four.

Texas does not have a state payday requirement that specifies a particular day of the week for payment. If your regular payday falls on a holiday or weekend, most employers pay on the business day before the holiday, though Texas law does not specifically require this.

Understanding Your Texas Pay Stub

Even without state tax, Texas pay stubs contain several line items that reduce your gross pay. Understanding each one helps you verify your paycheck accuracy.

Federal income tax withholding is calculated using IRS Publication 15-T tables based on the information you provide on your W-4 form. The key inputs are your filing status, the number of dependents you claim, and any additional withholding you request. If you filled out your W-4 before 2020, it uses the older allowances system. The current W-4 (2020 and later) uses a simpler approach based on filing status and a dollar amount for dependents.

Social Security tax appears as "OASDI" or "SS" on your pay stub. The rate is a flat 6.2% and stops once your year-to-date earnings hit the $168,600 wage base limit. If you earn above this cap, you will notice your take-home pay increases in the later months of the year when Social Security withholding stops. For someone earning $200,000, this creates an extra $1,953 in take-home pay for the remaining paychecks after hitting the cap.

Medicare tax appears as "MED" on your pay stub. The base rate of 1.45% applies to all wages. If you are a high earner, the additional 0.9% surtax on wages above $200,000 (single) kicks in automatically through employer withholding once you pass that threshold in a given year.

Pre-tax deductions reduce your taxable income before federal taxes are calculated. These include 401(k) contributions, traditional IRA deductions withheld through payroll, health insurance premiums, HSA contributions, FSA contributions, dependent care FSA, commuter benefits, and group life insurance premiums (up to $50,000 in coverage). Each of these lowers your federal tax bill, and most also reduce your FICA taxable wages.

Post-tax deductions do not reduce your taxable income. These include Roth 401(k) contributions, after-tax insurance premiums, union dues, charitable donations through payroll, and garnishments. These come out of your pay after all taxes are calculated.

Moving to Texas from a High-Tax State

If you are relocating to Texas from a state with income tax, the transition involves several financial considerations beyond just the immediate tax savings.

Establishing Texas residency for tax purposes requires more than simply moving. You should update your driver's license, vehicle registration, voter registration, and mailing address to Texas. Maintain records showing your intent to make Texas your permanent home. If your former state is aggressive about taxation (New York is notorious for this), they may attempt to claim you still owe state taxes unless you can demonstrate a clear break in residency.

The tax savings calculation is straightforward. Take your current state tax bill and that becomes your annual savings. If you were paying $5,000 per year in California state income tax, moving to Texas saves you $5,000 per year in state taxes. However, Texas property taxes are substantially higher than most states, and you should factor this into the total cost comparison.

For remote workers moving to Texas while keeping the same employer, update your W-4 or state tax forms to reflect your new Texas address. Your employer should stop withholding state income tax for your former state. Verify this on your first Texas paycheck. If they continue withholding for your old state, contact your payroll department immediately.

You may owe your former state income tax for the portion of the year you lived there. File a part-year resident return in your old state and report only the income earned while you were a resident. Texas does not require any state tax filing, so you will file only a federal return going forward.

Step by Step Guide to Estimating Your Texas Paycheck

I walk through this calculation the same way every time, whether I am helping someone evaluate a job offer or plan their monthly budget. The process is straightforward because Texas eliminates the state tax variable entirely.

Step 1 - Determine Your Gross Pay

Start with your annual salary or hourly rate. If you are salaried, divide the annual figure by the number of pay periods (26 for biweekly, 24 for semimonthly, 12 for monthly). If you are hourly, multiply your rate by the hours worked in the pay period. Overtime hours at 1.5x the regular rate go into gross pay as well. This gross figure is the starting point before any deductions.

Step 2 - Subtract Pre-Tax Deductions

Pre-tax deductions reduce your taxable income before federal taxes are calculated. Common pre-tax deductions include 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums, HSA contributions, and FSA contributions. If you contribute 6% of a $70,000 salary to a 401(k), that is $4,200 per year or about $161.54 per biweekly paycheck removed from your taxable income.

Step 3 - Calculate Federal Income Tax

Apply the 2026 federal tax brackets to your taxable income (gross minus pre-tax deductions minus the standard deduction). The standard deduction for a single filer in 2026 is $15,000. Federal taxes are progressive, meaning only the income within each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. A single filer earning $70,000 with a $15,000 standard deduction has $55,000 in taxable income, resulting in approximately $7,360 in federal tax.

Step 4 - Calculate FICA Taxes

Social Security tax is 6.2% of gross wages up to $168,600. Medicare tax is 1.45% of all wages, plus an additional 0.9% on wages above $200,000 for the Additional Medicare Tax. These percentages apply to gross pay, not taxable income. A $70,000 salary generates $4,340 in Social Security tax and $1,015 in Medicare tax, totaling $5,355 in FICA for the year.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

Paycheck estimation errors happen frequently, and each mistake throws off your budget by hundreds or thousands of dollars over a year.

Forgetting to account for the standard deduction is the most common error. Many people multiply their salary by a flat tax rate and get a number that is thousands of dollars too high. The standard deduction ($15,000 for single filers in 2026) is subtracted from your income before tax brackets apply. This single adjustment often reduces the estimated tax bill by $1,500 to $3,000 depending on your bracket.

Confusing marginal and effective tax rates leads to panic about tax bills. If your income puts you in the 22% bracket, you do not pay 22% on all your income. You pay 10% on the first $11,925, 12% on the next $36,550, and 22% only on the amount above $48,475. Your effective rate on $55,000 of taxable income is about 13.4%, not 22%.

Ignoring the Social Security wage base cap causes overestimation for high earners. Once your cumulative wages for the year exceed $168,600, Social Security tax stops. If you earn $200,000, Social Security only applies to the first $168,600. Your actual Social Security tax is $10,453, not $12,400. You will see larger paychecks in the last few months of the year once you pass the cap.

Not accounting for irregular deductions creates monthly budget mismatches. Some employers deduct health insurance from only 24 of 26 biweekly paychecks (the two three-paycheck months have no insurance deduction). This means two paychecks per year are larger than the others. Know your employer's deduction schedule so your monthly budget is precise.

Strategies to increase Your Texas Take-Home Pay

Texas gives you a built-in advantage by eliminating state income tax. On top of that baseline, several strategies can increase your net pay further.

Maximizing your 401(k) contribution up to the $23,500 annual limit in 2026 reduces your federal taxable income dollar for dollar. For someone in the 22% federal bracket, a $23,500 401(k) contribution saves $5,170 in federal taxes. You do not receive that money in your paycheck, but it goes into your retirement account tax-deferred rather than to the IRS.

Contributing to a Health Savings Account (HSA) if you have a high-deductible health plan provides a triple tax benefit. Contributions reduce your taxable income (up to $4,300 for individual coverage in 2026), growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are also tax-free. No other account type in the federal tax code offers all three benefits simultaneously.

Adjusting your W-4 withholding ensures you are not overpaying federal taxes throughout the year. If you consistently receive a large tax refund (over $1,000), you are effectively giving the government an interest-free loan. Claiming additional withholding allowances or entering a specific dollar amount on your W-4 increases each paycheck and reduces your refund to near zero.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Texas have a state income tax?

No. Texas is one of nine states with no state income tax. The Texas Constitution explicitly prohibits a personal income tax unless voters approve it in a statewide referendum. Your paycheck is only reduced by federal income tax, FICA, and any voluntary deductions.

How much is a $60,000 salary after taxes in Texas?

A single filer earning $60,000 in Texas with one federal allowance and no pre-tax deductions will take home approximately $48,800 per year, or about $1,877 per biweekly paycheck. This reflects federal income tax of about $4,300 and FICA of about $4,590 combined with the $15,000 standard deduction.

What taxes are deducted from a Texas paycheck?

Texas paychecks are subject to federal income tax at rates from 10% to 37%, Social Security tax at 6.2% up to the $168,600 wage base, and Medicare tax at 1.45% on all wages plus 0.9% on wages over $200,000. No state or local income taxes apply.

How does Texas fund its government without income tax?

Texas relies primarily on sales tax (6.25% state rate, up to 8.25% combined), property taxes (average 1.68% of assessed value), and severance taxes on oil and gas production. The state also collects franchise tax from businesses, motor vehicle sales taxes, and various fees and permits.

Is it better to live in Texas or California for take-home pay?

For take-home pay, Texas wins decisively. California's top marginal rate is 13.3%. A worker earning $100,000 keeps about $4,800 more annually in Texas. The gap grows at higher incomes. However, total financial comparison should include property taxes, sales taxes, housing costs, and available public services.

Do Texas residents pay federal taxes?

Yes. Federal income tax applies to all U.S. residents regardless of state. Texas residents pay identical federal tax brackets as residents of California, New York, or any other state. The no-tax advantage exists only at the state and local level.

What is the Texas sales tax rate?

The state sales tax rate is 6.25%. Local jurisdictions (cities, counties, transit authorities, special purpose districts) can add up to 2%, reaching a maximum combined rate of 8.25%. Most major Texas cities charge the full 8.25%. Groceries, prescription medicine, and over-the-counter drugs are exempt.

Are retirement contributions tax-deductible in Texas?

Since Texas has no state income tax, the deduction benefit is federal only. Contributing to a 401(k) or traditional IRA reduces your federal taxable income. At the 22% federal bracket, every $1,000 in 401(k) contributions saves $220 in federal taxes and increases your take-home pay correspondingly.

How much does a $100,000 salary take home in Texas?

A single filer earning $100,000 in Texas with standard deductions and no pre-tax contributions takes home approximately $78,200 per year. Federal income tax is about $14,150, Social Security is $6,200, and Medicare is $1,450. The biweekly net paycheck is approximately $3,007.69. Adding a 6% 401(k) contribution changes the take-home to about $74,400 while saving $1,320 in federal taxes.

When does Social Security tax stop being withheld in Texas?

Social Security tax (6.2%) stops once your cumulative gross wages for the year reach $168,600 (the 2026 wage base). If you earn $168,600 or more, you will see larger paychecks later in the year when Social Security withholding ceases. For a $200,000 salary, the last paycheck with Social Security deduction is typically in October, and paychecks from November through December are noticeably larger.

Texas vs Florida for No-Tax Living

Texas and Florida are the two largest no-income-tax states, and both attract relocators seeking tax savings. While the headline benefit is identical (zero state income tax), the overall financial picture differs in several ways.

Property taxes favor Florida. The average effective property tax rate in Florida is about 0.86%, compared to Texas at 1.68%. On a $400,000 home, that is $3,440 per year in Florida versus $6,720 in Texas, a difference of $3,280 annually. Florida also offers a homestead exemption of up to $50,000 (on the first $75,000 of assessed value), which further reduces property taxes for primary residences.

Sales tax is similar but varies by location. Florida's state rate is 6.0% with local additions up to 7.5% maximum. Texas's state rate is 6.25% with a maximum of 8.25%. In practice, most urban areas in both states charge between 7.5% and 8.25%, making the difference negligible for most consumers.

Insurance costs are a major differentiator. Florida homeowners insurance rates are among the highest in the nation due to hurricane risk, with average premiums exceeding $3,500 per year for a $300,000 home. Texas insurance is also above the national average but typically runs $2,000 to $2,800 for a comparable home. For coastal Texas properties (Galveston, Corpus Christi), rates can approach Florida levels.

The job market favors Texas for many industries. Texas has a more diversified economy with strong sectors in energy, technology, healthcare, defense, and finance. Houston alone has more Fortune 500 headquarters than any city except New York. Florida's economy is more dependent on tourism, hospitality, healthcare, and finance, with a growing tech presence in Miami and Tampa.

Cost of living is generally lower in Texas for comparable quality of life, particularly for housing. A three-bedroom house that costs $350,000 in a good Houston suburb might cost $400,000 to $450,000 in a comparable Orlando or Tampa suburb. Austin and Miami are the exceptions, both commanding premium prices in their respective states.

Investment and Passive Income in Texas

Beyond employment income, Texas residents benefit from the no-income-tax policy on all forms of income. Investment returns, rental income, and business profits are all exempt from state-level taxation.

Capital gains from stock sales, cryptocurrency transactions, and real estate sales face only federal taxes. In states like California (which taxes capital gains at ordinary income rates up to 13.3%), a $100,000 stock sale could cost $13,300 in state taxes alone. In Texas, that same sale incurs zero state tax.

Dividend income follows the same pattern. Qualified dividends are taxed at 0%, 15%, or 20% federally, depending on your income level. In Texas, there is no additional state tax on dividends. For retirees living on dividend income from a well-built portfolio, this can save thousands annually compared to residing in a state with income tax.

Real estate investors in Texas keep more of their rental income. After deducting mortgage interest, property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and depreciation, the net rental income is taxed only at the federal level. In California or New York, state taxes on rental income add another 5% to 13% to the tax bill. This tax advantage partially offsets Texas's higher property tax rates for real estate investors.

Business income from S-corporations, partnerships, and sole proprietorships passes through to the owner's personal return. In Texas, this pass-through income faces no state tax, making the state attractive for small business owners and entrepreneurs. The combination of no income tax, moderate business regulations, and a large consumer market has made Texas the top destination for corporate relocations over the past decade.

Retirement Planning in Texas

Texas is consistently ranked among the most retirement-friendly states, primarily because of the zero income tax policy. This affects retirement income from multiple sources.

Social Security benefits are exempt from state tax in Texas because there is no state income tax. In contrast, 12 states tax Social Security benefits at various levels, with Colorado, Connecticut, Minnesota, Montana, New Mexico, Rhode Island, Utah, Vermont, and West Virginia all imposing some level of state tax on Social Security. For a retiree receiving $30,000 per year in Social Security, the tax savings versus a state that fully taxes benefits at a 5% rate is $1,500 annually.

401(k) and IRA withdrawals in retirement face only federal income tax in Texas. In California, these distributions are taxed at rates up to 13.3%. A Texas retiree withdrawing $60,000 per year from traditional retirement accounts saves $3,000 to $5,000 annually compared to the same retiree in a high-tax state.

Pension income, whether from a corporate defined-benefit plan, the Texas Teacher Retirement System, or a military pension, is also exempt from state tax. Combined with the moderate cost of living in many Texas cities, pension income stretches further here than in most other states.

However, Texas retirees who own their homes face the ongoing burden of high property taxes. Since property taxes are based on assessed value and Texas has no cap on assessment increases (unlike California's Proposition 13), property taxes can rise significantly as home values appreciate. Texas does offer a homestead exemption that reduces the taxable value of a primary residence, and residents 65 and older can freeze their school district property tax levy, which provides some protection against rising assessments.

The lack of a state estate tax is another advantage. Texas does not impose a separate estate or inheritance tax. The federal estate tax exemption for 2026 is approximately $13.6 million per individual ($27.2 million per married couple), meaning the vast majority of Texas estates pass without any estate tax at either the state or federal level. Some states, including Maryland, Massachusetts, Oregon, and Washington, have state estate taxes with much lower exemption thresholds ($1 million to $6 million), which can create significant tax liability for upper-middle-class estates.

How to Verify Your Texas Paycheck

Paycheck errors are more common than most workers realize. The IRS estimates that approximately one-third of employers make payroll errors at some point, resulting in over-withholding or under-withholding of taxes. Here is how to verify that your Texas paycheck is correct.

Start with your gross pay. For salaried employees, divide your annual salary by the number of pay periods to confirm the gross amount. For $75,000 on biweekly pay, each gross check should be $2,884.62 (75,000 / 26). If the gross amount is wrong, everything downstream will be incorrect.

Check federal withholding against the IRS withholding tables in Publication 15-T. The calculation depends on your W-4 filing status, the number of dependents, and any additional withholding you requested. The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator (available on irs.gov) is a free tool that calculates what your withholding should be based on your specific inputs.

Verify FICA deductions. Social Security should be exactly 6.2% of your gross pay for each paycheck (until you reach the $168,600 annual cap). Medicare should be exactly 1.45% of gross. These are flat rates with no deductions or exemptions, making them easy to verify with a calculator. If your year-to-date Social Security withholding exceeds $10,453.20 (6.2% of $168,600) at any point, your employer is over-withholding.

Confirm pre-tax deductions match your benefit elections. Cross-reference the per-paycheck deduction amounts for 401(k), health insurance, HSA, and FSA against the annual amounts you elected during open enrollment, divided by the number of pay periods. A common error is failing to update deduction amounts after a mid-year benefit change, resulting in incorrect withholding for the remainder of the year.

Check that no state tax is being withheld. If you recently moved to Texas from another state, verify that your employer updated your tax status and stopped withholding state income tax. If state tax is still being withheld for your previous state, contact payroll immediately. You may need to file a state tax return in the other state to recover the incorrectly withheld amount.
