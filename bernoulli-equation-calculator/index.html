The Bernoulli Equation

Bernoulli's equation is a statement of energy conservation for a flowing fluid. Along a streamline in steady, incompressible, inviscid flow, the total mechanical energy per unit volume remains constant.

P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgh₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgh₂

Each term represents a different form of energy per unit volume:

  • P = static pressure (Pa) — pressure energy per unit volume
  • ½ρv² = dynamic pressure — kinetic energy per unit volume
  • ρgh = hydrostatic pressure — potential energy per unit volume

The equation can also be written in "head" form by dividing everything by ρg:

P₁/(ρg) + v₁²/(2g) + h₁ = P₂/(ρg) + v₂²/(2g) + h₂

In this form, each term has units of meters (or feet) and represents a height equivalent. This "head" form is common in hydraulic engineering because it makes physical intuition easier: pressure head, velocity head, and elevation head all add up to a constant total head.

Daniel Bernoulli published this principle in his 1738 work Hydrodynamica, though the mathematical formulation we use today was refined by Leonhard Euler. According to MIT OpenCourseWare fluid dynamics materials, Bernoulli's equation is actually a special case of the more general Euler equation for inviscid flow, which in turn is a special case of the Navier-Stokes equations.

Three Forms of Fluid Energy

Understanding what each term physically represents helps you apply the equation correctly and spot mistakes in your setup.

Pressure Energy (P)

Static pressure represents the internal energy stored in the fluid due to intermolecular forces. When you pressurize a fluid in a tank, you are adding energy that can do work on the surroundings. In a pipe system, pressure energy is what pushes the fluid along. It is measured with a pressure gauge perpendicular to the flow direction.

Kinetic Energy (½ρv²)

The dynamic pressure represents the kinetic energy of the moving fluid per unit volume. Faster flow means more kinetic energy. When fluid speeds up (like in a pipe constriction), kinetic energy increases and pressure energy must decrease to keep the total constant. This is the core mechanism behind the Venturi effect and many other fluid phenomena.

Potential Energy (ρgh)

The gravitational potential energy term accounts for the height of the fluid above a reference datum. Fluid at a higher elevation has more potential energy that can be converted to pressure or kinetic energy as it descends. This is why water pressure increases with depth and why elevated water tanks can supply pressure to buildings below without pumps.

Energy FormTermUnits (SI)Physical Meaning
Pressure energyPPa (N/m²)Work done by pressure forces
Kinetic energy½ρv²Pa (N/m²)Energy of fluid motion
Potential energyρghPa (N/m²)Energy due to elevation
Total energyP + ½ρv² + ρghPa (N/m²)Constant along streamline

A key insight: all three terms have the same units (Pascals in SI), which means they are all forms of "pressure" in a sense. This is why the total is sometimes called "total pressure" and the ½ρv² term is called "dynamic pressure." Thinking in terms of pressure makes it easier to work with real instruments, since pressure is what we actually measure.

The Venturi Effect

The Venturi effect is the reduction in fluid pressure that occurs when fluid flows through a constriction. It is a direct consequence of Bernoulli's equation combined with the continuity equation (conservation of mass).

How It Works

Consider fluid flowing through a pipe that narrows. The continuity equation requires:

A₁v₁ = A₂v₂ (for incompressible flow)

If the area decreases (A₂ < A₁), then the velocity must increase (v₂ > v₁). Bernoulli's equation then tells us that higher velocity means lower pressure. The pressure drop at the constriction is:

ΔP = ½ρ(v₂² - v₁²) = ½ρv₁²((A₁/A₂)² - 1)

This pressure difference can be quite large. If you reduce the pipe area by half, the velocity doubles and the dynamic pressure quadruples. For water flowing at 2 m/s in a pipe that narrows to half the area, the pressure drops by about 6,000 Pa (0.06 atm).

Venturi Meter

A Venturi meter uses this pressure difference to measure flow rate. Pressure taps are placed at the upstream section and at the throat (narrowest point). The measured pressure difference is related to flow rate by:

Q = Cd · A₂ · √(2ΔP / (ρ(1 - (A₂/A₁)²)))

Where Cd is the discharge coefficient (typically 0.97-0.99 for a well-designed Venturi). This is one of the most accurate flow measurement methods available, with uncertainties below 1% when properly calibrated.

Pitot Tube Applications

A Pitot tube (named after Henri Pitot, who invented it in 1732) measures fluid velocity by converting kinetic energy to pressure energy. It works by bringing the flow to rest at the tube opening, creating a stagnation point where all kinetic energy is converted to pressure.

The Physics

At the stagnation point (where v = 0), Bernoulli's equation gives:

P_static + ½ρv² = P_total (stagnation pressure)
v = √(2(P_total - P_static) / ρ)

A Pitot-static tube combines both measurements in one instrument: the forward-facing opening measures total pressure, and side holes (perpendicular to the flow) measure static pressure. The difference between them is the dynamic pressure ½ρv².

Aircraft Airspeed Measurement

Every aircraft has a Pitot-static system that provides airspeed, altitude, and vertical speed information. According to NASA's Pitot tube educational page, the indicated airspeed (IAS) is calculated from the dynamic pressure assuming sea-level density. True airspeed (TAS) requires correcting for actual air density at altitude.

ParameterFormulaNotes
Indicated Airspeedv_IAS = √(2q/ρ₀)q = dynamic pressure, ρ₀ = sea-level density
True Airspeedv_TAS = v_IAS × √(ρ₀/ρ)ρ = actual density at altitude
Mach NumberM = v_TAS / aa = local speed of sound

At sea level, air density is 1.225 kg/m³. At 10,000 meters (cruise altitude for commercial jets), density is about 0.414 kg/m³. This means the same dynamic pressure corresponds to a much higher true airspeed at altitude. An aircraft indicating 250 knots at cruise altitude is actually moving at roughly 430 knots true airspeed.

Pitot tube icing is a serious flight safety hazard. If the Pitot tube becomes blocked by ice, the airspeed reading becomes unreliable. Several aviation accidents have been linked to Pitot tube icing, which is why modern aircraft have heated Pitot tubes and multiple redundant systems.

Airplane Lift and Bernoulli

The relationship between Bernoulli's principle and airplane lift is one of the most misunderstood topics in physics. Let me set the record straight.

The Common (Incorrect) Explanation

Many textbooks claim that an airfoil generates lift because the curved upper surface is longer than the flat lower surface, so air must travel faster over the top to "meet up" with the air going underneath at the same time. This is the "equal transit time" theory, and it is wrong. There is no physical law requiring air parcels that split at the leading edge to rejoin at the trailing edge simultaneously. In fact, measurements show that air over the top surface arrives at the trailing edge before air going underneath.

The Better Explanation

Lift generation involves both Bernoulli's principle and Newton's laws working together:

  • Bernoulli contribution: The airfoil shape and angle of attack cause air to accelerate over the upper surface and decelerate under the lower surface. By Bernoulli's principle, this creates lower pressure above and higher pressure below. The net pressure difference over the wing area produces an upward force (lift).
  • Newton's contribution: The wing deflects the airflow downward (called downwash). By Newton's third law, the reaction force pushes the wing upward. The rate of downward momentum change in the air equals the lift force.

These are not competing explanations. They are two valid perspectives on the same physical phenomenon. The pressure distribution (Bernoulli view) and the momentum change (Newton view) must be consistent with each other and with the full Navier-Stokes equations.

The reason the air moves faster over the top is primarily because of the circulation pattern around the wing (described by the Kutta-Joukowski theorem), which is established by the wing's shape and angle of attack. The angle of attack is actually more important than the airfoil shape for generating lift, which is why flat plates and even upside-down wings can produce lift at sufficient angle of attack.

Torricelli's Theorem

Torricelli's theorem is a special case of Bernoulli's equation that applies to fluid draining from a tank through an orifice. It was published by Evangelista Torricelli in 1643, nearly a century before Bernoulli's work.

v = √(2gh)

Where v is the exit velocity, g is gravitational acceleration (9.81 m/s²), and h is the height of the fluid surface above the center of the orifice. This is the same speed an object would reach after falling from height h under gravity, which makes intuitive sense: the gravitational potential energy of the fluid is converted entirely to kinetic energy.

Derivation from Bernoulli's Equation

Apply Bernoulli between the free surface (Point 1) and the orifice exit (Point 2):

  • P₁ = P₂ = atmospheric pressure (both surfaces are open to atmosphere)
  • v₁ ≈ 0 (the tank surface drops slowly compared to the exit velocity)
  • h₁ - h₂ = h (the height difference)

Substituting: 0 + 0 + ρgh = 0 + ½ρv₂² + 0, which gives v₂ = √(2gh).

Discharge Coefficient

In reality, the exit velocity is less than the ideal Torricelli value because of friction and the contraction of the jet (vena contracta). The actual exit velocity is:

v_actual = Cd × √(2gh)

Practical Example: Water Tank Drain Time

For a cylindrical tank with cross-section A_tank and an orifice with area A_orifice at the bottom, the time to drain from height h₁ to h₂ is:

t = (A_tank / (Cd · A_orifice)) × (√(2h₁) - √(2h₂)) / √g

For a complete drain (h₂ = 0): t = (A_tank / (Cd · A_orifice)) × √(2h₁/g). This shows that drain time is proportional to the square root of the initial height, not linearly proportional. A tank that is 4 times taller takes only 2 times longer to drain.

Limitations and Assumptions

Bernoulli's equation is one of the most useful tools in fluid mechanics, but applying it outside its valid range leads to wrong answers. Here are the assumptions and when they break down.

AssumptionWhat It MeansWhen It Breaks Down
Incompressible flowFluid density is constantGas flows above Mach 0.3 (density changes exceed 5%)
Inviscid flowNo friction or viscous lossesLong pipes, boundary layers, flow through porous media
Steady flowConditions don't change with timePulsating flow, water hammer, starting/stopping pumps
Along a streamlinePoints 1 and 2 are on the same streamlineComparing points in different parts of the flow field
No energy addition/removalNo pumps, fans, or turbines between pointsPump-driven systems (use the extended energy equation instead)

The incompressible assumption is the most commonly violated in practice. For gas flows, the Bernoulli equation works well below about Mach 0.3, where density changes are less than 5%. Above that, you need compressible flow equations. For liquids, compressibility is rarely an issue unless you are dealing with extremely high pressures (hydraulic systems above 300 bar) or rapid transients (water hammer).

The inviscid assumption is more subtle. Bernoulli's equation works well in the "core" of the flow away from walls, where viscous effects are small. But near solid surfaces (in the boundary layer) and in long pipes where friction accumulates, viscous losses are significant. For pipe flow calculations, always use the modified Bernoulli equation with a head loss term.

Modified Bernoulli with Losses

For real engineering applications, the standard Bernoulli equation is modified to include energy losses due to friction and fittings, as well as energy added by pumps or removed by turbines:

P₁/(ρg) + v₁²/(2g) + h₁ + h_pump = P₂/(ρg) + v₂²/(2g) + h₂ + h_loss + h_turbine

Where h_pump is the energy added by a pump (in meters of head), h_turbine is energy removed by a turbine, and h_loss includes both major losses (pipe friction) and minor losses (fittings, valves, bends).

Major Losses (Pipe Friction)

h_f = f × (L/D) × v²/(2g)

Where f is the Darcy friction factor from the Moody diagram (based on Reynolds number and pipe roughness), L is pipe length, and D is pipe diameter.

Minor Losses (Fittings and Geometry Changes)

h_m = K × v²/(2g)

In a typical piping system with many fittings, minor losses can actually exceed major (friction) losses, especially for short pipe runs. The term "minor" is a historical misnomer. I always tell people to add up the K values for all fittings first before assuming friction dominates.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Water in a Narrowing Pipe

Problem: Water at 20°C flows through a horizontal pipe (h₁ = h₂). The upstream section has diameter 100mm with pressure 250 kPa and velocity 1.5 m/s. The pipe narrows to 50mm. Find the pressure and velocity at the narrow section.

Step 1: Find v₂ from continuity: A₁v₁ = A₂v₂

v₂ = v₁ × (D₁/D₂)² = 1.5 × (100/50)² = 1.5 × 4 = 6.0 m/s

Step 2: Apply Bernoulli (horizontal, so h terms cancel):

P₂ = P₁ + ½ρ(v₁² - v₂²) = 250,000 + ½(998.2)(1.5² - 6.0²)
P₂ = 250,000 + 499.1(2.25 - 36) = 250,000 - 16,845 = 233,155 Pa ≈ 233.2 kPa

Result: Velocity increases to 6.0 m/s, pressure drops by 16.8 kPa (from 250 to 233.2 kPa).

Example 2: Elevated Water Tank

Problem: A water tower has its tank surface 30m above ground level. What is the water pressure at ground level? (Assume negligible velocity in the large tank and at the ground-level tap.)

Solution: With v₁ ≈ v₂ ≈ 0, Bernoulli reduces to the hydrostatic equation:

P₂ = P₁ + ρg(h₁ - h₂) = 101,325 + (998.2)(9.81)(30)
P₂ = 101,325 + 293,771 = 395,096 Pa ≈ 395 kPa (57.3 psi)

Result: The gauge pressure at ground level is about 294 kPa (42.6 psi), which is well within the typical municipal water pressure range of 40-80 psi.

Example 3: Pitot Tube Airspeed

Problem: An aircraft Pitot tube reads a total pressure of 103,500 Pa while the static port reads 101,325 Pa. Air density at this altitude is 1.225 kg/m³. What is the airspeed?

v = √(2(P_total - P_static)/ρ) = √(2(103,500 - 101,325)/1.225)
v = √(2 × 2,175 / 1.225) = √(3,551) = 59.6 m/s ≈ 115.8 knots

Result: The airspeed is 59.6 m/s (about 116 knots or 214 km/h).

History of Bernoulli's Principle

Daniel Bernoulli (1700-1782) was a Swiss mathematician and physicist from a distinguished family of scientists. His father Johann and uncle Jacob were both prominent mathematicians. Daniel published Hydrodynamica in 1738, in which he derived the relationship between fluid velocity and pressure using conservation of energy arguments.

Interestingly, the "Bernoulli equation" as we write it today was not exactly what Daniel Bernoulli derived. His treatment was more qualitative and based on the concept of "vis viva" (living force, an early form of kinetic energy). It was Leonhard Euler who, working from 1752 to 1755, developed the rigorous mathematical framework (Euler's equations of fluid motion) from which the modern Bernoulli equation is derived.

There was actually a bitter priority dispute between Daniel and his father Johann. Johann Bernoulli published his own Hydraulica in 1742 but backdated it to 1732 to claim priority over his son's work. Modern historians generally credit Daniel with the original insight, though Johann's mathematical treatment was arguably more rigorous. The family drama is a fascinating footnote in the history of physics.

The practical impact of Bernoulli's principle was not felt until the Industrial Revolution, when steam-powered machinery created a need for understanding fluid behavior in pipes, nozzles, and hydraulic systems. Today, every piping system, ventilation design, and aerodynamic calculation uses some form of Bernoulli's equation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Bernoulli equation?

The Bernoulli equation states that for steady, incompressible, inviscid flow along a streamline, the sum of static pressure, dynamic pressure (½ρv²), and hydrostatic pressure (ρgh) remains constant. Written as: P₁ + ½ρv₁² + ρgh₁ = P₂ + ½ρv₂² + ρgh₂. It is fundamentally a statement of energy conservation applied to a flowing fluid.

How does the Venturi effect work?

When fluid flows through a constriction, the continuity equation requires velocity to increase (same mass flow through smaller area). Bernoulli's equation then dictates that pressure must decrease to compensate. This pressure drop at the constriction is the Venturi effect. It is used in flow meters, carburetors, aspirators, and many other devices.

What is Torricelli's theorem?

Torricelli's theorem states that the velocity of fluid draining from an orifice in a tank equals v = √(2gh), where h is the height of fluid above the orifice. This is a special case of Bernoulli's equation where both the surface and exit are at atmospheric pressure, and the surface velocity is negligible compared to the exit velocity.

How does a Pitot tube measure airspeed?

A Pitot tube brings flow to rest at its tip, converting kinetic energy to pressure. The resulting stagnation pressure minus the ambient static pressure gives the dynamic pressure (½ρv²). Solving for v gives the airspeed: v = √(2ΔP/ρ). Aircraft use this principle through a combined Pitot-static system for their airspeed indicators.

Does the Bernoulli equation explain airplane lift?

Partially. The pressure difference between upper (faster, lower pressure) and lower (slower, higher pressure) wing surfaces predicted by Bernoulli contributes to lift. But the full explanation also requires Newton's third law (wing deflects air downward, reaction force pushes wing up) and circulation theory. The common "equal transit time" explanation is incorrect.

What are the limitations of the Bernoulli equation?

Bernoulli's equation assumes incompressible flow (invalid for gas speeds above Mach 0.3), inviscid flow (no friction, breaks down in pipes and boundary layers), steady flow (no time variation), and applies only along a streamline. For real pipe systems with friction, use the modified Bernoulli equation with head loss terms.

What units should I use in the Bernoulli equation?

In SI: pressure in Pascals, density in kg/m³, velocity in m/s, height in meters, g = 9.81 m/s². All terms then have units of Pa. In US customary: pressure in psf, density in slugs/ft³, velocity in ft/s, height in ft, g = 32.174 ft/s². Consistent units are critical since mixing systems is a common error source.

How do I account for friction losses in real pipe systems?

Add a head loss term to the equation: P₁/(ρg) + v₁²/(2g) + h₁ = P₂/(ρg) + v₂²/(2g) + h₂ + h_loss. Head loss includes major losses from pipe friction (using the Darcy-Weisbach equation and Moody diagram) and minor losses from fittings and geometry changes (using loss coefficients K). This "extended Bernoulli" or "energy equation" is what practicing engineers actually use.
