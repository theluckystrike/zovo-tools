Flooring Types Compared

Choosing the right flooring type is the biggest decision in any flooring project, and it affects everything from cost to maintenance to home resale value. Here is a detailed breakdown of each type this calculator supports.

Hardwood ($6 to $12 per sqft material)

Solid hardwood remains the gold standard for home flooring. It is durable, beautiful, and can be refinished multiple times over its lifespan. Oak, maple, and hickory are the most popular species. The downside is cost and susceptibility to moisture, which makes it unsuitable for bathrooms and basements. Installation requires either nailing or gluing, and acclimation time (letting the wood adjust to your home's humidity) adds 3 to 5 days to the project timeline.

Laminate ($2 to $6 per sqft material)

Laminate has come a long way since its early days of looking obviously fake. Modern laminate uses high-resolution photographic layers that convincingly mimic wood, stone, or tile. It is significantly cheaper than hardwood, easier to install (click-lock floating installation), and more resistant to scratches and moisture. The trade-off is that it can't be refinished, and lower-quality laminate can sound hollow underfoot. I've found that spending a bit more on underlayment dramatically improves the feel and sound.

Tile ($5 to $10 per sqft material)

Ceramic and porcelain tile are ideal for wet areas like bathrooms, kitchens, and entryways. Porcelain is denser and more water-resistant than ceramic, making it suitable for outdoor use as well. Tile installation is more labor-intensive than other options because it requires a flat substrate, mortar application, and grouting. Waste factor for tile tends to be higher due to cuts and potential breakage, which is why I recommend at least a 10 percent waste factor for simple layouts and 15 to 20 percent for diagonal or pattern installations.

Vinyl / LVP / LVT ($2 to $5 per sqft material)

Luxury vinyl plank (LVP) and luxury vinyl tile (LVT) have exploded in popularity over the past five years. They are waterproof, comfortable underfoot, and available in remarkably realistic wood and stone patterns. Installation is typically click-lock floating, making it a popular DIY choice. The material cost is lower than hardwood or tile, and the labor cost is also lower due to faster installation. For more detail on vinyl flooring specifications, Wikipedia's article on vinyl flooring provides good background.

Carpet ($3 to $6 per sqft material)

Carpet remains the most comfortable and warmest flooring option, making it popular for bedrooms and living rooms. It also provides sound insulation. The material is typically sold by the square yard (1 sqyd = 9 sqft), and this calculator handles that conversion. Carpet requires padding underneath, which adds $0.50 to $1.00 per sqft to the cost. The main drawback is maintenance; carpet stains, harbors allergens, and wears in high-traffic areas.

Understanding the Waste Factor

The waste factor is arguably the most misunderstood aspect of flooring estimation. I can't tell you how many homeowners I've heard from who bought exactly the square footage of their room and ended up short. There are several reasons you need extra material:

First, cuts. Every wall edge requires cutting planks, tiles, or carpet to fit. The offcuts from one row rarely fit perfectly at the start of another row, especially with random-length planks. Second, pattern matching. If you are installing hardwood with a specific grain pattern or tile with veining that needs to align, you will discard more material to maintain the pattern. Third, damage. Some pieces arrive damaged or crack during cutting. Having extra material on hand means you don't have to halt the project for a reorder.

The standard industry recommendation is 10 percent waste for simple rectangular rooms. For L-shaped rooms, diagonal installations, or large-format tiles, you should increase to 15 or even 20 percent. Experienced installers on Stack Overflow and in contractor forums consistently recommend erring on the side of more rather than less, because matching dye lots later is often impossible.

How to Measure Your Rooms

Accurate measurements are the foundation of a good estimate. Here is the process I recommend:

For rectangular rooms, measure the length and width at the widest points, including any alcoves or closets you want to floor. For L-shaped rooms, divide them into two rectangles, measure each separately, and add them as separate rooms in this calculator. For rooms with bay windows or angled walls, measure to the farthest point and accept that the waste factor will account for the extra cuts needed.

Always measure in feet and inches, then convert to decimal feet. For example, 12 feet 6 inches is 12.5 feet. Measure at least twice and use the larger number. A mistake of even 6 inches across a 15-foot room changes the square footage by 7.5 sqft, which could mean you are a box short on materials.

Cost-Saving Tips

After working on this tool and researching flooring extensively, I've compiled the tips that actually make a meaningful difference in project cost:

Buy material during holiday sales. Major home improvement retailers typically offer the best flooring deals during Memorial Day, Labor Day, and Black Friday sales. Savings of 20 to 30 percent are common. You should also consider buying slightly more than you need (an extra 5 percent beyond your waste factor) and keeping leftover material for future repairs. Matching dye lots months or years later is nearly impossible.

If you are comfortable with DIY, laminate and vinyl are the easiest to install yourself. The click-lock systems used by most manufacturers require no special tools beyond a utility knife, rubber mallet, and pull bar. Hardwood and tile are much harder to DIY and mistakes are costly, so professional installation is usually worth the investment for those materials.

Our Testing Methodology

I've validated this calculator through original research that cross-references material costs from major retailers (Home Depot, Lowe's, Floor and Decor) and labor rates from contractor aggregation services. The pricing data was last updated in March 2026 and represents national average ranges.

The waste factor calculations were tested against industry guidelines published by the National Wood Flooring Association and the Tile Council of North America. Edge cases tested include: very small rooms (closets under 10 sqft), very large rooms (over 1,000 sqft), and projects with up to 10 rooms of varying sizes. The box/unit calculation rounds up to the nearest whole unit, which matches how materials are sold in practice.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much extra flooring should I buy for waste?
The standard recommendation is 10 percent extra for rectangular rooms with straightforward layouts. Increase to 15 percent for L-shaped rooms, rooms with many obstacles, or diagonal installations. For large-format tiles or intricate patterns, go with 20 percent. It is always better to have leftover material than to run short mid-project.
What is the cheapest flooring option?
Vinyl plank (LVP) and laminate are typically the cheapest options, ranging from $2 to $5 per sqft for materials. Vinyl has the added advantage of being waterproof and very easy to install yourself, which eliminates labor costs. Carpet can also be affordable at $3 to $6 per sqft for materials, but it doesn't last as long and requires professional stretching for best results.
How long does flooring installation take?
For a typical 500 sqft room, vinyl and laminate take 1 to 2 days, hardwood takes 2 to 5 days (including acclimation time), tile takes 3 to 7 days (including mortar cure time), and carpet takes about 1 day. These estimates assume the subfloor is already prepared. Add 1 to 3 days for subfloor preparation if needed.
Can I install flooring over existing flooring?
In some cases, yes. Laminate and vinyl can often be installed over existing hard flooring if it is flat and in good condition. You generally shouldn't install over carpet (except for carpet over carpet in some cases). Tile can be installed over existing tile if the original is well-bonded and flat. Always check the manufacturer's installation guidelines for your specific product.
What flooring is best for kitchens and bathrooms?
Tile and vinyl are the best choices for wet areas. Both are waterproof and resistant to the moisture common in kitchens and bathrooms. Porcelain tile is the most durable but also the most expensive to install. Luxury vinyl plank offers a great balance of water resistance, comfort, and cost. Avoid solid hardwood in these areas, as moisture will cause warping and damage over time.
How do I calculate flooring for irregularly shaped rooms?
Break the room into rectangular sections and measure each one separately. Add each section as a separate "room" in this calculator. For curved walls or very unusual shapes, measure the bounding rectangle (the smallest rectangle that would contain the room) and increase your waste factor to 15 or 20 percent to account for the extra cuts needed.
Does this calculator account for closets and doorways?
You should measure closets separately and add them as individual rooms if you plan to floor them. For doorways, the transition area is typically negligible (a few sqft). The waste factor generally covers minor areas like doorway transitions. If you have many closets or complex transitions, add a separate room entry for each to get the most accurate estimate.
