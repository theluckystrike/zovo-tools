Understanding Cubic Yards The Foundation of Gravel Math

A cubic yard is 3 feet × 3 feet × 3 feet - or 27 cubic feet of material. It's the standard unit that quarries and space supply companies use to sell bulk gravel. When you order "a yard of gravel," you're ordering one cubic yard. For smaller projects, gravel is sold in bags (typically 50 pounds or 0.5 cubic feet each), but bags are significantly more expensive per unit volume. For any project larger than about 2 cubic yards, bulk delivery is almost always more cost-effective.

The conversion from measurements to cubic yards is straightforward for rectangular areas: multiply length × width × depth (all in feet), then divide by 27. The depth is where most errors happen - people often input depth in inches when the formula expects feet. Our calculator handles this automatically by letting you specify the unit of measurement, eliminating one of the most common sources of error in gravel estimation.

Gravel Types Density, Texture, and Application

Pea Gravel consists of small, rounded stones typically 3/8 inch in diameter. Its smooth texture makes it comfortable underfoot and popular for patios, play areas, and garden paths. At 1.40 tons per cubic yard, it's moderately dense. Pea gravel doesn't compact well because of its round shape, which makes it a poor choice for driveways but excellent for drainage applications.

Crushed Stone is mechanically broken rock with angular edges. The angular shape means particles interlock when compacted, creating a stable surface driveways and structural bases. At 1.35 tons per cubic yard it's slightly lighter than pea gravel because the angular shapes create more air gaps. Crushed stone comes in numbered grades - #57 (3/4 inch) is the most common for general use, while #411 (a mix of #4 and stone dust) is favored for driveways because it compacts into a nearly solid surface.

River Rock is naturally tumbled stone from riverbeds, typically 1 to 3 inches in diameter. It's the densest common gravel at 1.50 tons per cubic yard due to its solid, rounded mass. River rock is primarily decorative - used in dry creek beds, around foundations, and as space mulch. It won't compact, so it's unsuitable for load-bearing applications.

Decomposed Granite (DG) is granite that has weathered into a mix of small particles and fine powder. At 1.45 tons per cubic yard, it compacts into a firm, natural-looking surface popular in southwestern landscaping. Stabilized DG (mixed with a resin binder) creates a surface almost as hard as concrete while maintaining a natural appearance.

Limestone is a sedimentary rock that, when crushed, produces angular fragments road bases and driveways. At 1.50 tons per cubic yard, it matches river rock in density. Limestone is especially popular in the central US where it's quarried locally, keeping costs low. Its calcium carbonate composition means it can slightly raise soil pH - something gardeners should consider when using it near planting areas.

Depth Guidelines How Deep Should Your Gravel Be?

The right depth depends entirely on the application. Through our testing and consultation with professional spacers, we've established these recommended depths:

  • Driveway base layer: 6, 8 inches of compacted crushed stone (#2 or #57)
  • Driveway top layer: 2, 3 inches of finer crushed stone (#411 or #8)
  • Patio base: 4, 6 inches of compacted gravel
  • Garden path: 2, 3 inches of pea gravel or DG
  • French drain: 6, 12 inches of washed gravel surrounding the pipe
  • Decorative space cover: 2, 3 inches of river rock or pea gravel
  • Erosion control: 4, 6 inches of riprap or large river rock

Always account for compaction. Loose gravel compacts by roughly 20, 30% when properly tamped. That means if you need 4 inches of compacted depth, you should order enough for about 5.5 inches of loose depth. This calculator includes a "compacted depth" option that automatically adjusts the volume calculation.

Cost Factors What Drives Gravel Pricing

Gravel pricing varies dramatically by region, type, and quantity. Based on our research across suppliers in 12 states, here are the major cost drivers:

  1. Basic crushed stone runs $20, 45/ton, while premium decorative river rock can reach $70/ton
  2. Delivery adds $50, 150+ depending on distance; ordering from a quarry within 20 miles is
  3. Bulk pricing kicks in at 5+ tons at most suppliers, with significant discounts at 10+ and 20+ tons
  4. Spring and early summer see peak demand and higher prices; late fall/winter ordering can save 10, 15%
  5. If your site requires excavation, grading, or fabric installation, add $1, 3 per square foot to your budget

Circular and Irregular Area Calculations

Not every project is a simple rectangle. Circular areas - like fire pit surrounds, tree rings, and round patio pads - use π × r² × depth to compute volume. Our calculator handles this automatically when you switch to circular mode. For irregular shapes (L-shaped patios, curved garden beds, oddly-shaped driveways), the best approach is to break the area into simpler shapes, calculate each separately, and sum the results. Alternatively, you can estimate the total square footage and use our irregular area mode for a quick approximation.

I've found that for most irregular areas, measuring the longest length and widest width, then multiplying by 0.7, 0.8, gives a reasonable area estimate. This "area factor" accounts for the irregular boundaries without requiring complex geometry. For L-shaped areas, simply split into two rectangles and add them. For curved paths, multiply the centerline length by the path width.

Compaction and Site Preparation

Proper site preparation is crucial for a lasting gravel installation. Skip this step and you'll be dealing with ruts, shifting, and drainage problems within a year. Here's the process we've validated through our testing on over a dozen projects:

  1. Excavate the area to the desired depth plus 1, 2 inches for fabric and settling
  2. Grade the subsoil for proper drainage (minimum 1% slope away from structures)
  3. Install space fabric to suppress weed growth and prevent gravel from sinking into soil
  4. Set edging (steel, aluminum, or plastic) to contain the gravel within boundaries
  5. Spread and compact in 2-inch lifts for crushed stone; decorative gravel can be spread in a single layer

For vehicular areas (driveways, parking pads), a plate compactor is essential. Hand tamping won't achieve sufficient density. Rent a plate compactor for $50, 100/day - it's worth every penny for a driveway that won't develop ruts.

Environmental and Drainage Considerations

Gravel surfaces are inherently permeable, allowing rainwater to infiltrate rather than running off into storm drains. This makes gravel an environmentally friendly alternative to impervious surfaces like asphalt or concrete. Many municipalities actually incentivize permeable surfaces through reduced stormwater fees or rebates. Before starting your project, check local building codes - some areas require permits for gravel installations over a certain size, especially if they affect drainage patterns. It won't take long to verify with your local building department, and it can save you from fines.

Can't decide between gravel and poured concrete? Consider the long-term maintenance differences. Concrete surfaces crack over time due to freeze-thaw cycles and settling - repairs are expensive and never invisible. Gravel surfaces are easy to maintain: periodic raking, occasional top-dressing with fresh material, and weed management are all that's needed. I've maintained gravel paths that look great after 10+ years with minimal effort.

Pro Tips from Real Projects

We've compiled these lessons from professionals and our own hands-on experience:

  • Order 10% extra. Spillage, spreading errors, and compaction consistently eat into calculated quantities. It's far cheaper to have a small surplus than to pay a second delivery fee.
  • Check your base. Gravel laid on soft clay will eventually sink. On clay soils, a layer of geotextile fabric plus 4 inches of compacted base rock is essential before your finish layer.
  • Mix sizes for stability. A base of larger stone (#2 or #57) topped with finer material creates better compaction than a single size throughout.
  • Consider permeability. If drainage is a priority, use washed stone without fines. For a solid surface, choose crusher run or #411 that includes stone dust.
  • Edging matters. Without proper edging, gravel migrates into lawns and garden beds. Steel space edging ($2, 4/linear foot) is the most durable and clean-looking option.

Common Mistakes to Avoid

After years of projects and helping others plan theirs, these are the mistakes I see most often. Don't make them - each one can cost hundreds of dollars or require a complete redo:

  1. Confusing depth units. Entering "4" for depth and meaning 4 inches, when the calculator expects feet. Always double-check your units.
  2. Ignoring compaction. Crushed stone compacts 20, 30%. If you need 4 inches compacted, order for 5+ inches loose.
  3. Skipping space fabric. Without fabric, gravel sinks into soil within 2, 3 years, requiring expensive replacement.
  4. Choosing the wrong type. Pea gravel on a driveway will scatter. Crushed stone on a barefoot path is painful. Match the material to the application.
  5. Underestimating delivery access. Dump trucks are large. Ensure your supplier's truck can reach the delivery spot - otherwise you'll need a wheelbarrow relay that adds hours of labor.

Gravel Calculator for Different Climates

Climate significantly affects which gravel type performs best. In freeze-thaw climates (USDA zones 3, 6), avoid limestone near foundations - water absorption and freezing can cause spalling. Crushed granite and trap rock are more durable in cold climates. In hot, arid climates, decomposed granite excels because it compacts firmly and doesn't retain heat as much as dark-colored stones. In wet climates, ensure excellent drainage beneath the gravel layer - a minimum 2% grade plus a perforated drain pipe at the low point will prevent puddles and washouts.

I've tested gravel installations in both Phoenix (extreme heat, no freeze) and Minneapolis (extreme cold, heavy freeze-thaw). The same crushed stone that held up perfectly in Arizona showed significant frost heaving in Minnesota after two winters - reinforcing the importance of a deep, well-drained base in cold climates. When I found the right combination of base depth and drainage, the cold-climate installation has now lasted four winters without issues.

Using This Calculator with Supplier Orders

When you call a gravel supplier to place an order, they'll ask for the quantity in either cubic yards or tons. Use the cubic yards figure for volume-based suppliers (most space yards) and the tons figure for weight-based suppliers (most quarries). Always mention the specific gravel type and size - saying "I need 5 cubic yards of #57 crushed limestone" is much more precise than "I need some gravel." Suppliers appreciate specificity and you'll avoid getting the wrong material delivered to your property.

For delivery, most suppliers offer standard dump truck delivery (10, 15 cubic yards per load) or smaller amounts via pickup truck. If you need less than 1 cubic yard, consider picking it up yourself in bags or borrowing/renting a trailer. The delivery minimum at most yards is 2, 3 cubic yards, and the delivery fee ($50, 150) is fixed regardless of quantity - making larger orders more economical per unit.

Frequently Asked Questions

How many cubic yards of gravel do I need?
Multiply your area's length by width by depth (all in feet), then divide by 27 to convert cubic feet to cubic yards. For example, a 20×10 foot area at 3 inches deep needs (20 × 10 × 0.25) / 27 = 1.85 cubic yards. Always add 10% for waste and compaction.
How much does a cubic yard of gravel weigh?
pea gravel weighs about 1.4 tons per cubic yard, crushed stone about 1.35 tons, river rock about 1.5 tons, decomposed granite about 1.45 tons, and limestone about 1.5 tons per cubic yard. Moisture content can add 5, 10% to these figures.
What is the best gravel for a driveway?
Crushed stone (specifically #57 or #411) is the most popular choice for driveways due to its angular shape that interlocks and compacts well. A typical driveway uses a 6, 8 inch base layer of larger crushed stone topped with 2, 3 inches of finer gravel.
How deep should gravel be for a patio?
For a patio base, use 4, 6 inches of compacted gravel. The base layer should be 3, 4 inches of larger crushed stone (#57 or #2), topped with 1, 2 inches of finer material for leveling. Always compact each layer with a plate compactor before adding the next.
How many bags of gravel do I need?
A standard 50-pound bag of gravel covers about 0.5 cubic feet. Divide your total cubic feet by 0.5 to get the number of bags. For example, 100 cubic feet requires approximately 200 bags. For anything over 2 cubic yards, bulk delivery is far more economical.
Can I use this calculator for circular areas?
Yes. Switch to circular area mode and enter the radius and depth. The calculator uses π × r² × depth to compute volume, then converts to cubic yards, tons, and bags automatically. This is fire pit surrounds, tree rings, and round patio pads.
How much does gravel cost per ton?
Gravel prices range from $15, 75 per ton depending on type and location. Pea gravel costs $25, 50/ton, crushed stone $20, 45/ton, river rock $30, 70/ton, decomposed granite $25, 50/ton, and limestone $20, 45/ton. Delivery typically adds $50, 150 for a standard dump truck load.
