Understanding the Equation

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation relates the pH of a solution to the pKa of a weak acid and the ratio of its conjugate base to its undissociated acid form:

pH = pKa + log10([A−] / [HA])

Key insights from this equation:

  • When [A−] = [HA], the log term is zero, so pH = pKa. This is the midpoint of a titration and where buffer capacity is greatest.
  • When [A−] > [HA], the log term is positive, so pH > pKa. There is more conjugate base than acid.
  • When [A−] < [HA], the log term is negative, so pH < pKa. There is more acid than conjugate base.
  • For every 10-fold change in the [A−]/[HA] ratio, the pH changes by exactly 1 unit.

The equation is derived from the acid dissociation equilibrium Ka = [H+][A−]/[HA]. Taking the negative log of both sides and rearranging gives the Henderson-Hasselbalch form. It assumes that the acid is weak (Ka is small) and that the concentrations used are equilibrium concentrations.

Buffer Preparation Guide

Preparing a buffer solution involves dissolving the correct amounts of weak acid and conjugate base (or titrating one form with strong acid/base) to achieve the desired pH. Here is a general procedure:

  1. Choose the buffer system. Pick a buffer with a pKa within 1.0 pH unit of your target pH.
  2. Calculate amounts. Use the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to determine the ratio of [A−]/[HA], then use the total concentration to find individual amounts. The Buffer Recipe Maker above does this for you.
  3. Weigh and dissolve. Weigh the acid and base forms on an analytical balance and dissolve in roughly 80% of the final volume of deionized water.
  4. Adjust pH. Use a calibrated pH meter and add small amounts of HCl or NaOH to fine-tune to the exact target pH.
  5. Bring to volume. Transfer to a volumetric flask and add water to the final volume mark.
  6. Verify and store. Check the pH once more, label with the buffer identity, concentration, pH, date, and your initials. Store at the recommended temperature.

Temperature affects pKa values. Tris buffer is especially temperature-sensitive: its pKa drops by about 0.03 units per degree Celsius increase. Always adjust pH at the temperature you will use the buffer.

Biological Buffer Systems

Blood Bicarbonate Buffer

The most important buffer in human blood is the bicarbonate system: CO2 + H2O ↔ H2CO3 ↔ H+ + HCO3−. Blood pH is maintained near 7.40, with the bicarbonate pKa at 6.1 (apparent pKa in the body, accounting for dissolved CO2). Despite being far from the pKa, this system works because the lungs continuously regulate CO2 and the kidneys regulate HCO3− reabsorption.

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation for blood becomes: pH = 6.1 + log([HCO3−] / [0.03 × pCO2]). Normal values are [HCO3−] = 24 mM and pCO2 = 40 mmHg, giving pH = 6.1 + log(24/1.2) = 6.1 + 1.30 = 7.40.

Respiratory and Metabolic Acid-Base Disorders

When the bicarbonate system is disrupted, four primary acid-base disorders result:

  • Respiratory acidosis occurs when CO2 accumulates (hypoventilation), increasing the denominator and lowering pH below 7.35. Common causes include COPD, pneumonia, and respiratory depression.
  • Respiratory alkalosis occurs when CO2 is blown off too rapidly (hyperventilation), decreasing the denominator and raising pH above 7.45. Causes include anxiety, fever, and high altitude.
  • Metabolic acidosis results from excess acid production or bicarbonate loss, lowering the numerator and dropping pH. Diabetic ketoacidosis and lactic acidosis are common examples.
  • Metabolic alkalosis results from bicarbonate excess or acid loss (e.g., prolonged vomiting), raising the numerator and increasing pH.

Clinicians use arterial blood gas (ABG) measurements and the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation to diagnose and manage these conditions. The body compensates for primary disorders: respiratory problems trigger renal compensation (slow, hours to days), while metabolic problems trigger respiratory compensation (fast, minutes).

Intracellular pH Regulation

Inside cells, pH is typically 7.0 to 7.2, maintained by phosphate buffers (H2PO4−/HPO42−, pKa 7.20) and proteins (histidine residues, pKa near 6.0). Ion transporters like the Na+/H+ exchanger and HCO3−/Cl− exchanger actively maintain cytoplasmic pH within the narrow range required for enzyme function.

Protein Buffering

Proteins are significant biological buffers because they contain ionizable amino acid side chains. Histidine (pKa 6.0) is the most effective protein buffer at physiological pH because its imidazole group has a pKa closest to 7.4. Hemoglobin is the most abundant intracellular protein buffer in red blood cells. The buffering capacity of blood proteins accounts for about 7% of the total blood buffer capacity, with bicarbonate accounting for roughly 64% and phosphate about 1%. The remaining 28% comes from hemoglobin and other proteins.

Ocean pH and the Carbon Cycle

Seawater is buffered by the carbonate system at pH 8.1 to 8.3. As atmospheric CO2 increases, more dissolves in the ocean, forming carbonic acid and lowering pH. Since the industrial revolution, ocean pH has dropped from about 8.2 to 8.1, a 30% increase in hydrogen ion concentration. This ocean acidification threatens calcifying organisms like corals, mollusks, and some plankton, because lower pH reduces the availability of carbonate ions needed to build calcium carbonate shells.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Finding pH of an Acetate Buffer

A buffer is prepared with 0.20 M sodium acetate (NaCH3COO) and 0.15 M acetic acid (CH3COOH). The pKa of acetic acid is 4.76. What is the pH?

  • [A−] = 0.20 M (sodium acetate provides the conjugate base)
  • [HA] = 0.15 M (acetic acid is the weak acid)
  • pH = 4.76 + log(0.20 / 0.15)
  • pH = 4.76 + log(1.333)
  • pH = 4.76 + 0.125 = 4.89

Example 2: How Much Sodium Acetate to Add?

You want a buffer at pH 5.00 using acetic acid (pKa 4.76). You already have 0.10 M acetic acid. What concentration of sodium acetate do you need?

  • 5.00 = 4.76 + log([A−] / 0.10)
  • log([A−] / 0.10) = 0.24
  • [A−] / 0.10 = 100.24 = 1.738
  • [A−] = 1.738 × 0.10 = 0.174 M sodium acetate

Example 3: Finding pKa from pH Measurement

A buffer contains 0.050 M of a weak acid and 0.075 M of its conjugate base. The measured pH is 9.25. What is the pKa?

  • 9.25 = pKa + log(0.075 / 0.050)
  • 9.25 = pKa + log(1.50)
  • 9.25 = pKa + 0.176
  • pKa = 9.25 - 0.176 = 9.07

A pKa of 9.07 is close to the pKa of borate buffer (9.24) or the amino group of glycine (9.78). The researcher could use this information to identify the unknown buffer system.

Example 4: Adding Strong Acid to a Buffer

Consider 1.0 L of a phosphate buffer at pH 7.20 (pKa = 7.20) with total concentration 0.10 M. At pH = pKa, [A−] = [HA] = 0.050 M each. What happens when 0.010 mol of HCl is added?

  • HCl converts conjugate base to acid: new [A−] = 0.050 - 0.010 = 0.040 M; new [HA] = 0.050 + 0.010 = 0.060 M
  • pH = 7.20 + log(0.040 / 0.060) = 7.20 + log(0.667) = 7.20 - 0.176 = 7.02
  • The pH dropped by only 0.18 units despite adding 0.010 mol of strong acid. Without the buffer, adding 0.010 mol HCl to 1.0 L of pure water would give pH 2.0, a drop of 5.0 units.

pKa and Temperature Effects

All pKa values are temperature-dependent. Most chemistry tables report pKa at 25 degrees Celsius, but laboratory and biological conditions may differ. The van't Hoff equation describes how Ka (and therefore pKa) changes with temperature.

Tris is notoriously temperature-sensitive. A Tris buffer adjusted to pH 8.07 at 25 degrees Celsius will have pH 8.82 at 4 degrees Celsius, a shift of 0.75 units. For cold-room work, always adjust pH at the working temperature. Phosphate and acetate buffers are much more stable across temperature ranges, making them preferred for applications where temperature varies.

Ionic Strength Effects on pH

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation uses concentrations, but thermodynamically rigorous calculations use activities. At high ionic strength, the activity coefficients of ions differ from 1.0, meaning the effective concentrations are not equal to the analytical concentrations.

For most practical buffer work below 0.5 M total ionic strength, the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation gives results accurate to about 0.1 pH units. For higher precision, the Davies equation or specific ion interaction theory can correct for ionic strength effects. In general, increasing ionic strength tends to lower the pKa of cationic acids (like Tris-H+) and raise the pKa of anionic acids (like H2PO4−).

Practical takeaway: when preparing buffers for precise work, always verify the final pH with a calibrated pH meter rather than relying solely on calculations.

Polyprotic Acids and Buffers

Polyprotic acids have more than one ionizable hydrogen. Each deprotonation step has its own Ka and pKa. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation applies to each step independently, as long as the pKa values are sufficiently separated (by at least 2 units, which is the case for most common polyprotic acids).

Phosphoric Acid (H3PO4)

Phosphoric acid has three ionization steps:

  • H3PO4 ↔ H+ + H2PO4− (pKa1 = 2.15)
  • H2PO4− ↔ H+ + HPO42− (pKa2 = 7.20)
  • HPO42− ↔ H+ + PO43− (pKa3 = 12.35)

The second dissociation (pKa2 = 7.20) makes phosphate one of the most popular biological buffers. At physiological pH, the predominant species are H2PO4− and HPO42−, which form the buffer pair. Phosphate buffers are inexpensive, non-toxic, and have minimal temperature dependence.

Citric Acid

Citric acid is a triprotic acid with pKa values at 3.13, 4.76, and 6.40. Because the second and third pKa values are only 1.64 units apart, there is some overlap in their buffer regions. Citrate buffers are commonly used in food science and enzymology for pH ranges from 3 to 7.

Carbonic Acid (CO2/H2CO3)

Carbonic acid is diprotic: H2CO3 ↔ H+ + HCO3− (pKa1 = 6.35) and HCO3− ↔ H+ + CO32− (pKa2 = 10.33). The first equilibrium governs the blood bicarbonate buffer. The second is relevant in water treatment and geological processes like limestone dissolution.

Amino Acids as Buffers

Amino acids are zwitterionic at physiological pH, with both a carboxyl group (pKa around 2) and an amino group (pKa around 9-10). Some amino acids have ionizable side chains that provide additional buffering. Histidine (side chain pKa 6.0) is particularly effective at near-physiological pH. Glycine buffers are commonly used in gel electrophoresis at both acidic (glycine-HCl, pH 2-3) and basic (glycine-NaOH, pH 9-11) ranges.

Buffer Troubleshooting Guide

Even carefully prepared buffers can present problems. Here are common issues and how to address them.

pH Drifts After Preparation

If the pH changes over hours or days, check for CO2 absorption from the atmosphere (lowers pH of alkaline buffers), microbial growth (produces acidic metabolites), or slow dissolution of poorly soluble components. Store buffers in sealed containers. Autoclave or filter-sterilize buffers used for cell culture.

Buffer Interferes with the Assay

Phosphate buffers chelate divalent cations (Mg2+, Ca2+, Mn2+), which can inhibit metalloenzymes. Tris buffers react with some aldehydes and can inhibit certain enzymes. HEPES and MOPS are "Good's buffers" designed to minimize interference with biological reactions. Always check the literature for known incompatibilities before choosing a buffer.

Cannot Reach Target pH

If adding acid or base does not bring the solution to the target pH, the target may be outside the buffer's effective range (pKa plus or minus 1). Switch to a buffer system with a pKa closer to the desired pH. Also verify that the pH meter is properly calibrated with fresh standard solutions.

Precipitation During Preparation

Some buffer salts have limited solubility. Sodium phosphate dibasic (Na2HPO4) is very soluble, but calcium phosphate precipitates readily. If preparing phosphate-buffered saline (PBS), add salts in the correct order and use continuous stirring. Some buffer concentrates crystallize when stored cold; warm to room temperature before use.

Wrong pH After Autoclaving

Autoclaving (121 degrees Celsius, 15 minutes) can shift buffer pH. Tris buffers drop in pH by about 0.5 units. Phosphate buffers are relatively stable. HEPES may produce hydrogen peroxide at high temperatures. For pH-sensitive applications, measure and readjust pH after autoclaving, or filter-sterilize instead.

pH Indicators and Buffers

pH indicators are weak acids or bases that change color at specific pH values. They work on the same principle as buffers: the ratio of protonated to deprotonated forms determines the observed color. Understanding the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation helps explain indicator behavior.

How Indicators Work

An indicator HIn exists in two forms: HIn (acid form, one color) and In− (base form, another color). The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation for the indicator is: pH = pKIn + log([In−]/[HIn]). When the ratio [In−]/[HIn] is between 0.1 and 10 (pH within 1 unit of pKIn), a color transition is visible.

For a weak acid / strong base titration (equivalence point above pH 7), phenolphthalein is often the best choice.

Mathematical Derivation of the Equation

Understanding where the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation comes from builds intuition about its assumptions and limitations.

Step 1: Start from the Ka Expression

For a weak acid HA dissociating in water:

HA ↔ H+ + A−

Ka = [H+][A−] / [HA]

Step 2: Solve for [H+]

[H+] = Ka · [HA] / [A−]

Step 3: Take the Negative Logarithm

-log[H+] = -log(Ka) + -log([HA] / [A−])

pH = pKa + log([A−] / [HA])

Assumptions

  • The acid is weak (Ka is small relative to concentrations), so the degree of dissociation is small.
  • Concentrations can be used as a reasonable approximation of activities (valid at low ionic strength).
  • The equilibrium concentrations of [A−] and [HA] are approximately equal to the analytical (prepared) concentrations. This breaks down for very dilute buffers or when pH is far from pKa.
  • Water's autoionization does not contribute significantly to [H+] or [OH−]. This fails at extreme pH values (below 2 or above 12).

When the Equation Breaks Down

The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation gives inaccurate results when:

  • The buffer is extremely dilute (total concentration below 0.001 M), because water autoionization becomes significant.
  • The acid is not truly "weak" (Ka greater than 0.01), because significant dissociation changes the equilibrium concentrations from the analytical concentrations.
  • pH is far from pKa (more than 2 units), where the ratio [A−]/[HA] exceeds 100:1 or is below 1:100.
  • Ionic strength is high (above 0.5 M), where activity corrections become significant.

In these edge cases, a full equilibrium calculation using the Ka expression directly (often requiring a quadratic or cubic equation) gives more accurate results.

Types of Acid-Base Titrations

The shape of a titration curve depends on the strengths of the acid and base involved. Understanding these patterns helps interpret titration data correctly.

Strong Acid + Strong Base

The curve starts at low pH, rises slowly, then jumps steeply through pH 7 at the equivalence point. There is no buffer region. The pH at the equivalence point is exactly 7.0 because the products (water and a neutral salt) do not affect pH.

Weak Acid + Strong Base

This is the case modeled by the titration curve generator above. The initial pH is higher than for a strong acid of the same concentration. There is a clear buffer region around the half-equivalence point where pH = pKa. The equivalence point pH is above 7 because the conjugate base A− is a weak base that reacts with water to produce OH−.

Weak Base + Strong Acid

The curve is an inverted version of weak acid + strong base. It starts at high pH, has a buffer region where pH = pKa of the conjugate acid, and the equivalence point pH is below 7.

Polyprotic Acid Titrations

Polyprotic acids show multiple equivalence points, one for each ionizable proton. Phosphoric acid titrated with NaOH shows three distinct buffer regions (near pH 2.15, 7.20, and 12.35) and three equivalence points. The titration curve looks like a staircase, with each step corresponding to the neutralization of one proton.

Practice Problems

Work through these problems to build confidence with the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation. Solve each one by hand, then use the calculator above to verify.

Problem 1: Basic pH Calculation

Question: A buffer contains 0.30 M acetic acid and 0.45 M sodium acetate. What is the pH? (pKa of acetic acid = 4.76)

Answer: pH = 4.76 + log(0.45/0.30) = 4.76 + log(1.50) = 4.76 + 0.176 = 4.94

Problem 2: Finding the Required Ratio

Question: What ratio of [NH3]/[NH4+] is needed to make a buffer at pH 9.50? (pKa of NH4+ = 9.25)

Answer: 9.50 = 9.25 + log(ratio). log(ratio) = 0.25. Ratio = 100.25 = 1.78. You need 1.78 times as much ammonia as ammonium.

Problem 3: Effect of Adding Acid

Question: A phosphate buffer at pH 7.40 contains 0.050 mol HPO42− and 0.030 mol H2PO4− in 1.0 L. What is the pH after adding 0.005 mol HCl?

Answer: HCl converts base to acid. New HPO42− = 0.045 M; new H2PO4− = 0.035 M. pH = 7.20 + log(0.045/0.035) = 7.20 + 0.109 = 7.31. The pH dropped by only 0.09 units.

Problem 4: Preparing a Buffer

Question: How many grams of sodium acetate (MW 82.03) must be added to 500 mL of 0.20 M acetic acid to prepare a pH 5.00 buffer?

Answer: 5.00 = 4.76 + log([A−]/0.20). log([A−]/0.20) = 0.24. [A−]/0.20 = 1.738. [A−] = 0.348 M. Moles = 0.348 × 0.500 = 0.174 mol. Mass = 0.174 × 82.03 = 14.27 g.

Problem 5: Buffer Capacity Comparison

Question: Which has greater buffer capacity: Buffer A (0.50 M total, pH = pKa) or Buffer B (0.10 M total, pH = pKa)?

Answer: Buffer A has 5 times the capacity. Buffer capacity at pH = pKa is beta = 2.303 × C × 0.25. For A: beta = 2.303 × 0.50 × 0.25 = 0.288. For B: beta = 2.303 × 0.10 × 0.25 = 0.058. Buffer A has 5x more capacity.

Problem 6: Blood pH

Question: A patient has [HCO3−] = 18 mM and pCO2 = 40 mmHg. What is the blood pH? Is this normal? (Use apparent pKa = 6.1, dissolved CO2 = 0.03 × pCO2)

Answer: [CO2] = 0.03 × 40 = 1.2 mM. pH = 6.1 + log(18/1.2) = 6.1 + log(15.0) = 6.1 + 1.176 = 7.28. This is below normal (7.35 to 7.45), indicating metabolic acidosis (low bicarbonate with normal pCO2).

Real-World Applications of Buffer Chemistry

Pharmaceutical Formulation

Many injectable drugs must be formulated at specific pH values for stability and to minimize pain at the injection site. Phosphate buffers (pH 6 to 8) and citrate buffers (pH 3 to 6) are commonly used in pharmaceutical formulations. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation guides the design of these formulations, ensuring the drug remains in its active form and the solution remains stable during shelf life.

Swimming Pool Chemistry

Pool water pH must be maintained between 7.2 and 7.8 for swimmer comfort and effective chlorine disinfection. At pH 7.5, about 50% of dissolved chlorine exists as hypochlorous acid (HOCl, the active disinfectant) and 50% as hypochlorite ion (OCl−, less effective). The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation with the pKa of HOCl (7.54) explains why: pH = 7.54 + log([OCl−]/[HOCl]) = 7.54 + log(1) = 7.54. Lowering pH increases the HOCl fraction, improving disinfection.

Fermentation and Brewing

Yeast activity depends strongly on pH. Most brewing yeasts work best at pH 4.0 to 5.5. The natural buffering of wort (the sugary liquid from mashing grain) comes from phosphates and amino acids in the malt. Brewers adjust mash pH using food-grade acids (lactic acid, phosphoric acid) or calcium salts. Understanding buffer chemistry helps predict how pH will change during fermentation as yeast produces CO2 and organic acids.

Soil Chemistry

Soil pH affects nutrient availability and plant growth. Most plants prefer pH 6.0 to 7.0. Soil acts as a buffer through clay minerals, organic matter, and the carbonate system. Adding lime (CaCO3) raises pH by introducing carbonate base; adding sulfur or aluminum sulfate lowers pH. The buffer capacity of soil determines how much amendment is needed per acre.

Clinical Diagnostics

Arterial blood gas (ABG) analysis is one of the most common diagnostic tests in hospital settings. The Henderson-Hasselbalch equation applied to the bicarbonate system helps clinicians distinguish between respiratory and metabolic causes of pH abnormalities, guide ventilator settings, and monitor the response to treatment. Electrolyte panels and anion gap calculations complement the ABG to provide a complete acid-base picture.

Good's Buffers for Biological Research

In 1966, Norman Good and colleagues published a set of criteria for buffers used in biological research. The resulting "Good's buffers" were specifically designed to minimize interference with biological systems.

Selection Criteria

  • pKa between 6.0 and 8.0 (physiological pH range)
  • High water solubility
  • Minimal membrane permeability (stays in the extracellular space)
  • Minimal metal ion binding
  • Chemically and enzymatically stable
  • Minimal absorption of ultraviolet or visible light
  • Known and manageable salt effects

When to Use Good's Buffers vs. Traditional Buffers

Use Good's buffers when your experiment involves live cells, enzymes sensitive to ionic interactions, or spectroscopic measurements in the UV range. Use simpler buffers (phosphate, Tris, acetate) for routine applications like gel electrophoresis, column chromatography, and protein purification where the buffer's specific properties are less critical. Cost is also a factor: phosphate buffer at $0.10/L versus HEPES at $1.50/L adds up when preparing large volumes for chromatography columns.

pH Measurement Methods

Accurate pH measurement is essential for preparing and verifying buffers. The two main methods are pH meters and pH indicator paper.

Glass Electrode pH Meters

A glass electrode pH meter measures the voltage across a thin glass membrane that is selectively permeable to hydrogen ions. It provides accuracy to 0.01 pH units when properly calibrated. Calibrate before each use with at least two standard buffer solutions (typically pH 4.00 and 7.00, or pH 7.00 and 10.00, depending on the range of interest). Store the electrode in storage solution (usually 3 M KCl), never in deionized water.

Common Calibration Errors

  • Using expired buffers. pH standards absorb CO2 from the air over time, changing their pH. Use fresh solutions.
  • Temperature mismatch. Most pH meters have automatic temperature compensation (ATC), but calibration should still be performed near the measurement temperature.
  • Dirty electrode. Protein deposits, oils, and salt crystals on the glass membrane degrade readings. Clean with appropriate solutions (pepsin/HCl for proteins, ethanol for oils).
  • Air bubbles. Bubbles trapped near the glass membrane or reference junction cause erratic readings. Gently swirl the electrode after immersion.

pH Paper and Indicator Strips

pH paper provides a quick, approximate measurement (accuracy of 0.5 to 1.0 pH unit). Universal indicator paper covers pH 1 to 14 with color changes. Narrow-range strips (e.g., pH 6.0 to 7.5) offer better resolution for specific applications. pH paper is useful for quick checks, field work, and situations where a pH meter is unavailable. It should not be used when precision better than 0.5 units is required.

Digital pH Pens

Portable pH pens combine the convenience of paper strips with the accuracy of bench-top meters. Most offer accuracy of 0.1 pH units and are suitable for field work, food science, and educational settings. They require calibration but are less delicate than research-grade glass electrodes.

Spectrophotometric pH Measurement

For environments where glass electrodes fail (high temperatures, very low ionic strength, or inside sealed vessels), spectrophotometric pH measurement using indicator dyes provides an alternative. A known concentration of pH-sensitive dye is added to the solution, and the absorbance ratio at two wavelengths gives the [A−]/[HIn] ratio. Applying the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation with the known pKIn of the dye yields the pH. This technique is widely used in oceanography, where the precision of glass electrodes is insufficient to detect the small pH changes associated with ocean acidification (about 0.002 pH units per year).

Intracellular pH Measurement

Measuring pH inside living cells requires specialized techniques. Fluorescent pH indicators (BCECF, SNARF, pHluorin) are loaded into cells and imaged with a fluorescence microscope. The ratio of fluorescence intensity at two excitation wavelengths is calibrated against known pH buffers using the Henderson-Hasselbalch relationship. This approach provides spatial resolution to map pH variations within different cellular compartments, revealing that lysosomes maintain pH 4.5 to 5.0 while the cytoplasm stays near 7.2.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation?

It is pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA]), relating the pH of a buffer solution to the acid dissociation constant (pKa) and the ratio of conjugate base to weak acid concentrations. It is fundamental for understanding buffer chemistry.

How do I calculate pH using Henderson-Hasselbalch?

Enter the pKa of the weak acid, the conjugate base concentration [A-], and the weak acid concentration [HA] into the calculator above. The formula is pH = pKa + log([A-]/[HA]). The calculator shows every step of the solution.

What is buffer capacity and why does it matter?

Buffer capacity is the amount of strong acid or base a buffer can absorb before the pH changes significantly. It is highest when pH equals the pKa and increases with total buffer concentration. It matters because experiments and biological processes require stable pH.

How do I choose the right buffer system?

Select a buffer with a pKa within 1.0 pH unit of your target pH. Check the common buffer systems table above for options. Also consider potential interactions with your experiment, such as metal chelation by phosphate or temperature sensitivity of Tris.

What is a titration curve?

A titration curve plots pH against the volume of titrant added. For a weak acid titrated with strong base, it shows a buffer zone near the pKa, an equivalence point where all acid has been neutralized, and a steep rise in pH. The half-equivalence point is where pH = pKa.

What is the effective pH range of a buffer?

Generally pKa plus or minus 1.0 pH unit. Outside this range, the ratio of conjugate base to acid exceeds 10:1 or drops below 1:10, and the buffer no longer resists pH changes effectively. Staying within this range is essential for reliable buffering.
