Understanding Time Zones

This explainer covers how time zones work, why we have them, and the history behind UTC, GMT, and the International Date Line.

How Timezone Conversion Works

Every timezone on Earth is defined as an offset from Coordinated Universal Time, or UTC. When you convert a time from one zone to another, the process is straightforward: subtract the source offset from the local time to get UTC, then add the target offset to get the destination time. For example, converting 3:00 PM EST (UTC-5) to CET (UTC+1) means adding 6 hours to get 9:00 PM CET.

What makes this tricky in practice is Daylight Saving Time. During DST transitions, the UTC offset for a timezone changes temporarily. EST (UTC-5) becomes EDT (UTC-4) in the summer, meaning the difference between New York and London shrinks from 5 hours to 4 hours for part of the year. This converter handles those transitions automatically by using the IANA timezone database, which tracks every DST rule worldwide.

The IANA Timezone Database

The IANA timezone database (also known as tz or zoneinfo) is the authoritative source for timezone rules worldwide. Maintained by a community of volunteers and overseen by ICANN, it contains historical and current timezone data for every region. Modern browsers implement this database through the Intl.DateTimeFormat API, which is what this converter uses for all calculations.

Each IANA timezone identifier follows a Region/City format, such as America/New_York, Europe/London, or Asia/Tokyo. This naming convention was chosen because city names are less ambiguous than abbreviations. For instance, "CST" could mean Central Standard Time (UTC-6), China Standard Time (UTC+8), or Cuba Standard Time (UTC-5).

Daylight Saving Time Explained

DST was first proposed by George Vernon Hudson in 1895 and has been adopted and abandoned by various countries ever since. Currently, about 70 countries observe some form of DST. The basic idea is to move clocks forward by one hour in spring and back in fall so that evenings have more daylight during the warmer months.

The transition dates vary by country. In the United States, DST begins on the second Sunday of March and ends on the first Sunday of November. In the European Union, it starts on the last Sunday of March and ends on the last Sunday of October. Australia's DST runs from the first Sunday of October to the first Sunday of April (reversed seasons in the Southern Hemisphere).

Not all regions observe DST. Arizona (except the Navajo Nation), Hawaii, most of Saskatchewan, Iceland, China, Japan, India, and large portions of Africa and South America don't change their clocks. This means the offset between two cities can change by 0, 1, or even 2 hours depending on the time of year and whether one, both, or neither observe DST.

The International Date Line

The International Date Line (IDL) is an imaginary boundary that runs roughly along the 180th meridian in the Pacific Ocean. When you cross it traveling westward, you skip ahead one calendar day. Crossing eastward, you repeat a day. The line doesn't follow the meridian exactly because it zigzags to avoid splitting countries across two dates.

Some interesting consequences of the Samoa switched from the east side to the west side of the date line in December 2011, skipping December 30 entirely. The Republic of Kiribati spans the date line, with the Line Islands using UTC+14, making them the first places on Earth to enter each new day. This means Kiribati's time is 26 hours ahead of American Samoa (UTC-11), despite being only a short distance apart geographically.

Fractional UTC Offsets

While most timezones use whole-hour offsets from UTC, several use 30- or 45-minute offsets. India Standard Time (IST) is UTC+5:30, covering the entire country despite its east-west span of about 30 degrees longitude. Iran uses UTC+3:30, and Nepal is the only country at UTC+5:45. The Chatham Islands of New Zealand use UTC+12:45 (UTC+13:45 during DST).

These fractional offsets exist because countries wanted solar noon to more closely match clock noon for the majority of their population. India chose +5:30 as a compromise between Kolkata's natural time (closer to +6) and Mumbai's (closer to +5). This decision, made at independence in 1947, has been debated ever since, with some advocating for two time zones.

Best Practices for Scheduling Across Time Zones

When scheduling meetings across multiple timezones, I've found a few strategies that consistently work well. First, always share the time in UTC along with the local time for each participant. This eliminates ambiguity, especially around DST transitions. Second, rotate meeting times if your team spans many zones so that the inconvenience of early mornings or late evenings is shared equitably.

For teams spread between the US West Coast (UTC-8/-7) and Asia (UTC+8/+9), there is essentially zero overlap during standard 9-5 business hours. The most common solution I've found is to use an "early bird / night owl" approach where one team starts slightly early and the other stays slightly late, creating a 1-2 hour window. The meeting planner tab in this tool visualizes exactly where those windows fall.

Another consideration is that some countries have a single timezone despite spanning multiple natural zones. China uses UTC+8 for the entire country, meaning western China experiences sunrise as late as 10 AM in winter. Similarly, India's single timezone means eastern regions experience an effective solar time offset of nearly an hour from western regions.

Common Timezone Conversion Pairs

Based on the most frequent conversions I see, here are the key pairs and their typical offsets (standard time, not DST):

  • EST to GMT/UTC - +5 hours (New York to London)
  • PST to EST - +3 hours (Los Angeles to New York)
  • EST to IST - +10:30 hours (New York to Mumbai)
  • GMT to CET - +1 hour (London to Berlin)
  • PST to JST - +17 hours (Los Angeles to Tokyo)
  • EST to AEST - +15 hours (New York to Sydney)
  • CET to SGT - +7 hours (Berlin to Singapore)
  • GMT to GST - +4 hours (London to Dubai)

Military / NATO Time Zones

The military uses a phonetic alphabet system for timezones. UTC is "Zulu" (Z), which is why UTC times are often written with a Z suffix (e.g., 1400Z). East of UTC, zones are labeled Alpha (A, UTC+1) through Mike (M, UTC+12), skipping J. West of UTC, zones run from November (N, UTC-1) through Yankee (Y, UTC-12). Juliet (J) is reserved for the observer's local time.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a time zone converter?

A time zone converter translates a specific date and time from one timezone to another, accounting for UTC offsets and Daylight Saving Time transitions automatically. This tool supports 50+ IANA timezones and can compare up to 6 zones simultaneously.

How does Daylight Saving Time affect time conversions?

DST shifts clocks forward or backward by one hour at specific dates, changing the UTC offset temporarily. A reliable converter accounts for these transitions automatically using IANA timezone data. During transition periods, the offset between two cities can change by 1 or even 2 hours.

What is UTC and why is it used?

UTC (Coordinated Universal Time) is the global time standard from which all time zones are defined as offsets. It does not observe DST, making it the universal reference point for scheduling across zones. UTC replaced GMT as the international standard in 1972.

How do I find business hours overlap between time zones?

Use the Meeting Planner tab to select multiple participant timezones and define working hours. The tool highlights the overlapping hours where all participants are within business hours. You can also use the Business Hours Overlap tab for a visual two-zone comparison.

What is the International Date Line?

The International Date Line (IDL) is an imaginary line at roughly 180 degrees longitude. Crossing it westward advances the calendar by one day, and crossing it eastward moves the calendar back one day. The line zigzags to avoid splitting countries across two calendar dates.

How many time zones are there in the world?

There are 38 distinct UTC offsets in use worldwide, ranging from UTC-12 to UTC+14. Some offsets are in 30- or 45-minute increments, such as India (UTC+5:30), Iran (UTC+3:30), and Nepal (UTC+5:45).

Does this converter work offline?

Yes. After the initial page load, all time zone calculations run entirely in your browser using the -in JavaScript Intl API. No internet connection is required for conversions.

Why do some time zones have 30- or 45-minute offsets?

Some countries chose fractional offsets to better align noon with solar noon for their geographic location. India uses UTC+5:30, Iran uses UTC+3:30, Nepal uses UTC+5:45, and the Chatham Islands use UTC+12:45.
