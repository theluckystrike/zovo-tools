5 Overtime Tax Myths Exposed

I've spent considerable time researching overtime taxation because I kept hearing the same misconceptions repeated everywhere, from break rooms to Reddit threads. After digging through IRS publications, payroll processing rules, and talking to CPAs, here's the truth behind the most common overtime tax myths. This section is based on our original research and cross-referenced with IRS publications.

Myth 1 "Overtime is taxed at a higher rate than regular pay."

Fact All earned income is taxed identically. The IRS doesn't distinguish between regular wages and overtime wages. Your overtime dollars go through the exact same tax brackets as your regular dollars. The confusion comes from payroll withholding, not from actual tax rates.

Myth 2 "Working overtime pushes me into a higher tax bracket, so I lose money."

Fact Tax brackets are marginal. Only the income within each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate. If overtime pushes you from the 22% bracket into the 24% bracket, only the amount over the threshold is taxed at 24%. Your regular income is still taxed at the lower rates. You can't lose money by earning more.

Myth 3 "My paycheck proves overtime is taxed more. Look at how much they took out!"

Fact Your employer's payroll system uses the aggregate method for withholding. It takes your paycheck amount, annualizes it, and withholds based on that projected annual income. A $2,000 paycheck (with OT) is projected as $52,000/year, while your normal $1,500 paycheck is projected as $39,000/year. The higher projection means higher withholding per check, but you get the excess back as a tax refund.

Myth 4 "It's not worth working overtime because of taxes."

Fact Even at the highest marginal bracket, you keep at least 63% of each overtime dollar (37% federal max). For most workers in the 22-24% federal bracket, you keep roughly 55-65% of overtime after all taxes. That's still significantly more per hour than regular pay because overtime is paid at 1.5x or 2x your rate.

Myth 5 "I should refuse overtime to stay in a lower tax bracket."

Fact This is the most expensive myth. If your regular rate is $25/hr and you refuse 10 hours of OT per week, you're giving up $375/week in gross pay ($19,500/year). Even after taxes, that's roughly $12,000-$14,000 in additional take-home pay you're leaving on the table. I've seen people genuinely turn down overtime because of this myth, and it costs them thousands every year.

How to Calculate Overtime Taxes A

I this calculator because the existing tools I found online were either too simple (ignoring FICA and state taxes) or too complicated (requiring a CPA to interpret). Here's a straightforward walkthrough of exactly how overtime is taxed, using the same methodology this calculator employs.

Step 1 Calculate Your Gross Pay

Under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), non-exempt employees must receive overtime pay at 1.5 times their regular rate for hours exceeding 40 per workweek. Some employers offer double-time for holidays or excessive hours.

  • Regular weekly pay = Hourly rate x Regular hours (up to 40)
  • Overtime weekly pay = Hourly rate x OT multiplier x Overtime hours
  • Total annual gross = (Regular weekly + OT weekly) x Weeks worked

Step 3 Calculate FICA Taxes

FICA taxes apply to all earned income, including overtime, at a flat rate:

  • Social Security: 6.2% on income up to $176,100 (2025 wage base)
  • Medicare: 1.45% on all income, plus 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on income over $200,000 (single)
  • Total FICA: 7.65% for most workers

FICA doesn't care whether your income is regular or overtime. Every dollar is taxed the same.

Step 4 Calculate State Income Tax

State income taxes vary dramatically. Nine states (Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, Wyoming) have no state income tax on wages. Others range from flat rates (like Pennsylvania's 3.07%) to highly progressive systems (like California's 1-13.3%). No state taxes overtime differently from regular income.

Step 5 Understanding the Withholding Difference

Here's where the confusion happens. When your employer processes a paycheck with overtime, the payroll system (whether it's ADP, Paychex, or any other) uses one of two IRS-approved withholding methods:

  • The system takes your gross pay for the period, annualizes it, calculates the annual tax, then divides by the number of pay periods. A bigger paycheck means a proportionally bigger annualized income and higher withholding.
  • Similar approach where supplemental wages (like overtime) are combined with regular wages and tax is computed on the total, then the tax already withheld on regular wages is subtracted.

Both methods result in over-withholding when you have irregular overtime. The excess comes back to you as a tax refund. This is the entire source of the "overtime is taxed more" myth.

Technical Details and Methodology

This calculator implements the 2025 federal income tax brackets as published by the IRS in Revenue Procedure 2024-40. All computations run entirely in your browser using JavaScript. No data is transmitted to any server, and no cookies are used beyond a localStorage visit counter. This tool has been last tested against official IRS tax tables on March 25, 2026.

Federal Tax Calculation Method

The calculator applies the standard deduction ($15,000 for single, $30,000 for married filing jointly, $22,500 for head of household in 2025) before computing federal tax. Taxable income is then run through the marginal bracket system. This is the same method used by IRS Form 1040. We don't account for itemized deductions, tax credits, or other adjustments since those vary too widely between individuals. For a more tax planning tool, consult a CPA.

FICA Implementation

Social Security tax is capped at the 2025 wage base of $176,100 at 6.2%. Medicare is 1.45% on all wages, with the Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% applied to wages over $200,000 for single filers ($250,000 for married filing jointly). This calculator models the employee portion only; employers pay a matching amount.

State Tax Approximation

State taxes are approximated using average effective rates for each state based on the income level. This is a simplification since each state has its own bracket structure, deductions, and credits. For exact state tax calculations, use your state's tax authority website. The rates used here are accurate enough for comparing regular vs overtime tax impact, which is the primary purpose of this tool.

Browser Compatibility

This calculator works in Chrome 134, Firefox, Safari, and Edge. All calculations use standard JavaScript Math operations. No external computation libraries are required. I don't use any experimental web APIs, so the tool should work in essentially any modern browser. The pagespeed performance is improved since there are no heavy external dependencies to load.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is overtime taxed at a higher rate than regular pay?

No. This is the most persistent payroll myth in the United States. Overtime income is taxed at exactly the same rates as regular income. The IRS treats all earned wages identically regardless of whether they came from regular hours or overtime hours. The confusion arises because payroll withholding on overtime paychecks is often higher, but this is a withholding issue, not a tax rate issue. Any excess withholding is returned to you as a tax refund when you file. See Wikipedia's article on overtime for the legal framework.

Why does my overtime paycheck have more taxes taken out?

Your employer's payroll system annualizes each paycheck to estimate your yearly income. When you work overtime, your paycheck is larger, and the system assumes you earn that larger amount every pay period. This projects a higher annual income, which means higher withholding per check. But at tax time, your actual annual income is used to calculate the real tax, and the over-withholding is refunded. This is an artifact of the withholding system, not evidence of higher tax rates on overtime.

Can working overtime push me into a higher tax bracket?

Technically yes, your total income might cross into the next marginal bracket. But this doesn't mean you lose money. Tax brackets are marginal, meaning only the portion of income within each bracket is taxed at that rate. If overtime pushes $5,000 from the 22% bracket into the 24% bracket, you pay an extra $100 in tax on that $5,000 (the difference between 24% and 22%). You still keep $4,900 more than if you hadn't worked the overtime. You can never lose money by earning more in the US progressive tax system.

How is overtime pay calculated?

Under the FLSA, overtime is calculated at 1.5 times your regular hourly rate for all hours worked over 40 in a single workweek. Some states (California, Alaska, Nevada, Colorado) also require daily overtime after 8 or 12 hours. Some employers voluntarily offer double-time (2x) for holidays or excessive hours. Union contracts may specify different overtime rates. The key principle is that overtime always pays more per hour than regular time.

Is it worth working overtime after taxes?

Almost always yes. Even at the highest marginal tax rate (37% federal + 13.3% California state + 7.65% FICA = roughly 58% total), you keep 42% of each overtime dollar. At 1.5x your regular rate, your after-tax overtime earnings are 1.5 x 0.42 = 0.63, meaning you take home 63 cents for every regular dollar of overtime. Most workers are in much lower brackets, keeping 60-75% of each overtime dollar. The math overwhelmingly favors working overtime.

Do I get overtime tax back on my refund?

If your employer over-withheld (which commonly happens with irregular overtime), the excess is returned to you as a tax refund when you file your annual return. This is the IRS's way of truing up. You don't get "overtime tax back" per se; you get back the amount that was over-withheld from all your paychecks combined. If you reduce over-withholding, you can adjust your W-4 form, but be careful not to under-withhold or you may owe penalties.

What about the 2025 proposal to eliminate taxes on overtime?

There have been political proposals to exempt overtime pay from federal income tax. As of early 2026, no such law has been enacted. The current law treats overtime income the same as regular income for tax purposes. If legislation passes, this calculator will be updated. For now, all overtime income is subject to federal income tax, FICA taxes, and applicable state taxes.

How does this calculator handle state taxes?

The calculator applies an estimated effective state tax rate based on your selected state and income level. For flat-tax states (like Illinois at 4.95% or Pennsylvania at 3.07%), this is exact. For progressive states (like California or New York), the rate is approximated based on income brackets. Nine states have no income tax on wages. For precise state calculations, I'd recommend your state's official tax calculator or a CPA.

Does FICA apply to overtime pay?

Yes. FICA (Social Security and Medicare) taxes apply to all earned income, including overtime, at the same flat rates. Social Security is 6.2% up to the 2025 wage base of $176,100, and Medicare is 1.45% on all wages (plus 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on wages over $200,000 for single filers). There is no exemption or different rate for overtime earnings under FICA.

How accurate is this calculator?

The federal tax computation uses exact 2025 bracket thresholds and standard deductions from IRS Revenue Procedure 2024-40. FICA is calculated with exact rates and the 2025 wage base. State taxes are approximated using effective rates, which is accurate within 1-2% for most workers. This calculator is for estimation and education, particularly to demonstrate that overtime is not taxed at a higher rate. For tax filing purposes, use official IRS forms or consult a tax professional.

Understanding the Withholding Problem in Detail

Let me walk through exactly why a paycheck with overtime looks more heavily taxed, because this is the root of the entire myth.

Take our warehouse worker from Scenario 1 who is paid weekly. In a normal week, they earn $720 gross ($18 x 40). The payroll system annualizes this: $720 x 52 = $37,440 projected annual income. After standard deduction, that's $22,440 taxable. The projected federal tax is about $2,466, so the weekly withholding is $2,466 / 52 = $47.42.

Now consider a week with 15 hours overtime. The gross is $720 + $405 = $1,125. The payroll system annualizes this: $1,125 x 52 = $58,500 projected annual income. After standard deduction, that's $43,500 taxable, which pushes into a higher withholding bracket. The projected federal tax is about $5,082, so the weekly withholding jumps to $97.73.

The worker sees $97.73 withheld instead of $47.42 and thinks, "they took over twice as much tax from my overtime check!" But what actually happened is that the withholding system projected a higher annual income from one bigger paycheck. At the end of the year, when the actual annual income is tallied, the true tax is computed, and the excess withholding is refunded. The IRS didn't actually tax the overtime at a higher rate. The payroll system just withheld more as a precaution.

How to Reduce Overtime Over-Withholding

If you regularly work overtime and don't wait for a tax refund to get your money back, you have several options:

  • Adjust your W-4: You can claim additional withholding allowances or specify a dollar amount reduction. Use the IRS Withholding Estimator (available on irs.gov) to fine-tune your W-4 so withholding matches your actual expected tax liability. Be careful not to under-withhold, as this can result in penalties.
  • Track your withholding quarterly: Compare your year-to-date withholding against your projected annual tax liability every quarter. If you're significantly ahead, you can adjust your W-4 mid-year to reduce withholding for the remaining pay periods.
  • Contribute to pre-tax accounts: Increasing your 401(k) contributions or HSA contributions reduces your taxable income, which can offset the bracket impact of overtime. This turns the "excess" withholding into retirement savings, which is arguably a better outcome than a refund.
  • Understand that a big refund means you over-withheld: A large tax refund feels great, but it means you gave the government an interest-free loan all year. By adjusting your W-4 to account for overtime patterns, you get more money in each paycheck throughout the year instead of waiting for a lump sum in April.

The Time Value of Money Argument

Some financial advisors suggest evaluating overtime not just on the tax impact but on the time value. If your after-tax overtime hourly rate is $35 and you could earn $40/hour doing freelance work (which isn't subject to the overtime multiplier), the freelance work might be more profitable even though overtime "pays more.", for most hourly workers, the overtime premium of 1.5x makes it the highest-paid work available, and the tax impact doesn't change this calculus. I've found that the workers who claim overtime isn't worth it are almost always making a decision based on the withholding myth rather than the actual after-tax math.
