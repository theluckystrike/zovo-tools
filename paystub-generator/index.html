How Pay Stubs Work and What Each Section Means

A pay stub, also called an earnings statement or check stub, is the document that accompanies your paycheck. It shows exactly how much you earned, what was taken out, and what you're left with. Every employer who withholds taxes is required to provide this information, though the specific format varies.

The top section identifies the employer and employee. You'll see the company name, address, and often an Employer Identification Number (EIN). Below that is your name, address, and employee ID. Some stubs include the last four digits of your Social Security number, though this practice is becoming less common for security reasons.

The earnings section breaks down your pay. For hourly employees, it shows the number of regular hours, overtime hours, and the corresponding rates. For salaried employees, it shows the per-period amount derived from the annual salary. Additional earnings like bonuses, commissions, and tips appear as separate line items.

Understanding Your Deductions

Deductions fall into two categories. Mandatory deductions are required by law. These include federal income tax, state income tax (in most states), Social Security tax, and Medicare tax. Together, Social Security and Medicare are called FICA taxes, named after the Federal Insurance Contributions Act.

Voluntary deductions are things you've opted into. Common examples include 401(k) contributions, health insurance premiums, HSA contributions, life insurance, and union dues. Pre-tax deductions reduce your taxable income, while post-tax deductions don't. The distinction matters because pre-tax deductions like 401(k) contributions lower both your income tax and your take-home pay, effectively giving you a tax break on those dollars.

FICA Breakdown

Social Security tax is 6.2% of your gross earnings, up to the annual wage base limit ($168,600 for 2025). Your employer pays a matching 6.2%. Once you've earned above the wage base, you stop paying Social Security tax for the rest of the year. Medicare tax is 1.45% on all earnings, with no cap. If you earn above $200,000, you pay an additional 0.9% Medicare tax on the excess. This check stub maker calculates both automatically.

Payroll is the process by which employers pay an employee for the work they have performed. Payroll is also used to refer to the total of all compensation a business must pay to its employees for a set period of time or on a given date. 5 Pay Stub Scenarios Explained

1. Hourly Employee with Straight Time

An employee earning $20/hour works 80 hours in a bi-weekly pay period. Gross pay is $1,600. Federal tax might be around $160 (depending on filing status and allowances), state tax varies, Social Security is $99.20, and Medicare is $23.20. With a $100 401(k) contribution and $85 health insurance deduction, net pay comes to roughly $1,080. The actual numbers depend heavily on location and filing status, which is why this paystub maker lets you customize every variable.

2. Salaried Employee

Someone earning $65,000 annually, paid bi-weekly, receives $2,500 gross per period. The per-period calculation is straightforward: divide the annual salary by the number of pay periods (26 for bi-weekly). Deductions follow the same rules as hourly pay. The difference is that salaried employees don't track hours on their stub, though some employers still show "80 hours" to indicate a full-time period.

3. With Overtime

Under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), non-exempt employees earn 1.5x their regular rate for hours worked beyond 40 in a workweek. If you earn $25/hour and work 45 hours, that's 40 hours at $25 ($1,000) plus 5 hours at $37.50 ($187.50), totaling $1,187.50 for the week. Over a two-week pay period with similar overtime, your stub would show $2,375 gross. The overtime rate is always calculated from the regular rate, not from any shift differential or premium pay.

4. With Bonus

Bonuses appear as separate line items in the earnings section. A $500 bonus on top of $2,000 regular pay gives $2,500 gross. The important thing to know is that bonuses can be taxed differently. Employers often use the supplemental flat rate of 22% for federal tax on bonuses, or they might aggregate it with regular pay and calculate withholding on the combined amount. This free pay stub template uses the aggregation method for simplicity.

5. Multiple Deductions

A more realistic pay stub might include 401(k) at 6% of gross, health insurance at $150/period, dental at $25, vision at $10, HSA contribution of $50, and life insurance at $15. That's $250+ in voluntary deductions on top of mandatory taxes. When people see their first "real" paycheck and it's significantly less than they expected, the deductions are usually why. Common Pay Stub Abbreviations Decoded

Pay stubs are notorious for cryptic abbreviations. Here are the ones you'll see most often. FED or FIT means federal income tax. FICA covers both Social Security (sometimes listed as OASDI) and Medicare (sometimes MED or MEDI). SIT or SWT means state income tax. REG is regular pay, OT is overtime, and HOL is holiday pay.

On the deductions side, you might see 401K or RET for retirement contributions, MED or HLT for health insurance, DEN for dental, VIS for vision, LIF for life insurance, and UN for union dues. YTD next to any number means year-to-date, the accumulated total since January 1st. Understanding these abbreviations helps you verify that your pay is correct and catch errors before they compound.

Pay Stub vs W-2 vs 1099

These three documents serve different purposes, and mixing them up can cause confusion during tax season. A pay stub is issued every pay period and shows current earnings and deductions alongside year-to-date totals. It's a running record that resets each January.

A W-2 is an annual summary your employer sends by January 31st for the previous tax year. It shows total wages, taxes withheld, and benefits. You use it to file your income tax return. The numbers on your last pay stub of the year should match your W-2 closely, though there can be minor differences due to adjustments.

A 1099 is for independent contractors and other non-employee income. If you receive a 1099-NEC, it means taxes weren't withheld and you're responsible for paying self-employment tax (the full 15.3% FICA, since there's no employer match) plus income tax. Contractors don't receive pay stubs because they're not on payroll.

Federal Income Tax Brackets (2025)

Understanding how tax brackets work clears up a common misconception. Many people think that moving into a higher bracket means all their income is taxed at that rate. That's not how it works. Only the income within each bracket is taxed at that bracket's rate.

For a single filer in 2025, the first $11,600 is taxed at 10%. Income from $11,601 to $47,150 is taxed at 12%. From $47,151 to $100,525 at 22%. From $100,526 to $191,950 at 24%. From $191,951 to $243,725 at 32%. From $243,726 to $609,350 at 35%. Anything above $609,350 is taxed at 37%. This pay stub pdf generator uses these brackets to estimate federal withholding, though actual withholding depends on your W-4 elections.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this paystub generator completely free?

Yes. There are no hidden costs, no premium features locked behind a paywall, and no registration required. You can use this check stub maker as many times as you want.

Can I submit these pay stubs for a loan application?

No. These are for personal reference and education only. Lenders require official pay stubs issued by your employer's payroll system. Submitting a self-generated document as an official pay stub could constitute fraud.

How accurate are the tax calculations?

The federal tax calculation uses 2025 brackets with a simplified withholding method. It's generally within 2-5% of actual payroll calculations. State taxes use average effective rates, not full bracket systems. For exact numbers, you'd need a certified payroll system that accounts for your specific W-4 elections, local taxes, and year-to-date adjustments.

Is my personal information stored anywhere?

No. Everything runs in your browser using JavaScript. The names, addresses, SSN digits, and salary information you enter never leave your device. There's no server, no database, no cookies tracking your inputs. When you close the tab, it's gone.

How is overtime calculated?

Overtime is calculated at 1.5 times your regular hourly rate, following the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA). You enter regular hours and overtime hours separately. If you work 80 regular hours and 10 overtime hours in a bi-weekly period at $20/hour, that's $1,600 regular + $300 overtime = $1,900 gross.

What does FICA mean on my pay stub?

FICA stands for the Federal Insurance Contributions Act. Social Security (6.2% of gross pay up to $168,600) and Medicare (1.45% of all gross pay). Your employer pays a matching amount. Together, these fund Social Security retirement benefits and the Medicare health insurance program.

Can I print the pay stub?

Yes. Click the Print button and the tool opens a print-friendly view with a white background. It's formatted for standard letter-size paper and also works on A4.

What's the difference between pre-tax and post-tax deductions?

Pre-tax deductions (like 401k and health insurance) are subtracted from your gross pay before taxes are calculated, reducing your taxable income. Post-tax deductions (like Roth 401k or some life insurance) come out after taxes. Pre-tax deductions save you money on taxes now but are taxed when you withdraw them later (in the case of retirement contributions).

How do year-to-date totals work?

Year-to-date (YTD) totals show the accumulated amounts since January 1st. This paystub maker estimates YTD by multiplying current-period amounts by the pay period number you enter. Real payroll systems track exact YTD from actual historical records, so there can be differences if your pay or deductions varied across periods.

Why doesn't my state appear to have income tax?

Nine states don't levy a state income tax: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. If you live in one of these states, the state tax deduction on your pay stub will be $0. New Hampshire and Washington do have some taxes on investment income and capital gains respectively, but don't tax regular wages.
