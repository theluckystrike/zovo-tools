What is bionic reading?

Bionic reading is a way of formatting text that bolds the first few letters of each word and leaves the rest in a regular weight. Those bold leading letters act as fixation points - small visual anchors that draw your eye to the start of every word. The theory is that once your eye lands on the highlighted part, your brain recognizes the whole word from just the opening letters, so you spend less time scanning the middle and end of each word. The result is a page where every word has a built-in starting handle for your gaze.

This converter applies that formatting automatically. You paste in plain text, choose how strong you want the effect, and it wraps the leading portion of each word in bold tags while leaving spaces, punctuation, numbers, and line breaks completely untouched. The actual words never change - only which part of each word is bold.

Who bionic reading helps

People reach for bionic reading for a few different reasons. Readers with ADHD sometimes report that the fixation points help anchor their attention and reduce the urge to skip around the page. Some readers describe the format as dyslexia-friendly because the strong word boundaries make it easier to keep their place, though experiences here vary widely and it is not a substitute for tools designed specifically for dyslexia. People interested in speed reading use it to push through long articles with fewer stops, and students often apply it to dense notes and study material to maintain momentum while reviewing.

An honest note on the research: despite the popular marketing, independent studies have generally found little to no measurable improvement in average reading speed or comprehension from bionic-style formatting, and a few readers are slightly slowed by it. The benefit, when it exists, seems to be mostly about focus and reduced re-reading rather than raw speed. The sensible approach is to try it on your own material, judge it by how it actually feels for you, and keep it only if it genuinely helps.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is bionic reading?
Bionic reading is a text formatting method that bolds the first part of each word while leaving the rest in a normal weight. The idea is that your eyes fixate on the highlighted leading letters, called fixation points, and your brain fills in the rest of each word from memory. By giving the eye a clear visual anchor at the start of every word, the layout aims to guide you through a line of text more smoothly. This converter applies that formatting to any text you paste in.
Does bionic reading actually make you read faster?
The honest answer is that the evidence is mixed. Bionic reading became popular through marketing claims, but independent studies have generally found little or no measurable gain in reading speed or comprehension on average, and some readers are slightly slowed by the extra bold formatting. That said, results vary by person. Many people report that it helps them stay focused and avoid re-reading lines, which can feel faster even if a stopwatch disagrees. Treat it as a focus aid worth trying, not a guaranteed speed boost.
Who does bionic reading help most?
Anecdotally, the people who report the most benefit are those who struggle to keep their place in dense text, including some readers with ADHD who find the fixation points help anchor attention, and some readers who find plain blocks of text visually fatiguing. Students skimming notes and anyone reading long articles on screen may also find it useful for maintaining momentum. Because responses are so individual, the best approach is to try it on your own material and keep it only if it genuinely helps you.
What does the fixation slider do?
The fixation control sets how much of each word is bolded. At 40 percent only the first few letters of each word are emphasized, which is subtle and lighter on the page. At 60 percent a larger leading portion is bold, giving a stronger anchor but a busier look. The default of 50 percent bolds roughly the first half of each word, rounded up. Adjust it live and pick whatever feels most comfortable to read; there is no single correct setting.
