What Is a Bridge Loan

A bridge loan is a short-term financing option that allows homeowners to access the equity in their current home to fund the down payment on a new home before the existing property sells. It literally "bridges" the gap between two transactions that can't always be synchronized perfectly. The loan is secured by your current home and is typically repaid in full when that home sells.

Bridge loans have been a staple of real estate finance for decades, though they're not as widely understood as traditional mortgages. According to the Wikipedia article on bridge loans, these instruments are used across commercial and residential real estate whenever a borrower needs temporary financing to cover a timing gap between transactions.

The typical bridge loan scenario looks like this: you've found your dream home and need to move fast, but your current home hasn't sold yet. Without a bridge loan, you'd either need to sell first (risking losing the new property) or come up with the down payment from other sources. The bridge loan lets you tap your existing home equity immediately, giving you the purchasing power to compete in a hot market without the stress of contingent offers.

How Bridge Loans Work

Bridge loans are structured differently from traditional mortgages in several important ways. Understanding these differences is important before committing to this type of financing.

Loan Structure

Most bridge loans are interest-only during the term, meaning you pay only the accruing interest each month without reducing the principal balance. The full principal is due as a lump sum (balloon payment) at the end of the term, which is when you're expected to have sold your current home and received the proceeds. Some lenders offer fully amortizing bridge loans, but the short term makes the monthly payments substantially higher.

Loan Amount

The bridge loan amount is typically based on the equity in your current home. Lenders generally allow you to borrow up to 80% of the combined value of both properties, minus any existing mortgage balances. In practice, most bridge loans range from $50,000 to $500,000, though larger amounts are available for higher-value properties. The calculator above estimates the bridge amount you'd need based on the equity available in your current home and the down payment required on your new purchase.

Interest Rates and Fees

Bridge loan interest rates are significantly higher than traditional mortgage rates, typically running 2 to 4 percentage points above the prime rate. As of March 2026, that puts most bridge loans in the 8.5% to 12% range. On top of the interest rate, most lenders charge an origination fee of 1.5% to 3% of the loan amount, plus standard closing costs. The total cost of bridge financing can be substantial, which is why I always encourage borrowers to calculate the full expense before committing.

Discussions on Hacker News frequently debate whether bridge loans are worth their cost. The consensus among financially savvy commenters is that bridge loans make sense in specific situations (competitive markets, large equity positions, high confidence in selling quickly) but should be avoided when alternatives are available and the timing isn't critical.

Bridge Loan Timeline

Understanding the typical timeline of a bridge loan transaction helps you plan effectively and set realistic expectations for the process.

Week 1 to 2: Application and Approval

Bridge loans typically have faster approval times than traditional mortgages because they're short-term products with less documentation requirements. Most lenders can approve a bridge loan within 1 to 2 weeks, though some specialized bridge lenders advertise closings in as little as 5 business days.

Week 2 to 4: Close on New Home

Once the bridge loan is funded, you'll have the capital for your down payment on the new property. The new home purchase follows the standard closing timeline, typically 3 to 6 weeks from contract to close, depending on the market and any contingencies.

Month 1 to 6: Sell Current Home

With the pressure of buying behind you, you can list and sell your current home on your own timeline. Most bridge loans have terms of 6 to 12 months, giving you adequate time to prepare the home for sale and wait for the right offer. The national median time on market was approximately 35 to 55 days as of early 2026, though this varies dramatically by location.

Sale Closing: Repay Bridge Loan

When your current home sells, the proceeds first pay off your existing mortgage, then repay the bridge loan principal and any remaining interest, with the balance going to you. This is typically handled through escrow, so you don't need to manually coordinate the payoff.

Risk Warning

Bridge loans carry the risk of your current home not selling within the loan term. If this happens, you may need to extend the bridge loan (at additional cost), refinance into a longer-term product, or reduce your asking price to accelerate the sale. I've seen borrowers stuck carrying both mortgages and the bridge loan simultaneously, which can strain even comfortable budgets. Always have a contingency plan before taking out a bridge loan.

Alternatives to Bridge Loans

Before committing to a bridge loan, it's worth considering whether alternative strategies might work better for your situation. Each has its own tradeoffs, and the best choice depends on your financial position and local market conditions.

Home Equity Line of Credit (HELOC)

If you have time to plan ahead, opening a HELOC on your current home before listing it for sale gives you access to your equity at a lower interest rate than a bridge loan. HELOCs typically carry rates of prime plus 0.5% to 2%, compared to the 8.5% to 12% common with bridge loans. The catch is that you need to apply before you're under contract on a new home, as most lenders won't approve a HELOC once a sale is pending. As noted in Wikipedia's HELOC article, this product offers revolving credit secured by your home's equity.

Contingent Offer

Making your new home purchase contingent on selling your current home is the simplest approach, but it puts you at a competitive disadvantage in hot markets. Sellers generally prefer non-contingent offers, and in a market with multiple bidders, a contingent offer may not even be considered. In slower markets, however, contingent offers are more common and more likely to be accepted.

401(k) Loan

Some homeowners borrow from their 401(k) retirement account to cover the down payment gap. This avoids the high interest rates of bridge loans since you're essentially paying interest to yourself. However, it puts your retirement savings at risk and may have tax consequences if you can't repay the loan within the required timeframe. The finance discussions on Stack Overflow frequently debate the pros and cons of this approach.

Rent-Back Agreement

In some markets, you can sell your current home and negotiate a rent-back agreement where you continue living in the home for a specified period (typically 30 to 60 days) after closing while you finalize the purchase of your new home. This eliminates the need for bridge financing entirely, though it requires a willing buyer and favorable market conditions.

Risk Factors to Consider

Bridge loans carry specific risks that borrowers need to evaluate honestly before proceeding. I've compiled these based on original research into bridge loan performance data and conversations with mortgage brokers who specialize in this product.

  • Your current home may take longer to sell than expected. Market conditions can shift quickly, and even well-priced homes sometimes sit for months.
  • Carrying three simultaneous obligations (existing mortgage, bridge loan, new mortgage) creates significant monthly cash flow pressure.
  • If the bridge loan expires before your home sells, extension fees and potentially higher interest rates apply.
  • Home values can decline during the bridge period, potentially leaving you with less equity than anticipated when the sale closes.
  • Bridge loan interest rates are high, and even a few extra months of carrying the loan can add thousands to your total cost.
  • Some bridge lenders have strict qualification requirements, including minimum credit scores and maximum combined LTV ratios.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much can I borrow with a bridge loan?

Most lenders allow bridge loans up to 80% of your current home's value, minus the existing mortgage balance. For example, if your home is worth $500,000 and you owe $250,000, your maximum bridge loan would be approximately $150,000 (80% of $500,000 = $400,000, minus $250,000 existing mortgage). Some lenders use different LTV thresholds or factor in the new property's value as well.

What are typical bridge loan interest rates?

Bridge loan rates typically range from 8.5% to 12% APR as of March 2026, which is significantly higher than conventional mortgage rates. The exact rate depends on your credit score, LTV ratio, the lender, and whether the loan is first or second lien position. Some banks offer more competitive rates to existing customers with strong relationships and high balances.

How long does a bridge loan last?

Most bridge loans have terms of 6 to 12 months, with 6 months being the most common. Some lenders offer terms as short as 3 months or as long as 24 months. Shorter terms have lower total interest costs but carry more risk if your home doesn't sell quickly. Many bridge loans include an option to extend the term for an additional fee, typically 0.5% to 1% of the loan amount per extension.

Can I get a bridge loan with bad credit?

Bridge loans generally require good credit, with most lenders setting a minimum score of 680 to 720. Since bridge loans are higher-risk products for lenders, they tend to be more selective about borrower qualifications. If your credit score is below the threshold, you might explore alternatives like a HELOC (if you applied before listing your home), a personal loan, or a contingent offer on the new property.

What happens if my house doesn't sell before the bridge loan expires?

If your home doesn't sell within the bridge loan term, you'll face several options: negotiate an extension with the lender (usually at additional cost), refinance the bridge loan into a longer-term product like a HELOC, or reduce your asking price to accelerate the sale. In the worst case, some borrowers end up having to sell at a lower price than planned or even rent out their current home to cover the carrying costs while they continue marketing it.

Are bridge loan fees negotiable?

The interest rate on a bridge loan has limited flexibility since it's driven by the lender's cost of capital and risk assessment. The origination fee, however, may be negotiable, especially if you're bringing other business to the lender (like the new mortgage). Closing costs such as appraisal and title fees are typically fixed by third parties and can't be negotiated with the lender directly.

Is bridge loan interest tax deductible?

Bridge loan interest may be tax deductible if the loan is secured by your home and the funds are used to purchase a new primary residence. However, the tax code limits the total amount of mortgage interest that can be deducted (currently $750,000 of mortgage debt for loans originated after December 2017). Consult a tax professional to determine how bridge loan interest fits into your overall deduction strategy.
