How CD Interest Works

A Certificate of Deposit is a time-bound savings product offered by banks and credit unions. You deposit a fixed amount of money for a predetermined period (the term), and the bank pays you interest at a guaranteed rate. When the term ends (the CD matures), you receive your original deposit plus all earned interest.

The interest calculation uses the compound interest formula:

A = P (1 + r/n)^(n * t) A = final amount, P = principal, r = annual rate, n = compounding periods per year, t = years

For example, depositing $10,000 in a 12-month CD at 5.00% APY compounded monthly:

1. Monthly rate: 0.04889 / 12 = 0.004074 (using APR equivalent of 5.00% APY)
2. Compounding periods: 12 months x 1 year = 12
3. Final amount: $10,000 x (1 + 0.004074)^12 = $10,500.00
4. Interest earned: $10,500.00 - $10,000 = $500.00

The key advantage of CDs over regular savings accounts is the guaranteed rate. Once you open a CD, the interest rate is locked in for the entire term regardless of what happens to market rates. If rates drop the next month, your CD keeps earning at the original rate.

APY vs APR Explained

These two acronyms cause more confusion than any other terms in banking. Understanding the difference is critical when comparing CD offers from different banks.

APR (Annual Percentage Rate)

APR is the simple annual interest rate before compounding. It is the base rate that the bank uses to calculate your interest. If a bank says the rate is 4.89%, that is the APR. It does not account for the benefit of compounding, where interest earned in earlier periods earns additional interest in later periods.

APY (Annual Percentage Yield)

APY includes the effect of compounding over one year. It represents what you actually earn on your deposit. An APR of 4.89% compounded daily produces an APY of approximately 5.01%. The more frequently interest compounds, the wider the gap between APR and APY.

Conversion Formulas

APR to APY: APY = (1 + APR/n)^n - 1

APY to APR: APR = n x ((1 + APY)^(1/n) - 1)

where n = number of compounding periods per year

Compounding Frequency

How often your CD compounds interest affects the final amount you earn. More frequent compounding means you earn interest on your interest sooner, which results in a slightly higher return.

The difference between annual and daily compounding on a $10,000 deposit at 5% is $12.67 per year. That may seem insignificant, but on a $100,000 deposit over 5 years, the difference grows to over $630. For larger deposits and longer terms, daily compounding adds up meaningfully.

Most banks and credit unions compound CD interest either daily or monthly. Very few still use quarterly or annual compounding. When comparing CD offers, the APY already accounts for the compounding frequency, so comparing APY values directly gives you an precise comparison regardless of how often each bank compounds.

Types of CDs

The traditional fixed-rate CD is the most common, but banks have created several variations to meet different needs. Here is what is available.

Traditional (Fixed-Rate) CD

The standard CD with a fixed interest rate for a set term. You deposit money, earn a guaranteed rate, and get your principal plus interest at maturity. Early withdrawal triggers a penalty. Terms range from 1 month to 10 years. This is the right choice when you have a specific sum you will not need until a known date and you want a guaranteed return.

High-Yield CD

High-yield CDs offer above-average rates, typically from online banks that have lower overhead costs than traditional brick-and-mortar banks. The trade-off is that you cannot walk into a branch for in-person service. High-yield CDs work the same way as traditional CDs but with rates that are often 0.50% to 1.50% higher.

Jumbo CD

Jumbo CDs require a higher minimum deposit, usually $100,000 or more. In return, they sometimes offer a slightly higher rate than standard CDs at the same bank, though the rate premium has shrunk in recent years. Many banks now offer their best rates on standard CDs with minimums as low as $500 or $1,000, making jumbo CDs less advantageous than they once were.

No-Penalty CD

No-penalty CDs let you withdraw your full balance before maturity without paying an early withdrawal penalty. This flexibility comes at a cost: the APY is typically 0.25% to 0.75% lower than a comparable traditional CD. No-penalty CDs are a good choice when you want a better rate than a savings account but are not certain you can lock the money away for the full term.

Bump-Up (Raise-Your-Rate) CD

Bump-up CDs give you the option to increase your rate once (sometimes twice) during the term if the bank raises its rates. This protects you from locking in a rate and then watching rates rise. The catch is that the starting rate is lower than a comparable traditional CD. Bump-up CDs are most useful in a rising rate environment, but predicting rate direction is difficult.

Step-Up CD

Step-up CDs have a rate that automatically increases at predetermined intervals during the term. For example, a 2-year step-up CD might pay 4.00% for the first 6 months, 4.50% for the next 6 months, 5.00% for the third 6 months, and 5.50% for the final 6 months. The blended effective rate over the full term may be competitive with or lower than a traditional CD, so compare the overall APY carefully.

Brokered CD

Brokered CDs are sold through brokerage firms rather than directly from banks. They can be bought and sold on the secondary market before maturity, providing liquidity that traditional CDs lack. However, selling before maturity means the price depends on current interest rates. If rates have risen, you sell at a loss. If rates have fallen, you can sell at a premium. Brokered CDs are more like bonds in this regard.

IRA CD

IRA CDs are held within an Individual Retirement Account (traditional or Roth IRA). The interest grows tax-deferred (traditional) or tax-free (Roth). The CD terms and rates are similar to regular CDs. The main difference is the tax treatment and the fact that withdrawals before age 59-1/2 may trigger IRA early withdrawal penalties to any CD penalties.

CD Ladder Strategy

A CD ladder is one of the most effective strategies for balancing higher returns with regular access to your money. Instead of putting all your funds into a single CD, you spread them across CDs with staggered maturity dates.

How a CD Ladder Works

Take $50,000 and divide it into five equal parts of $10,000 each:

  • $10,000 in a 1-year CD
  • $10,000 in a 2-year CD
  • $10,000 in a 3-year CD
  • $10,000 in a 4-year CD
  • $10,000 in a 5-year CD

When the 1-year CD matures after 12 months, you reinvest it into a new 5-year CD (typically the highest-rate option). When the 2-year CD matures the following year, you do the same. After 5 years, you have five 5-year CDs, all earning the highest long-term rate, but with one maturing every single year.

Benefits of a CD Ladder

The ladder gives you annual access to a portion of your money without paying early withdrawal penalties. It captures higher long-term rates while maintaining regular liquidity. It also reduces the risk of locking all your money at a single rate that might turn out to be below market. If rates rise, each maturing CD can be reinvested at the new, higher rate.

Mini-Ladders

For shorter horizons or more frequent access, you can build a mini-ladder with 3-month, 6-month, 9-month, and 12-month CDs. When each matures, reinvest into a new 12-month CD. After one year, you have four 12-month CDs with one maturing every quarter. This works well for emergency fund portions where you want better rates than a savings account but need quarterly access.

FDIC Insurance

CDs at FDIC-insured banks are protected by the Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation. This government-backed insurance means that even if the bank fails, your deposits are safe up to the coverage limit.

A married couple can have up to $1,000,000 in FDIC coverage at a single bank: $250,000 in each individual account plus $500,000 in a joint account. Adding beneficiaries through a revocable trust can increase coverage even further.

If you have more than $250,000 to invest in CDs, spread the deposits across multiple FDIC-insured banks. Services like CDARS (Certificate of Deposit Account Registry Service) and IntraFi Network Deposits automate this by splitting large deposits across multiple banks through a single account, ensuring full FDIC coverage on amounts well above $250,000.

Important: FDIC insurance covers principal and accrued interest up to the limit. If you have $248,000 in a CD that has accrued $5,000 in interest, your total insured amount is $250,000, meaning $3,000 would be uninsured. Factor in accrued interest when calculating coverage.

Early Withdrawal Penalties

Life does not always go according to plan, and sometimes you access CD funds before maturity. Banks charge early withdrawal penalties (EWPs) to discourage this, and the penalties vary widely by institution and term length.

When Early Withdrawal Makes Sense

Sometimes paying the penalty is the right financial decision. If you are locked into a 3.00% CD and rates have jumped to 5.50%, breaking the old CD, paying the penalty, and opening a new one at 5.50% can come out ahead over the remaining term. Run the numbers with the early withdrawal calculator above before deciding.

CD Rate Trends

CD rates are influenced primarily by the federal funds rate set by the Federal Reserve. When the Fed raises rates, CD rates tend to follow within weeks. When the Fed cuts rates, CD rates decline, though the lag can be longer on the way down.

Historical Context

From 2009 to 2021, CD rates were historically low. A 1-year CD rarely paid above 1.00% APY during that period, with many offering 0.10% to 0.50%. Starting in early 2022, the Federal Reserve began raising the federal funds rate aggressively to combat inflation, and CD rates rose in response. By mid-2023, 1-year CD rates reached 5.00% to 5.50% at competitive banks, levels not seen since before the 2008 financial crisis.

As of early 2026, CD rates remain attractive by historical standards. High-yield 1-year CDs are generally available in the 4.00% to 5.00% APY range, though rates have been drifting down from their 2023-2024 peaks as the Fed has begun easing monetary policy. Longer-term CDs (3 to 5 years) may offer slightly lower rates than shorter-term CDs in the current environment, reflecting market expectations for future rate cuts.

Rate Environment Strategy

  • Rising rates: Favor shorter-term CDs (3 to 12 months) so you can reinvest at higher rates as they mature. Avoid long-term CDs that lock you in below future rates.
  • Falling rates: Lock in longer-term CDs (3 to 5 years) to preserve today's higher rates before they decline. This is when long-term CDs shine.
  • Uncertain rates: Build a CD ladder to hedge your bets. You capture some long-term rates while maintaining flexibility to reinvest at short-term maturities.

Tax Implications

CD interest is taxable as ordinary income at your federal and state tax rates. The IRS treats CD interest the same as wages or salary income for tax purposes. There are no special capital gains rates or tax advantages for CD interest (unless the CD is held in a tax-advantaged account like an IRA).

When Is CD Interest Taxed?

You owe federal income tax on CD interest in the year it is credited to your account, even if you do not withdraw it. For a multi-year CD, you pay tax on the interest accrued each year, not just when the CD matures. Your bank will issue a 1099-INT form each January showing the interest earned in the previous year.

Tax Impact Example

If you earn $500 in CD interest and you are in the 24% federal tax bracket plus a 5% state tax bracket, you owe approximately $145 in combined taxes on that interest ($120 federal + $25 state). Your effective after-tax return on a 5.00% APY CD in this scenario is about 3.55% APY.

For savers in higher tax brackets, consider I Bonds (tax-deferred until redemption and state tax exempt) or municipal bonds (federally tax exempt) as alternatives. Treasury bills are also worth considering since they are exempt from state and local income tax, effectively boosting your after-tax return in high-tax states.

When to Open a CD

Timing your CD purchase can make a meaningful difference in your returns. While no one can perfectly predict interest rate movements, understanding the factors that drive CD rates helps you make better decisions.

Good Times to Open a CD

  • When the Federal Reserve signals rate cuts are coming. Lock in current high rates before they decline.
  • When you receive a lump sum (bonus, inheritance, home sale proceeds) that you will not need for a specific period.
  • When CD rates are significantly higher than savings account rates, making the liquidity sacrifice worthwhile.
  • When you remove the temptation to spend money. The early withdrawal penalty acts as a behavioral nudge to leave the money invested.
  • When you have a specific financial goal with a known date (down payment in 18 months, tuition payment in 2 years).

Times to Reconsider

  • When rates are actively rising and short-term CDs pay more than long-term ones (inverted yield curve). In this environment, short-term CDs or high-yield savings may be better.
  • When you might need the money. If there is a reasonable chance you need the funds before maturity, a high-yield savings account avoids the penalty risk.
  • When after-tax returns on CDs are lower than inflation. If inflation is 4% and your after-tax CD return is 3.5%, your purchasing power is declining.

Frequently Asked Questions

How is CD interest calculated?

CD interest uses compound interest: A = P(1 + r/n)^(nt). P is your deposit, r is the annual rate (APR), n is compounding periods per year, and t is time in years. Most banks compound daily or monthly. The advertised APY already accounts for compounding.

What is the difference between APY and APR?

APR is the base annual rate without compounding. APY includes compounding and shows what you actually earn. A 4.89% APR compounded daily equals about 5.01% APY. Always compare CDs by APY for an precise comparison.

Are CDs worth it right now?

With APYs in the 4-5% range as of early 2026, CDs are offering rates well above the historical average. Whether they are worth it depends on your time horizon and alternatives. If you can lock money away for 6 to 24 months and the rate beats your savings account by a meaningful margin, CDs are a solid choice for that portion of your savings.

Can I lose money on a CD?

Not in the traditional sense if you hold to maturity. Your principal is guaranteed by the bank and insured by the FDIC up to $250,000. However, if you withdraw very early and the penalty exceeds the interest earned, you will get back less than you deposited. Also, if your after-tax return is below inflation, you lose purchasing power.

How much does a $10,000 CD earn in a year?

At 5.00% APY, a $10,000 CD earns $500 in one year. At 4.50%, it earns $450. At 4.00%, it earns $400. The exact amount depends on the APY and compounding frequency. Use the calculator above for precise results with your specific terms.

What is the best CD term to choose?

The best term depends on when you need the money and your rate outlook. If you believe rates will fall, lock in a longer term (2-5 years) at today's rate. If rates might rise, stay short (3-12 months) so you can reinvest at higher rates. A CD ladder eliminates the guessing by spreading across multiple terms.

Do I pay taxes on CD interest?

Yes, CD interest is taxable as ordinary income at your federal and state tax rates. You owe tax on interest in the year it accrues, even on multi-year CDs where you have not withdrawn anything. Your bank sends a 1099-INT each year. CDs held in IRAs grow tax-deferred or tax-free depending on the IRA type.

What is a CD ladder?

A CD ladder divides your total investment across CDs with staggered maturity dates (for example, 1-year, 2-year, 3-year, 4-year, and 5-year). When each CD matures, you reinvest into the longest term. This gives you annual access to a portion of your money while earning higher long-term rates on the rest.

How to Choose a Bank for CDs

Not all banks offer the same CD rates, and the difference between the best and worst rates at any given time can be 1% to 2% APY. That gap translates to hundreds or thousands of dollars in lost interest on a significant deposit. Here is what to look for when choosing where to open your CD.

Online Banks vs Traditional Banks

Online banks consistently offer higher CD rates than brick-and-mortar banks. The reason is straightforward: online banks have dramatically lower overhead (no branch buildings, fewer employees per customer, lower real estate costs) and pass those savings to depositors in the form of higher rates. As of early 2026, a top online bank might offer 5.00% APY on a 12-month CD while a large traditional bank offers 3.50% to 4.00% for the same term. On a $25,000 deposit for one year, that is a difference of $250 to $375 in your pocket.

The trade-off is that online banks lack in-person service. You manage your account entirely through a website and mobile app, and customer service is by phone, chat, or email. For a CD, which you typically set and forget until maturity, this is rarely a problem. You are not making frequent transactions or needing in-person help.

Credit Unions

Credit unions are member-owned financial cooperatives that often offer competitive CD rates (they call them "share certificates"). Because credit unions are not-for-profit, they can return more value to members through higher savings rates and lower loan rates. Credit union CDs are insured by the NCUA (National Credit Union Administration) up to $250,000, the same coverage level as FDIC insurance. Many credit unions have membership requirements (living in a certain area, working for a certain employer, or simply making a small donation to a qualifying organization), but these are usually easy to meet.

What to Check Before Opening

  • FDIC or NCUA membership: Verify the institution is insured. You can check at fdic.gov/bankfind or mycreditunion.gov.
  • APY, not APR: Compare the APY, which includes compounding. This is the true return.
  • Minimum deposit: Some CDs require $500, $1,000, or $10,000 minimum deposits. Others have no minimum.
  • Early withdrawal penalty: Compare penalty terms. They vary widely and can make a significant difference if you break the CD.
  • Auto-renewal policy: Most banks auto-renew CDs at maturity. Know the grace period (usually 7 to 14 days) to withdraw or change terms.
  • How interest is paid: Most CDs compound interest and add it to the principal. Some pay interest monthly to an external account, which reduces compounding but provides income.

CD Strategies for Different Goals

How you use CDs depends on your financial situation and goals. Here are specific strategies tailored to common scenarios.

Saving for a Down Payment

If you are saving for a home purchase in 12 to 24 months, a CD locks in your rate and removes the temptation to dip into the funds. Choose a term that matches your expected purchase timeline. If you plan to buy in 18 months, an 18-month CD is . The guaranteed return provides certainty for your budget, and you know exactly how much you will have when the CD matures.

Emergency Fund Strategy

Financial advisors recommend keeping 3 to 6 months of expenses in an emergency fund. Rather than parking the entire amount in a savings account earning a lower variable rate, consider keeping 1 to 2 months of expenses in a high-yield savings account for immediate access and placing the rest in a short-term CD ladder (3-month, 6-month, 9-month, 12-month CDs). This earns a higher fixed rate on the majority while ensuring you have some funds maturing every quarter.

Retirement Income

Retirees who need predictable income can use a CD ladder to generate regular cash flow. A 5-rung monthly ladder (5 CDs maturing one month apart) provides monthly income while keeping the majority of funds earning interest. For example, $120,000 split into 12 CDs maturing one per month gives you $10,000 plus interest available every month, with each maturing CD rolling into a new 12-month term.

Parking Large Windfalls

If you receive a large sum (inheritance, business sale, bonus) and need time to decide on long-term investments, a short-term CD (3 to 6 months) parks the money safely while you plan. This earns more than a checking account and gives you a defined decision deadline when the CD matures. Many people who receive windfalls feel pressure to invest immediately; a CD gives you breathing room without leaving money idle.
