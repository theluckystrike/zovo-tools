Understanding AWG Wire Gauges

What Is AWG (American Wire Gauge)?

American Wire Gauge (AWG), also known as the Brown & Sharpe wire gauge, is a logarithmic stepped standardized wire gauge system used since 1857 predominantly in North America for the diameters of round, solid, nonferrous, electrically conducting wire. Dimensions of the wires are given in ASTM standard B 258. - Wikipedia, "American wire gauge," accessed March 2026.

I've been working with AWG wire gauges for years, and one thing that always trips people up is that the gauge number goes down as the wire gets thicker. It's counter at first, but it makes sense once you understand the history. The gauge number originally referred to the number of drawing dies the wire was pulled through - more draws meant thinner wire.

The AWG system follows a precise geometric progression. The ratio of diameters between successive gauges is constant: each 6-gauge increase halves the diameter, and each 3-gauge increase halves the cross-sectional area. This calculator uses the exact d(n) = 0.127 × 92(36-n)/39 mm, which is what you'll find in the ASTM B 258 standard.

Don't confuse AWG with SWG (Standard Wire Gauge) used in the UK, or metric wire sizes. They're completely different systems. If you're doing electrical work in North America, AWG is what you need. For most residential wiring, you'll use 14 AWG (15A circuits) or 12 AWG (20A circuits) most often. I've included a reference table below with all the common gauges so you can quickly look things up.
