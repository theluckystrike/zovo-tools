The Hall Tax What Happened

You might come across references to the "Hall Tax" when researching Tennessee taxes. Here is the quick history. The Hall Income Tax was a 1929 law that taxed interest and dividend income at a flat rate. At its peak, the rate was 6%. Starting in 2016, Tennessee began a phased elimination of the Hall Tax, reducing the rate by 1% per year. The tax was fully repealed effective January 1, 2021.

This means that since 2021, Tennessee has had absolutely zero state income tax on any type of income, whether wages, interest, dividends, or capital gains. This is why I've seen Tennessee ranked as one of the top five most tax-friendly states for retirees and investors, not just workers. The Wikipedia article on the Hall Income Tax provides a thorough legislative history if you want the full story.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Tennessee have any income tax at all?

No. As of January 1, 2021, Tennessee has zero state income tax on any type of income. The old Hall Tax on interest and dividends was fully repealed. Wages, salaries, tips, commissions, bonuses, capital gains, interest, and dividends are all free from state income tax in Tennessee.

What taxes do I still pay in Tennessee?

You pay federal income tax (10% to 37%), Social Security (6.2% up to $176,100), and Medicare (1.45% plus 0.9% surtax over $200k). Tennessee also has a 7% state sales tax (one of the highest in the country), plus local sales taxes that can bring the combined rate to 9.75%. Property taxes vary by county. But there is no payroll withholding for state income tax.

Is Tennessee better than Texas or Florida for taxes?

Tennessee, Texas, and Florida all have zero state income tax on wages, so the income tax burden is identical. The differences come down to other taxes. Tennessee has higher sales tax (9.75% combined max) but lower property taxes than Texas. Florida has no income tax and moderate sales tax (6-7.5%), but higher property insurance costs. For pure income tax purposes, all three are equivalent.

What happened to the Hall Tax?

The Hall Income Tax was enacted in 1929 and taxed interest and dividend income (not wages) at up to 6%. Starting in 2016, the Tennessee legislature began a phased repeal, reducing the rate by 1% per year. The tax rate hit 0% on January 1, 2021, making Tennessee completely income-tax-free for all types of income.

How accurate is this calculator?

This calculator typically comes within $5-10 per paycheck of your actual amount. Because Tennessee has no state tax, the only variables are federal brackets and FICA, which are straightforward to calculate. Minor differences can arise from employer-specific deductions, rounding methods, and withholding allowance calculations.

Do I file a state tax return in Tennessee?

No. Since Tennessee has no income tax, there is no state income tax return to file. You still file your federal return with the IRS. This is another benefit of living in Tennessee: one less set of forms to deal with every April.

Understanding Your Tennessee Paycheck

Your Tennessee paycheck is simpler than what workers in income-tax states see. Here are the deduction lines you will typically find.

Federal Income Tax Withholding

Calculated from your W-4 form selections, this is your largest deduction. The amount varies based on your salary, filing status, and any additional withholding or credits you claim on the W-4. For a single filer earning $75,000, expect about $600 per month in federal withholding on a bi-weekly paycheck. The W-4 form was redesigned in 2020 to eliminate "allowances" and use a simpler system based on dependents, additional income, and deductions.

Social Security (OASDI)

A flat 6.2% of your gross pay up to $176,100. This shows up as "OASDI" or "SS" on your pay stub. Your employer matches this amount. Once you hit the wage base cap during the year, this deduction stops and your take-home pay increases for the remaining paychecks. If you earn exactly $176,100, you pay the maximum of $10,918 in Social Security tax for 2025.

Medicare

A flat 1.45% on all earnings with no cap. There is an Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% on earnings above $200,000 for single filers. Your employer matches the 1.45% base rate. Unlike Social Security, Medicare never stops being deducted regardless of how much you earn.

No State Income Tax Line

This is the beauty of working in Tennessee. There is no state withholding line on your paycheck. Compared to workers in states like California, New York, or even moderate-tax states like Missouri, you keep that portion of every paycheck. For someone earning $75,000, that is roughly $200-300 per month that stays in your pocket instead of going to the state.

Relocating to Tennessee

Tennessee has become one of the top relocation destinations in the country, particularly for workers moving from high-tax states. Nashville has consistently ranked among the fastest-growing metro areas, and Memphis, Knoxville, and Chattanooga are also seeing population growth. Here are some practical considerations if you are thinking about the move.

If you work remotely for a company based in another state, the tax treatment depends on where you physically perform the work. Generally, you owe state income tax to the state where you perform the work, not where the company is headquartered. So if you move to Tennessee and work remotely, you typically won't owe state income tax to your employer's state (with some exceptions like New York's "convenience of the employer" rule). Always verify this with a tax professional, as reciprocity agreements and convenience rules vary by state.

Tennessee does not have an estate tax or inheritance tax, which is another financial advantage for long-term planning. The state also has relatively low vehicle registration fees and no personal property tax on automobiles (unlike states like Virginia or Connecticut that tax car ownership annually).

For retirees, Tennessee is particularly attractive. Social Security benefits are not taxed at the state level (because nothing is taxed), and pension income, 401(k) withdrawals, and IRA distributions are all free from state income tax. This makes Tennessee one of the most tax-friendly states for retirement income.

Nashville has been one of the fastest-growing cities in the country for the past decade. Memphis has a vibrant food and music scene and some of the most affordable housing of any major metro in the country. Chattanooga

Chattanooga has reinvented itself as a tech-friendly city, largely thanks to its municipally-owned fiber-optic internet service (EPB Fiber). This has attracted tech startups and remote workers. The outdoor recreation options along the Tennessee River and nearby mountains have earned it the nickname "Scenic City." The cost of living is moderate, with median home prices around $310,000. Volkswagen's North American manufacturing headquarters is based here, along with a growing insurance technology sector.

Self-Employment in Tennessee

Self-employed workers in Tennessee benefit from the same zero-income-tax advantage as W-2 employees. You still pay federal self-employment tax (15.3% on net earnings) and federal income tax, but you don't make any state estimated tax payments. This simplifies your quarterly filing significantly compared to self-employed workers in income-tax states who must file and pay estimates to both the IRS and their state revenue department.

Tennessee does have a business tax (a gross receipts tax) that applies to businesses with substantial receipts, and there is a franchise and excise tax that applies to certain business entities. Sole proprietors and single-member LLCs with modest revenue are generally not subject to these. If you are running a business in Tennessee, consult the Tennessee Department of Revenue or a local CPA for guidance on these taxes.

For gig workers and freelancers, the practical benefit is even clearer when you look at quarterly filing obligations. In a state like California or New York, a freelancer needs to make estimated quarterly payments to both the IRS and the state revenue department. In Tennessee, you only deal with the IRS. That means fewer forms, fewer deadlines, and fewer chances to incur an underpayment penalty. I've talked to freelancers who moved from New York to Nashville specifically because of how much simpler their tax compliance became. The time savings alone is worth something, on top of the actual dollar savings from zero state income tax.

Tennessee also doesn't require business licenses for most online-only businesses operating from home, though local municipalities may have their own requirements. The state's overall business-friendly regulatory environment, combined with no income tax, has made cities like Nashville and Chattanooga increasingly popular for startups and small businesses. The Kauffman Foundation has ranked Tennessee among the top states for entrepreneurial activity in recent years.

Tennessee Sales Tax and Its Impact on Your Budget

Tennessee compensates for its zero income tax with one of the highest sales tax rates in the nation. The state sales tax rate is 7%, and local jurisdictions can add up to 2.75%, bringing the combined rate to as high as 9.75% in many areas. Nashville, Memphis, Knoxville, and Chattanooga all sit at or near the maximum combined rate. For a household spending $45,000 per year on taxable goods and services, that translates to roughly $4,390 in annual sales tax. Groceries are taxed at a reduced rate of 4% (state portion only), which provides some relief for families. Tennessee generated approximately $13.5 billion in sales and use tax revenue in fiscal year 2024, making it the largest single source of state government funding. When you compare Tennessee to a state like Oregon, which has no sales tax but levies a 9.9% top income tax rate, the trade-off becomes clear: Tennessee shifts the tax burden from earning to spending, which generally benefits higher-income households that save a larger share of their income.

For workers relocating from states with both income and sales taxes, such as Minnesota (6.875% sales plus 9.85% top income) or New Jersey (6.625% sales plus 10.75% top income), Tennessee almost always results in a lower total tax burden. However, workers moving from states with no sales tax and moderate income taxes, like Oregon or Montana, should calculate the trade-off carefully. The break-even point depends on your spending habits, savings rate, and income level. As a general rule, if you save or invest more than 25% of your gross income, Tennessee's consumption-based tax model works strongly in your favor compared to income-tax states.

Tennessee Property Tax Rates by County

Tennessee's property tax system is another advantage for residents. The state has one of the lowest effective property tax rates in the country, with a statewide median of approximately 0.56%. However, rates vary significantly by county and municipality. Davidson County (Nashville) has an effective rate of about 0.75% due to city services and infrastructure spending, while Shelby County (Memphis) averages around 1.10%, which is notably higher but still below the national median of 1.02%. Suburban counties surrounding Nashville, including Williamson County (0.48%), Rutherford County (0.62%), and Wilson County (0.52%), offer some of the lowest property tax rates in the state while providing excellent school systems and growing job opportunities. For a home valued at $350,000, the annual property tax ranges from approximately $1,680 in Williamson County to $3,850 in Shelby County, a difference of $2,170 per year.

Tennessee uses an assessment ratio system where residential property is assessed at 25% of its appraised value, and the county tax rate is applied to that assessed value. This means a home appraised at $400,000 has an assessed value of $100,000, and a county rate of $2.00 per $100 of assessed value results in a $2,000 annual tax bill. Understanding this system is important when comparing Tennessee property taxes to other states, because the headline tax rates in Tennessee appear high but the 25% assessment ratio effectively reduces the burden by 75%. Combined with zero income tax and no estate or inheritance tax, Tennessee's overall tax structure is particularly favorable for homeowners building long-term wealth. The state conducted a property reappraisal cycle in 2024 for many counties, which updated values to reflect current market conditions. If your home value increased significantly, your property tax may have risen even if the rate stayed flat.

For workers comparing Tennessee counties, rural counties often have even lower effective property tax rates, sometimes below 0.40%. Hamilton County (Chattanooga) sits at approximately 0.58%, and Knox County (Knoxville) averages about 0.62%. When you combine these low property tax rates with zero income tax, the total annual tax burden for a homeowner earning $75,000 with a $300,000 home in a suburban Tennessee county can be as low as $9,200, which includes federal income tax, FICA, property tax, and estimated sales tax. That same household in a state like New Jersey would face a total burden exceeding $18,000 when you factor in state income tax (6.37% marginal), property tax (2.23% effective), and sales tax (6.625%). The difference of nearly $9,000 per year is the kind of long-term advantage that compounds into significant wealth over a career.
