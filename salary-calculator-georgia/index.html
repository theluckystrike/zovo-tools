Georgia-Specific Deductions & Credits

Georgia offers several state-specific deductions and credits that can reduce your tax burden. I've compiled the most impactful ones based on our research:

  • Georgia Standard Deduction - $5,400 (single), $7,100 (married joint / HOH). This is separate from the federal standard deduction.
  • Personal Exemptions - $2,700 per taxpayer, $3,700 per dependent. Georgia still uses personal exemptions even though the federal exemption was eliminated by the TCJA.
  • Retirement Income Exclusion - Taxpayers 62+ can exclude up to $35,000 ($65,000 if 65+) of retirement income from Georgia taxable income.
  • Georgia 529 Plan Deduction - Contributions to Georgia's Path2College 529 plan are deductible up to $8,000 per beneficiary per year ($4,000 if married filing separately).
  • Low-Income Credit - Georgia provides a modest tax credit for low-income filers, calculated based on federal AGI thresholds.
  • Military Income Exclusion - Active duty military pay earned outside Georgia is exempt from Georgia income tax.

How Georgia State Income Tax Actually Works

Georgia taxes income using a graduated system with 6 brackets. Unlike the federal system which has wide brackets spanning tens of thousands of dollars, Georgia's brackets are compressed into the first $7,000 of taxable income. This means virtually every working Georgian pays the top 5.49% rate on most of their income. Here's the math for a single filer earning $75,000:

  1. Start with gross income: $75,000
  2. Subtract Georgia standard deduction: $75,000 - $5,400 = $69,600
  3. Subtract personal exemption: $69,600 - $2,700 = $66,900 (Georgia taxable income)
  4. Apply brackets:
    • 1% on first $750 = $7.50
    • 2% on next $1,500 = $30.00
    • 3% on next $1,500 = $45.00
    • 4% on next $1,500 = $60.00
    • 5% on next $1,750 = $87.50
    • 5.49% on remaining $59,900 = $3,288.51
  5. $3,518.51 (effective rate: ~4.69%)

This graduated structure means the first $7,000 of taxable income is taxed at blended rates below 5.49%, creating a small but meaningful tax benefit. For lower-income earners, this progressive structure provides more significant relief. Someone earning $30,000 pays an effective Georgia rate of only about 3.6%.

Georgia's Tax Reform Journey From Graduated to Flat

In 2022, Governor Brian Kemp signed HB 1015, beginning Georgia's transition from a graduated income tax to a flat tax. The original top rate of 5.75% has been gradually decreasing. For 2025, the effective top rate is 5.49%, and the plan calls for continued reductions toward a target of 4.99% - contingent on the state meeting revenue benchmarks. This makes Georgia increasingly competitive with flat-tax neighbors like North Carolina (4.5%).

The transition to a flat tax doesn't mean lower-income Georgians will pay more. The standard deduction and personal exemptions still apply, ensuring that the first several thousand dollars of income remain effectively untaxed. The primary effect is simplification and a lower marginal rate on higher incomes. Based on our testing of various income levels, the transition benefits middle-to-high earners the most while keeping effective rates roughly stable for lower earners.

Your Take-Home Pay in Georgia

Based on our analysis and original research, here are the most effective strategies to your Georgia take-home pay:

1. pre-tax retirement contributions. Every dollar you contribute to a traditional 401(k) (up to $23,000 in 2025, or $30,500 if 50+) reduces both your federal and Georgia state taxable income. At a combined marginal rate of approximately 27-30% (22% federal + 5.49% Georgia), a $23,000 contribution saves you roughly $6,300-$6,900 in current-year taxes. Don't leave this money on the table, especially if your employer offers a match.

2. Use an HSA if eligible. Health Savings Accounts offer triple tax benefits: contributions are pre-tax (reducing federal and Georgia state taxes), growth is tax-free, and qualified withdrawals are tax-free. The 2025 contribution limits are $4,150 for self-only and $8,300 for family coverage. An HSA is arguably the most tax-efficient savings vehicle available, and Georgia fully conforms to the federal HSA deduction.

3. your W-4. Many people over-withhold, giving the government an interest-free loan. Use the IRS Tax Withholding Estimator to ensure your W-4 accurately reflects your tax situation. If you consistently get large refunds, you're withholding too much. Adjusting your W-4 increases each paycheck without changing your overall tax liability.

4. Consider Georgia's 529 Plan. If you have children or plan to pursue education, contributions to Georgia's Path2College 529 plan are deductible on your Georgia return up to $8,000 per beneficiary. For a couple with two children, that's up to $32,000 in Georgia-deductible contributions annually (if each parent contributes the max for each child). At the 5.49% rate, that's a state tax savings of up to $1,757.

Georgia Cost of Living Context

Understanding your take-home pay requires context. Georgia generally offers a lower cost of living than the national average, particularly outside metro Atlanta. According to Bureau of Economic Analysis data, Georgia's regional price parity is approximately 92.4 (national average = 100), meaning goods and services cost about 7.6% less than the national average.

, metro Atlanta skews higher, with a regional price parity around 103-105. If you're earning a $75,000 salary in Atlanta, your purchasing power is roughly equivalent to $71,000-$73,000 nationally. In more affordable areas like Savannah, Augusta, or Macon, that same $75,000 stretches significantly further - potentially equivalent to $80,000+ in purchasing power. Georgia vs. Florida The Tax Migration Question

One of the most common questions I've encountered is whether moving to Florida for its zero state income tax makes financial sense. The answer isn't as straightforward as it seems. On a $75,000 salary, you'd save approximately $3,475 in state income tax by moving to Florida. But consider:

  • Florida's effective property tax rate (0.89%) is comparable to Georgia's (0.92%), so no major savings there
  • Florida's combined state and average local sales tax rate (7.01%) is slightly lower than Georgia's average (7.35%), a minor difference
  • Florida has significantly higher homeowner's insurance - often 2-3x what you'd pay in Georgia, potentially offsetting income tax savings
  • Comparable Florida cities (Tampa, Orlando) generally have higher housing costs than equivalent Georgia cities

for most middle-income earners, the income tax savings of moving to Florida are partially offset by higher costs elsewhere. The math becomes more favorable for high earners ($150,000+) where the income tax savings outweigh other cost differences. This isn't financial advice - just our analysis of the publicly available data.

Understanding Marginal vs. Effective Tax Rate

A common source of confusion in salary calculations is the difference between marginal and effective tax rates. Your marginal rate is the rate applied to your last dollar of income - the highest bracket you reach. Your effective rate is the average rate across all your income. They can differ substantially.

For example, a single Georgia filer earning $75,000 has a federal marginal rate of 22% and a Georgia marginal rate of 5.49%, for a combined marginal rate of 27.49%. But their effective rates are much lower: approximately 14% federal and 4.69% Georgia, for an effective combined rate of about 18.69%. The difference matters for financial planning - using the marginal rate for budgeting would significantly underestimate your take-home pay.

This is exactly why I this calculator - to show you the actual tax computation, not just the bracket your top dollar falls into. Pre-Tax Deductions The Double Tax Savings

One of the most features of this Georgia salary calculator is the pre-tax deduction modeling. When you contribute to a 401(k) or pay health insurance premiums pre-tax, you get a "double tax savings" - reducing both federal and Georgia state taxes simultaneously. Here's the concrete impact:

A $500/month 401(k) contribution ($6,000/year) for a single Georgia filer earning $75,000 saves approximately:

  • $6,000 × 22% = $1,320
  • $6,000 × 5.49% = $329
  • Total annual tax savings: $1,649
  • Net cost of the $6,000 contribution: only $4,351

This means your 401(k) effectively costs you 72.5 cents for every dollar contributed. If your employer matches contributions (say 50% up to 6%), you're getting $3,000 in free money on top of the $1,649 tax savings. Don't overlook this - it's the single most impactful financial available to most Georgia workers.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the Georgia state income tax brackets for 2025?
Georgia uses a graduated income tax with 6 brackets for 2025: 1% on the first $750, 2% on $751, $2,250, 3% on $2,251, $3,750, 4% on $3,751, $5,250, 5% on $5,251, $7,000, and 5.49% on income over $7,000. These apply to Georgia taxable income after standard deductions and personal exemptions.
What is the standard deduction in Georgia for 2025?
For 2025, the Georgia standard deduction is $5,400 for single filers, $7,100 for head of household, and $7,100 for married filing jointly. These amounts reduce your Georgia taxable income before state tax brackets are applied. Note that Georgia's standard deduction is separate from and lower than the federal standard deduction.
How much is FICA tax in Georgia?
FICA tax is a federal tax and is the same in Georgia as in all states: 6.2% for Social Security (on income up to $168,600 in 2025) and 1.45% for Medicare (no income cap). High earners pay an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax on income over $200,000 for single filers. FICA isn't reducible by state deductions - it applies to your gross pay minus certain pre-tax items.
Does Georgia have local income taxes?
No, Georgia does not have local or city income taxes. Only the state income tax applies, which simplifies payroll compared to states like Ohio, Pennsylvania, or New York that have municipal income taxes., Georgia does have varying local sales tax rates and property tax rates that differ by county.
How does Georgia's tax rate compare to neighboring states?
Georgia's top rate of 5.49% is moderate compared to neighbors. Florida and Tennessee have no state income tax on wages. North Carolina has a flat 4.5% rate. Alabama's top rate is 5%, and South Carolina's is 6.4%. Georgia's ongoing tax reform aims to lower the top rate to 4.99% over the coming years, which would make it more competitive regionally.
What pre-tax deductions reduce my Georgia taxes?
Common pre-tax deductions that reduce both federal and Georgia state taxes include: traditional 401(k) contributions (up to $23,000 in 2025, or $30,500 if 50+), 403(b) contributions, health insurance premiums, HSA contributions ($4,150 single / $8,300 family), and FSA contributions ($3,200). These are deducted from gross pay before taxes are calculated, providing a double tax benefit at both the federal and state level.
Is Georgia planning to change its income tax system?
Yes, Georgia passed HB 1015 in 2022 which began transitioning the state from a graduated income tax to a flat tax. The rate is decreasing from 5.75% toward a flat 4.99% over several years, contingent on revenue benchmarks being met. For 2025, the effective top rate is 5.49%. This gradual transition makes Georgia increasingly competitive with flat-tax states in the Southeast.
