Understanding Internet Speeds

The biggest source of confusion in download time estimates is the difference between bits and bytes. ISPs advertise speeds in megabits per second (Mbps), but file sizes are measured in megabytes (MB). There are 8 bits in a byte, so a 100 Mbps connection transfers about 12.5 MB per second, not 100 MB per second. I can't tell you how many times I've seen people make this mistake.

Here is the conversion chain: 1 byte = 8 bits. So 1 MB/s = 8 Mbps. A "100 Mbps" connection gives you 12.5 MB/s of raw throughput, and after protocol overhead (typically 5% to 15%), you get roughly 10 to 11 MB/s of actual file transfer speed. This is well documented on Wikipedia's data rate units article and regularly discussed on Stack Overflow's bandwidth tag.

Protocol overhead comes from TCP/IP headers, HTTP headers, TLS encryption, and retransmission of dropped packets. For typical web downloads, 10% overhead is a reasonable estimate. For VPN connections or high-latency links, overhead can be higher. The speedtest-net npm package can help you measure your actual connection speed programmatically.

Real-World vs Advertised Speeds

Your ISP says you have a 500 Mbps connection, but your downloads never seem that fast. There are several reasons for this, and understanding them helps set realistic expectations.

Server-side limits. The download speed is constrained by the slowest link in the chain. If the server you are downloading from has a 100 Mbps connection, your 500 Mbps pipe won't help. CDNs help with this, but not all downloads come from CDN-cached servers.

Network congestion. Shared bandwidth means peak hours (evenings, weekends) often deliver slower speeds. Cable internet is particularly susceptible to this because you share bandwidth with your neighbors. Fiber connections are less affected.

WiFi degradation. WiFi speeds drop significantly with distance, walls, and interference. A 1 Gbps fiber connection tested via WiFi from the next room might only deliver 200 to 400 Mbps. For maximum speed, use a wired Ethernet connection. This is a frequent topic on Hacker News networking discussions.

Protocol overhead. TCP/IP, TLS encryption, and HTTP headers all consume bandwidth. For small files, the overhead ratio is worse because header sizes are fixed regardless of payload size.

ISP throttling. Some ISPs throttle specific types of traffic (streaming, torrents) or reduce speeds after you hit a data cap. This is harder to detect but can significantly affect actual throughput.

Connection Types Explained

Understanding the differences between connection types helps you set realistic expectations for download speeds. Each technology has fundamentally different characteristics that affect real-world performance.

3G (HSPA+) delivers 1 to 10 Mbps in practice, though theoretical maximums are higher. Latency is typically 50 to 100 ms. 3G is being phased out by most carriers, but it remains the fallback in areas without 4G or 5G coverage. At 3G speeds, downloading a 1 GB file takes 15 to 30 minutes, and streaming 4K video isn't feasible.

4G LTE delivers 10 to 100 Mbps in most areas, with latency of 30 to 50 ms. modern variants like LTE-A can reach 300 Mbps+ under ideal conditions. 4G is currently the backbone of mobile internet and works well for streaming, video calls, and moderate file downloads. Most people on 4G can download a movie-sized file (4 to 5 GB) in under 10 minutes.

5G comes in three flavors: low-band (similar to 4G speeds but broader coverage), mid-band (100 to 900 Mbps with decent coverage), and mmWave (1 to 4+ Gbps but only works within a few hundred feet of the tower). Mid-band 5G is the sweet spot for most users and is rapidly expanding. At 300 Mbps, you can download a 50 GB game in about 20 minutes.

Cable internet uses coaxial cable and delivers 100 to 1200 Mbps download speeds. The major limitation is that bandwidth is shared with your neighbors on the same node, so peak-hour speeds can drop significantly. Upload speeds are typically 5% to 20% of download speeds, which matters for cloud backups and video uploads.

Fiber optic uses light pulses through glass strands and delivers 100 Mbps to 10 Gbps symmetrically. Fiber has the lowest latency, the most consistent speeds, and isn't affected by distance or electromagnetic interference. It is the gold standard for internet connectivity. If fiber is available in your area, it is almost always the best choice for both speed and reliability.

DSL runs over telephone copper lines and maxes out at 25 to 100 Mbps (VDSL2) with speeds degrading rapidly over distance from the exchange. DSL is being phased out in favor of fiber and fixed wireless, but it remains the only option in many rural areas.

Satellite internet (Starlink, HughesNet) delivers 25 to 250 Mbps but with higher latency (20 to 600 ms depending on orbit). Starlink's low-earth orbit satellites offer significantly better latency than traditional geostationary satellites. For rural areas without other broadband options, satellite can provide meaningful connectivity.

Download Speed Optimization

If your downloads are consistently slower than expected, there are several practical steps you can take to improve performance. I've tested these across dozens of setups and they consistently make a difference.

Use Ethernet instead of WiFi. A wired connection eliminates WiFi interference, signal degradation, and protocol overhead. In my testing, switching from WiFi to Ethernet typically increases throughput by 20% to 50%, especially on faster connections (500 Mbps+). WiFi 6 has narrowed the gap, but Ethernet remains king for consistent maximum performance.

Position your router optimally. If Ethernet isn't feasible, router placement matters enormously. Central location in your home, improved position, away from metal objects and microwaves. Every wall between you and the router reduces signal strength by roughly 30% to 50% depending on the material. Concrete and brick walls are especially bad.

Use the 5 GHz band. Most modern routers broadcast on both 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz frequencies. The 5 GHz band is faster but has shorter range. If you are within 30 feet of your router, always use 5 GHz. The 2.4 GHz band is more congested (microwaves, Bluetooth, and your neighbors all use it) and slower.

Update your DNS. Slow DNS resolution adds latency to every new connection. Switching to a fast DNS provider like Cloudflare (1.1.1.1) or Google (8.8.8.8) can shave milliseconds off each request, which adds up for pages with many resources. DNS doesn't affect raw download speed for large files, but it improves the responsiveness of browsing and the initiation of downloads.

Use a download manager. Multi-threaded download managers split files into chunks and download them simultaneously. This can circumvent per-connection speed limits imposed by some servers. Tools like aria2 (available via the aria2 npm package) support up to 16 simultaneous connections per download and can significantly improve transfer rates from servers that throttle individual connections.

Schedule large downloads. Network congestion follows predictable patterns. Early morning (2 AM to 7 AM) typically offers the best speeds because fewer people are online. If you have a large file to download (50 GB+ game update, operating system image, dataset), scheduling it for off-peak hours can cut the transfer time significantly.

Data Caps and Fair Use Policies

Many ISPs impose data caps that limit how much you can download per month. Comcast's cap is 1.2 TB, AT&T offers unlimited on fiber but caps DSL plans, and mobile carriers have various throttling thresholds. Understanding your cap is important for planning large downloads.

To put data caps in perspective: streaming Netflix at 4K uses about 7 GB per hour. A 1.2 TB monthly cap allows about 170 hours of 4K streaming, or about 5.7 hours per day. Most households won't hit this from streaming alone, but adding game downloads (50 to 100 GB each), OS updates, cloud backups, and work-from-home video calls can push you toward the limit.

If you regularly hit your data cap, consider upgrading to an unlimited plan, switching to a provider that doesn't cap (many fiber ISPs don't), or using quality-of-service settings on your router to prioritize traffic and reduce unnecessary data consumption from background processes and automatic updates.

Mobile data caps are far more restrictive. Most "unlimited" mobile plans throttle speeds after 20 to 50 GB of high-speed data. For mobile downloads, I'd recommend doing large transfers on WiFi whenever possible and reserving mobile data for email, messaging, and light browsing.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my download slower than the calculator estimates?

Several factors can reduce actual speed: server-side bandwidth limits, network congestion during peak hours, WiFi signal degradation, VPN overhead, ISP throttling, and background applications consuming bandwidth. The calculator shows the theoretical best case based on your connection speed minus protocol overhead.

What is the difference between Mbps and MB/s?

Mbps (megabits per second) is how ISPs measure connection speed. MB/s (megabytes per second) is how operating systems display transfer rates. There are 8 bits in a byte, so 100 Mbps equals 12.5 MB/s. Most download managers and browsers show speed in MB/s, which is why the number looks about 8x smaller than your advertised connection speed.

How do I find my actual internet speed?

Use a speed test service like Speedtest.net or Fast.com. For the most precise results, connect via Ethernet (not WiFi), close all other applications, and run the test multiple times at different times of day. Your actual speed will almost always be lower than what your ISP advertises.

Why are upload speeds so much slower than download speeds?

Most residential internet connections are asymmetric, meaning download bandwidth is much higher than upload. This is because most consumers download far more than they upload. Cable connections typically have upload speeds that are 1/10th to 1/5th of download speeds. Fiber connections often offer symmetric speeds (equal upload and download), which is one of their major advantages.

Does using a VPN slow down downloads?

Yes, typically by 10% to 30%. VPN adds encryption overhead and routes your traffic through an additional server. The further the VPN server is from your location, the more latency increases. For large file downloads, the throughput impact is usually more significant than the latency impact.

Can I download faster by using multiple connections?

Download managers that split files into chunks and download them in parallel can sometimes achieve faster speeds, especially when the server limits per-connection bandwidth. However, this won't exceed your total connection speed. It works because it circumvents per-connection throttling, not because it increases your overall bandwidth.

How much data does streaming use compared to downloads?

Netflix 4K uses about 7 GB per hour. A 2-hour 4K movie is roughly 14 GB. YouTube 4K uses about 12 to 15 GB per hour due to higher bitrates. Spotify music streaming uses about 150 MB per hour at high quality. These numbers help contextualize file sizes when estimating download times.
