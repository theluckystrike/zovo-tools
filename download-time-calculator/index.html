Understanding Internet Speeds

The biggest source of confusion in download time estimates is the difference between bits and bytes. ISPs advertise speeds in megabits per second (Mbps), but file sizes are measured in megabytes (MB). There are 8 bits in a byte, so a 100 Mbps connection transfers about 12.5 MB per second, not 100 MB per second. I can't tell you how many times I've seen people make this mistake.

Here is the conversion chain: 1 byte = 8 bits. So 1 MB/s = 8 Mbps. A "100 Mbps" connection gives you 12.5 MB/s of raw throughput, and after protocol overhead (typically 5% to 15%), you get roughly 10 to 11 MB/s of actual file transfer speed. This is well documented on Wikipedia's data rate units article and regularly discussed on Stack Overflow's bandwidth tag.

Protocol overhead comes from TCP/IP headers, HTTP headers, TLS encryption, and retransmission of dropped packets. For typical web downloads, 10% overhead is a reasonable estimate. For VPN connections or high-latency links, overhead can be higher. The speedtest-net npm package can help you measure your actual connection speed programmatically.

Real-World vs Advertised Speeds

Your ISP says you have a 500 Mbps connection, but your downloads never seem that fast. There are several reasons for this, and understanding them helps set realistic expectations.

Server-side limits. The download speed is constrained by the slowest link in the chain. If the server you are downloading from has a 100 Mbps connection, your 500 Mbps pipe won't help. CDNs help with this, but not all downloads come from CDN-cached servers.

Network congestion. Shared bandwidth means peak hours (evenings, weekends) often deliver slower speeds. Cable internet is particularly susceptible to this because you share bandwidth with your neighbors. Fiber connections are less affected.

WiFi degradation. WiFi speeds drop significantly with distance, walls, and interference. A 1 Gbps fiber connection tested via WiFi from the next room might only deliver 200 to 400 Mbps. For maximum speed, use a wired Ethernet connection. This is a frequent topic on Hacker News networking discussions.

Protocol overhead. TCP/IP, TLS encryption, and HTTP headers all consume bandwidth. For small files, the overhead ratio is worse because header sizes are fixed regardless of payload size.

ISP throttling. Some ISPs throttle specific types of traffic (streaming, torrents) or reduce speeds after you hit a data cap. This is harder to detect but can significantly affect actual throughput.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why is my download slower than the calculator estimates?

Several factors can reduce actual speed: server-side bandwidth limits, network congestion during peak hours, WiFi signal degradation, VPN overhead, ISP throttling, and background applications consuming bandwidth. The calculator shows the theoretical best case based on your connection speed minus protocol overhead.

What is the difference between Mbps and MB/s?

Mbps (megabits per second) is how ISPs measure connection speed. MB/s (megabytes per second) is how operating systems display transfer rates. There are 8 bits in a byte, so 100 Mbps equals 12.5 MB/s. Most download managers and browsers show speed in MB/s, which is why the number looks about 8x smaller than your advertised connection speed.

How do I find my actual internet speed?

Use a speed test service like Speedtest.net or Fast.com. For the most accurate results, connect via Ethernet (not WiFi), close all other applications, and run the test multiple times at different times of day. Your actual speed will almost always be lower than what your ISP advertises.

Why are upload speeds so much slower than download speeds?

Most residential internet connections are asymmetric, meaning download bandwidth is much higher than upload. This is because most consumers download far more than they upload. Cable connections typically have upload speeds that are 1/10th to 1/5th of download speeds. Fiber connections often offer symmetric speeds (equal upload and download), which is one of their major advantages.

Does using a VPN slow down downloads?

Yes, typically by 10% to 30%. VPN adds encryption overhead and routes your traffic through an additional server. The further the VPN server is from your location, the more latency increases. For large file downloads, the throughput impact is usually more significant than the latency impact.

Can I download faster by using multiple connections?

Download managers that split files into chunks and download them in parallel can sometimes achieve faster speeds, especially when the server limits per-connection bandwidth. However, this won't exceed your total connection speed. It works because it circumvents per-connection throttling, not because it increases your overall bandwidth.

How much data does streaming use compared to downloads?

Netflix 4K uses about 7 GB per hour. A 2-hour 4K movie is roughly 14 GB. YouTube 4K uses about 12 to 15 GB per hour due to higher bitrates. Spotify music streaming uses about 150 MB per hour at high quality. These numbers help contextualize file sizes when estimating download times.
