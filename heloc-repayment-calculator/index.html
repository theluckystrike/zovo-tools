HELOC Draw Period vs Repayment Period

Every HELOC has two distinct phases, and understanding both is essential to managing your debt effectively. I see many borrowers get comfortable with the low payments during the draw period, only to face a sudden and significant payment increase when the repayment phase begins.

The Draw Period (Typically 5 to 10 Years)

During the draw period, your HELOC functions like a credit card secured by your home. You can borrow funds up to your credit limit, repay them, and borrow again. Most lenders require only interest-only payments during this phase. On a $50,000 balance at 8.5%, that comes out to roughly $354 per month. The principal balance stays the same unless you voluntarily pay it down.

The flexibility is the appeal. You only borrow what you need, when you need it. But that flexibility creates a trap: you can spend the entire draw period paying only interest and owe the full original amount when repayment begins.

The Repayment Period (Typically 10 to 20 Years)

Once the draw period ends, the credit line closes and you enter repayment. Your payments now include both principal and interest, amortized over the repayment term. That same $50,000 at 8.5% over 15 years becomes approximately $493 per month. That is a 39% increase from the interest-only payment.

The repayment period works like a standard amortizing loan. Early payments go mostly toward interest, with the principal portion gradually increasing over time. If your repayment period is shorter (10 years instead of 20), payments are higher but total interest is significantly lower.

The Two-Phase Timeline

A typical 10/20 HELOC means 10 years of draw plus 20 years of repayment, for a total of 30 years. A 5/15 HELOC means 5 years of draw plus 15 years of repayment (20 years total). The combination determines your total timeline and total interest cost. Shorter terms cost less overall but require higher monthly payments.

How HELOC Payments Work

HELOC payment calculations differ from standard mortgage math because of the two-phase structure. Understanding the formulas behind each phase helps you plan accurately.

Interest-Only Payment Formula (Draw Period)

The draw period payment is straightforward. Take your outstanding balance, multiply by the annual interest rate, and divide by 12. For a $60,000 balance at 9.0% APR, the monthly interest-only payment is $60,000 x 0.09 / 12 = $450. This payment changes whenever your rate adjusts or your balance changes.

Amortizing Payment Formula (Repayment Period)

The repayment period uses the standard amortization formula: M = P x [r(1+r)^n] / [(1+r)^n - 1], where P is the balance at the start of repayment, r is the monthly interest rate, and n is the number of repayment months. This formula produces a fixed payment amount (assuming the rate does not change) that gradually shifts from interest-heavy to principal-heavy over time.

One detail that catches borrowers off guard: during the draw period, if you make only the minimum interest-only payment, your balance at the start of repayment equals your full drawn amount. If you have been making principal payments during the draw period, your repayment-phase balance is lower, and so are your required payments. This is why I always recommend paying at least something toward principal during the draw period, even if the lender does not require it.

Some lenders require minimum principal payments during the draw period (typically 1% to 2% of the balance). Others allow pure interest-only payments. Check your HELOC agreement for the specific terms. The difference between these structures matters when projecting your repayment timeline.

Variable Rates and Your HELOC

Nearly all HELOCs carry variable interest rates, which means your payment amount changes as rates change. The rate is typically calculated as the prime rate plus a margin. In March 2026, the prime rate sits at 7.5%, so a HELOC with a 1% margin would carry an 8.5% rate.

When the Federal Reserve raises or lowers the federal funds rate, the prime rate follows, and your HELOC rate adjusts accordingly. During 2022 and 2023, the Fed raised rates aggressively, pushing HELOC rates from around 4% to over 9% in less than two years. Borrowers who had budgeted for $200 monthly payments suddenly faced $400 or more.

Some lenders offer fixed-rate HELOC options or the ability to convert a portion of your variable balance to a fixed rate. Understanding HELOC Rate Caps

Federal and state regulations require most HELOCs to include rate caps that limit how high your interest rate can go. These caps provide a ceiling on your worst-case payment scenario.

If your HELOC started at 5.0% with a lifetime cap of 6 percentage points, the maximum rate is 11.0%. On a $50,000 balance, that cap means your worst-case interest-only payment would be $458 per month. Knowing your cap helps you stress-test your budget against the worst-case scenario.

I recommend every HELOC borrower locate their rate cap in their loan documents and calculate what their payment would be at the maximum rate. If you cannot comfortably afford the payment at the capped rate, you may be carrying too much HELOC debt relative to your income.

HELOC vs Home Equity Loan

These two products both tap your home equity, but they work differently. The right choice depends on how you plan to use the funds and your tolerance for payment variability.

For a home renovation with a defined budget, a home equity loan gives you a known payment from the start. For ongoing expenses like college tuition paid over several semesters, a HELOC lets you draw funds as needed without paying interest on unused capacity. Many homeowners end up choosing based on whether they value flexibility (HELOC) or predictability (home equity loan).

One consideration that often gets overlooked: a HELOC with no balance costs nothing to maintain. You can open one, use it only in emergencies, and pay zero interest. A home equity loan starts accruing interest immediately on the full amount. This makes a HELOC an effective emergency fund backup, though it should not replace actual liquid savings.

HELOC Interest Tax Deductibility (2018+ Rules)

The rules around HELOC interest deductions changed significantly with the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (TCJA) of 2017, effective for tax years 2018 through 2025. Many borrowers still operate under the old assumption that all HELOC interest is deductible. That is no longer the case.

Current IRS Rules for HELOC Interest Deduction

HELOC interest is deductible only if the borrowed funds are used to "buy, build, or substantially improve" the home that secures the HELOC. Using HELOC funds for debt consolidation, car purchases, vacations, or college tuition means the interest is not deductible, even though the loan is secured by your home.

The combined limit for mortgage interest deduction (primary mortgage plus HELOC or home equity loan) is $750,000 for loans originated after December 15, 2017. For loans originated before that date, the limit is $1,000,000. These limits apply to the total debt across all qualifying loans.

To claim the deduction, you must itemize deductions on Schedule A rather than taking the standard deduction. With the standard deduction at $15,000 for single filers and $30,000 for married filing jointly in 2025, many homeowners find that itemizing no longer provides a benefit. You need total itemized deductions (including HELOC interest, state taxes, charitable donations, etc.) to exceed the standard deduction for the interest deduction to matter.

If you do use HELOC funds for home improvements, keep detailed records of how the money was spent. The IRS may require documentation to substantiate the deduction. I recommend maintaining a separate account for HELOC draws used on home improvements to create a clear paper trail.

Risks of Rising Interest Rates

Variable-rate debt in a rising rate environment can erode your budget faster than most borrowers anticipate. I have seen borrowers who initially found their HELOC payments manageable face real financial strain after consecutive rate increases.

Mitigation strategies include paying down the balance aggressively during low-rate periods, converting a portion to a fixed rate if your lender offers that option, and maintaining a budget buffer that accounts for potential rate increases. Some financial planners recommend budgeting at the rate cap level to ensure you can always afford your HELOC, regardless of rate movements.

HELOC Payoff Strategies

Paying off a HELOC efficiently requires a deliberate approach. Here are the strategies I recommend based on different financial situations.

Strategy 1: Aggressive Principal Payments During Draw Period

The draw period offers an opportunity most borrowers waste. Since your required payment is interest-only, any extra money you send goes entirely to reducing the principal. A $200 per month extra payment on a $50,000 balance over 5 years of draw period reduces the repayment-phase starting balance to $38,000, saving over $15,000 in total interest.

Strategy 2: Convert to Fixed-Rate Before Repayment

If your lender offers a fixed-rate conversion option, consider locking in before the repayment period begins. This eliminates rate risk during the longest and most expensive phase of your HELOC. The fixed rate is typically 0.25% to 0.75% higher than the current variable rate, but the predictability is worth the premium for large balances.

Strategy 3: Refinance into a Home Equity Loan

As the draw period nears its end, you can refinance the HELOC balance into a fixed-rate home equity loan. This resets the clock with a known payment schedule and eliminates the variable rate exposure. Compare the new loan's total cost (including closing costs) against the projected HELOC repayment cost to determine if refinancing makes financial sense.

Strategy 4: Lump Sum Paydown

If you receive a bonus, tax refund, inheritance, or other windfall, applying it directly to your HELOC balance provides an immediate and guaranteed return equal to your HELOC interest rate. At 8.5%, a $10,000 lump sum saves $850 per year in interest and shortens your repayment timeline.

Strategy 5: Bi-Weekly Payments

Making half your monthly payment every two weeks results in 26 half-payments per year, which equals 13 full payments instead of 12. That extra payment goes entirely to principal and can shave years off your repayment timeline without a significant impact on your cash flow.

HELOC Refinance and Conversion Options

If your HELOC is nearing the end of its draw period or you want to restructure the debt, several options exist.

Opening a new HELOC to replace the existing one restarts the draw period and can be useful if you still need access to the credit line. However, this extends the timeline and total interest cost. You will also need to qualify based on current income, credit, and home value.

Refinancing into a cash-out mortgage rolls the HELOC balance into your primary mortgage at (typically) a lower rate. This works well when mortgage rates are favorable and you have sufficient equity. The downside is that you spread a potentially small balance over 30 years, paying more total interest unless you maintain higher payments.

A fixed-rate home equity loan replaces the variable-rate HELOC with predictable payments. Closing costs typically run 2% to 5% of the loan amount, so this option works best for larger balances where the rate certainty justifies the upfront expense.

Some lenders offer HELOC modification programs that extend the draw period, reduce the rate, or restructure the repayment terms. Contact your lender directly to ask about modification options, especially if you are facing payment difficulties.

Common HELOC Borrower Mistakes

After working through hundreds of HELOC scenarios, I have identified the mistakes that cost borrowers the most money.

Treating the HELOC like free money is the most expensive mistake. The draw period's low payments create an illusion of affordability. A $100,000 HELOC at 8.5% costs $708 per month in interest alone. Over a 10-year draw period with zero principal payments, you pay $84,960 in interest and still owe the full $100,000. That is the equivalent of paying for the money twice.

Ignoring the repayment phase is a close second. Many borrowers sign HELOC documents without fully understanding the payment transition. Every HELOC disclosure includes the repayment terms, but the draw period's flexibility overshadows the future obligation in most people's minds.

Drawing down the full limit immediately defeats the purpose of a HELOC's revolving structure. Only borrow what you need, when you need it. A $100,000 credit limit does not mean you should have a $100,000 balance.

Using HELOC funds for depreciating assets (cars, vacations, consumer goods) puts your home at risk for items that lose value. If you cannot repay, the lender can foreclose. HELOC funds are best used for investments that increase home value or provide a return that exceeds the borrowing cost.

Failing to budget for rate increases leaves you vulnerable. If your budget works only at the current rate, a 2 to 3 percentage point increase could create a shortfall. Always stress-test your budget at a higher rate scenario.

Real World HELOC Scenarios

Let me walk through three common HELOC situations to show how the numbers work in practice.

Scenario A: Kitchen Renovation

A homeowner draws $45,000 from a HELOC at 8.25% to fund a kitchen renovation. The draw period has 4 years remaining, and the repayment term is 15 years. During the draw period, the interest-only payment is $309 per month. When repayment begins, the P+I payment rises to $437 per month. Over the full life of the HELOC (4 + 15 = 19 years), total interest paid is $63,472. If the homeowner adds $150 per month in extra principal during the draw period, they reduce the repayment starting balance to $37,800 and total interest drops to $50,159, saving $13,313.

Scenario B: Debt Consolidation

A homeowner consolidates $35,000 in credit card debt (average 22% APR) into a HELOC at 9.0%. The interest-only payment drops from approximately $642 per month (credit card minimums) to $263 per month. The savings are real, but the risk has shifted to the home. If this homeowner continues paying $642 per month toward the HELOC (the same amount as the old credit card payments), the HELOC is paid off in approximately 5.5 years with total interest of $9,240. Compare that to the credit card path: at minimum payments, the credit cards would take 15+ years and cost over $40,000 in interest.

Scenario C: Emergency Medical Expense

A homeowner faces $25,000 in unexpected medical bills and draws from a HELOC at 8.5% with 6 years remaining on the draw period. The interest-only payment is $177 per month. With a 20-year repayment period, the P+I payment will be $217 per month. Total interest over the 26-year life: $38,668. By doubling the draw-period payment to $354 per month (adding $177 in principal), the balance at repayment start drops to $12,244, the repayment payment becomes $106, and total interest drops to $16,912.

Use the results as a planning tool and verify with your lender for exact figures.
What is a good HELOC interest rate in 2026?
As of March 2026, competitive HELOC rates range from 7.25% to 9.5% for borrowers with good to excellent credit and combined loan-to-value ratios below 80%. Rates above 10% typically indicate either a high LTV ratio or credit score below 680. Shopping multiple lenders can yield a 0.5% to 1.0% rate improvement.
Can I deduct HELOC interest on my taxes?
Only if the funds are used to buy, build, or substantially improve the home securing the HELOC. Under the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act (2018 through 2025), HELOC interest used for other purposes (debt consolidation, education, etc.) is not deductible. The combined mortgage and HELOC debt limit for the deduction is $750,000.
What happens if I cannot make my HELOC payment?
A HELOC is secured by your home. Missing payments can lead to late fees, credit score damage, and eventually foreclosure. If you are struggling with payments, contact your lender immediately to discuss modification options, forbearance, or restructuring before you miss a payment.
Should I pay off my HELOC or invest the money?
At current HELOC rates of 8% to 10%, paying off the HELOC provides a guaranteed, risk-free return equal to your rate. Historically, stock market returns average 10% annually before inflation, but with significant volatility. For most borrowers, paying off variable-rate HELOC debt first provides better risk-adjusted returns than investing.
Can I refinance my HELOC before the draw period ends?
Yes. You can refinance a HELOC at any time into a new HELOC, a home equity loan, or a cash-out refinance of your primary mortgage. Refinancing before the draw period ends can be strategic if rates have dropped or if you want to lock in a fixed rate before repayment begins.
How much equity do I need for a HELOC?
Most lenders require at least 15% to 20% equity in your home after accounting for the primary mortgage and the HELOC combined. The combined loan-to-value (CLTV) ratio limit is typically 80% to 90%. Some lenders offer programs up to 95% CLTV for borrowers with excellent credit.
What is the difference between HELOC APR and the margin?
The HELOC rate is calculated as the prime rate plus a margin. The margin is set by the lender based on your creditworthiness (typically 0% to 2%). The APR for a HELOC includes the rate plus any annual fees or other charges, making it the more complete cost measure.
Can my lender freeze my HELOC?
Yes. Lenders can freeze or reduce your HELOC credit limit if your home value declines significantly, your financial situation changes materially, or during economic downturns. Under the Truth in Lending Act, the lender must notify you and provide the reason for the freeze. You can request reinstatement if conditions improve.
Is a HELOC better than a personal loan?
HELOCs typically carry lower interest rates than personal loans because they are secured by your home. However, a personal loan does not put your home at risk. For borrowers who can comfortably make the payments, a HELOC saves money on interest. For those with unstable income or spending habits, an unsecured personal loan may be the safer choice despite the higher rate.
How long does it take to pay off a HELOC?
The total HELOC timeline includes both the draw period and the repayment period. Common structures are 10/20 (30 years total) and 5/15 (20 years total). With extra payments, you can significantly shorten the repayment period. A $50,000 balance at 8.5% with $200 monthly extra payments on a 15-year repayment can be paid off in approximately 10.5 years instead of 15.
