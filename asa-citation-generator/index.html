What Is ASA Citation Style

ASA citation style is the official formatting system developed by the American Sociological Association for academic writing in sociology and closely related disciplines. First established to bring consistency to sociological scholarship, the ASA style has become the expected standard across sociology journals, university coursework, and professional publications in the field.

The style operates on an author-date system. When referencing a source within the body of a paper, writers include the author's last name and the year of publication in parentheses. The corresponding full citation appears in an alphabetically organized reference list at the end of the document. This dual-layer approach allows readers to quickly identify sources while reading and then locate the complete bibliographic details when needed.

ASA style draws some structural similarities from other social science citation formats but maintains distinctive conventions that set it apart. The formatting of titles, the punctuation sequences, and the treatment of different source types all follow rules specific to the ASA Style Guide. Sociology students and researchers who have previously used APA or Chicago should pay careful attention to these differences, as even minor formatting deviations can result in point deductions or manuscript rejections.

The current edition of the ASA Style Guide is the 7th edition, published by the American Sociological Association. It covers not only citation formatting but also manuscript preparation, writing style conventions, and guidelines for tables and figures. Any researcher submitting to an ASA-affiliated journal must follow these guidelines precisely.

ASA Formatting Rules by Source Type

Journal Articles

Journal articles are the most frequently cited source type in sociology. The ASA format for a journal article follows this structure:

Last, First. Year. "Article Title." Journal Name Volume(Issue):Pages. doi:xx.xxxx/xxxxx

Note several key details. The article title is enclosed in quotation marks and uses sentence case, meaning only the first word, proper nouns, and the first word after a colon are capitalized. The journal name is italicized and uses title case. A colon separates the issue number from the page range with no spaces. When a DOI is available, it appears at the end without a trailing period.

For example, a properly formatted journal article citation looks like this:

Wilson, Sarah. 2023. "Racial disparities in educational attainment across metropolitan areas." American Sociological Review 88(4):612-645. doi:10.1177/00031224231187

Books

Book citations in ASA style use italics for the title rather than quotation marks, and they include the publication city and publisher name:

Last, First. Year. Title of Book. City: Publisher.

Book titles follow sentence case in ASA style. The city of publication is followed by a colon and then the publisher name. A period closes the citation. If multiple cities are listed by the publisher, use only the first one.

Edited Book Chapters

When citing a specific chapter within an edited volume, the format shifts to credit both the chapter author and the book editor:

Last, First. Year. "Chapter Title." Pp. xx-xx in Book Title, edited by F. Last. City: Publisher.

The abbreviation "Pp." precedes the page range of the chapter. The editor's name appears with first initial and last name, preceded by "edited by." If there are multiple editors, list all of them separated by commas with "and" before the final name.

Websites

Web sources require a retrieval date and the full URL. The format is:

Last, First. Year. "Page Title." Website Name. Retrieved Month Day, Year (URL).

The URL is enclosed in parentheses. If no author is available, the organization name takes the author position. If no date is available, use "N.d." in place of the year.

Newspaper Articles

Newspaper citations include the specific date of publication and the section or page number when available:

Last, First. Year. "Article Title." Newspaper Name, Month Day, pp. X.

For online newspaper articles without page numbers, include the URL at the end following the same retrieval format used for websites.

ASA In-Text Citations

ASA in-text citations use the author-date format enclosed in parentheses. The basic form is straightforward, but several rules govern specific situations that arise frequently in academic writing.

Single Author

For a work by one author, include the last name and year separated by a space with no comma: (Weber 2024). When the author's name appears naturally in the sentence, only the year goes in parentheses: Weber (2024) argued that institutional structures shape individual behavior.

Two Authors

When a source has two authors, include both names connected by "and": (Smith and Jones 2023). Always list both names in every citation. Do not abbreviate to "et al." for two-author works under any circumstances.

Three or More Authors

For works with three or more authors, use the first author's last name followed by "et al." in every in-text citation: (Johnson et al. 2022). This applies from the very first citation of that source. The full list of authors appears only in the reference list entry.

Multiple Works in One Citation

When citing several sources to support a single point, separate them with semicolons and arrange them alphabetically by the first author's last name: (Adams 2020; Chen 2021; Rodriguez 2019). If multiple works share the same author, list them chronologically: (Mills 2018, 2020, 2023).

Page Numbers in In-Text Citations

When quoting directly or referencing a specific passage, add the page number after a colon: (Durkheim 1897:44). There is no space between the colon and the page number. For a range, use an en-dash or hyphen: (Giddens 2015:112-115).

Institutional Authors

Organizations and institutions can serve as authors. On first citation, spell out the full name: (American Sociological Association 2019). In subsequent citations, an abbreviation may be used if it is well known: (ASA 2019).

Formatting the ASA Reference List

The reference list appears at the end of the paper on a new page. The heading "References" is centered and not bolded or italicized. Every source cited in the text must appear in the reference list, and every entry in the reference list must correspond to at least one in-text citation.

Entries are arranged alphabetically by the first author's last name. When multiple works share the same first author, single-author works come first, followed by two-author works (alphabetized by the second author's last name), then multi-author works. Works by the same author or author group are arranged chronologically from oldest to newest.

Each entry uses a hanging indent: the first line is flush with the left margin, and all subsequent lines are indented 0.5 inches. Entries are single-spaced within each citation and have a blank line between entries, though some instructors may prefer double-spacing throughout. Always verify with your specific journal or instructor guidelines.

Alphabetization Rules

ASA alphabetization follows letter-by-letter sorting. Prefixes such as "de," "van," and "von" are handled according to the author's own usage. If the author capitalizes the prefix and uses it as part of their surname (De Tocqueville), file under "D." If the prefix is lowercase and separate (van der Berg), file under "B" with a cross-reference. Mac and Mc are filed as spelled, not combined.

Multiple Works by the Same Author

When an author has several works in the same year, distinguish them by appending lowercase letters to the year: 2023a, 2023b, 2023c. The letters are assigned based on the alphabetical order of the titles. These letter suffixes must also appear in the corresponding in-text citations.

ASA vs. APA Style

ASA and APA are both author-date citation systems used in the social sciences, which leads to frequent confusion. While they share a general structure, the specific formatting rules differ in important ways.

The most visible difference is the treatment of article titles. ASA places them in quotation marks, while APA does not. The in-text citation punctuation also differs: ASA omits the comma between author and year, while APA requires it. These may seem minor, but journal editors and professors notice them immediately.

Another significant difference lies in DOI formatting. ASA uses the older doi: prefix format, while APA 7th edition requires the full https://doi.org/ URL format. Always check which style your publication or course requires before submitting.

ASA vs. Chicago Style

ASA style was originally adapted from the Chicago Manual of Style, so the two share historical roots. However, they have diverged considerably over the decades. Chicago offers two systems: notes-bibliography (commonly used in humanities) and author-date (used in sciences). ASA's author-date system more closely resembles Chicago's author-date variant but with distinct sociology-specific conventions.

Key differences include the handling of capitalization in titles, the formatting of publisher information, and the level of detail required for electronic sources. ASA is more prescriptive about DOI inclusion and retrieval dates for web sources, while Chicago author-date tends to be more flexible. If you are writing for a sociology journal or course, use ASA. If your work crosses into history, political science, or another discipline, check whether Chicago or ASA is preferred.

Common ASA Citation Mistakes

Even experienced academic writers make ASA formatting errors. The following are the most frequently encountered mistakes in manuscript submissions and student papers.

Using a Comma in In-Text Citations

The most common error is writing (Smith, 2023) instead of (Smith 2023). ASA does not use a comma between the author name and year. This mistake typically comes from writers who are more familiar with APA conventions.

Incorrect Title Capitalization

ASA requires sentence case for article and book titles, meaning only the first word, proper nouns, and the first word after a colon are capitalized. Writing "The Social Construction Of Reality" instead of "The social construction of reality" is a formatting error. Journal names, however, retain title case and italics.

Missing Quotation Marks on Article Titles

Forgetting to enclose journal article, chapter, and web page titles in quotation marks is another frequent oversight. Book titles and journal names are italicized, but shorter work titles must be in quotes.

Incorrect DOI Format

ASA uses the doi: prefix format rather than the full URL format. Writing https://doi.org/10.1177/xxxxx instead of doi:10.1177/xxxxx does not conform to ASA standards. Always strip the URL prefix and use the lowercase doi: format.

Inconsistent Et Al. Usage

Some writers spell out all authors in the first in-text citation and then switch to "et al." for subsequent citations. ASA does not follow this convention. For three or more authors, use "et al." from the very first in-text mention.

Omitting the Retrieval Date for Web Sources

ASA requires a retrieval date for online sources because web content can change or be removed. Leaving out "Retrieved [Date]" for websites and online newspaper articles is a common omission that can flag your paper for incomplete citations.

The ASA Style Guide

The ASA Style Guide, published by the American Sociological Association, is the authoritative reference for all matters of style in sociological writing. The current 7th edition covers a comprehensive range of topics beyond citation formatting.

The guide addresses manuscript preparation, including guidelines for margins, fonts, spacing, headings, tables, figures, and appendices. It also covers principles of clear sociological writing, ethical standards for attributing sources, and conventions for statistical notation. Researchers submitting to ASA journals such as the American Sociological Review, American Journal of Sociology, or Social Forces should treat the Style Guide as their primary formatting reference.

University libraries typically have copies available. The ASA also sells the guide directly through its website. For students working on term papers or theses, investing in a personal copy can save significant time compared to piecing together formatting rules from online summaries.

Writing Tips for Sociology Papers

Beyond proper citation formatting, strong sociological writing requires clarity, precision, and careful engagement with existing scholarship. The following guidelines will help strengthen any sociology paper.

Engage Critically with Sources

Sociological writing goes beyond summarizing what other scholars have said. Effective papers analyze, compare, and synthesize perspectives. Rather than simply stating that "Smith (2023) found X," explain why the finding matters, how it relates to competing perspectives, and what implications it carries for the broader theoretical framework of your paper.

Use Active Voice When Possible

While passive voice is sometimes appropriate in methodology sections, active voice generally produces clearer and more engaging prose. "The researchers conducted interviews" reads more directly than "Interviews were conducted by the researchers."

Define Sociological Concepts

Specialized terms like "social capital," "structural functionalism," or "intersectionality" carry specific theoretical weight. Define these concepts when you first introduce them, even if your audience is familiar with the terms. This demonstrates precision and prevents misinterpretation.

Integrate Quantitative and Qualitative Evidence

Many sociology papers draw on both statistical data and qualitative observations. Present quantitative findings clearly, using appropriate notation and referencing specific tables or figures. When including qualitative evidence such as interview excerpts, set them off as block quotations if they exceed 40 words, and always provide context before and analysis after each excerpt.

Maintain Consistent Formatting Throughout

Inconsistency in formatting signals carelessness to reviewers and professors. Decide on your heading levels, table styles, and citation formatting at the outset and apply them uniformly. The ASA Style Guide provides a complete template, but many departments also distribute their own style sheets that supplement or modify ASA conventions.

ASA places the year after the author followed by a period, while APA puts the year in parentheses.
Does ASA use hanging indentation in the reference list?
Yes. ASA reference list entries use a hanging indent where the first line is flush with the left margin and all subsequent lines are indented 0.5 inches. Entries are arranged alphabetically by the first author's last name, and a blank line typically separates each entry.

Does ASA require DOIs?
Yes. When a DOI is available, ASA requires its inclusion at the end of the citation in the format doi:xx.xxxx/xxxxx. Do not add a period after the DOI. Use the doi: prefix rather than the full URL format. If no DOI is available for an online source, include the URL instead.
