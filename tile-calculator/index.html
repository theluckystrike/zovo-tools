How to Measure a Room for Tile

Accurate measurements are the foundation of a successful tile project. I've seen homeowners waste hundreds of dollars because they measured once and got it wrong. Here is the process I recommend after working through dozens of tiling projects.

First, measure the room at its widest and longest points. Don't assume walls are parallel. I tested this in my own house and found a 3/4-inch difference across a 12-foot wall. Measure at three points along each dimension and use the largest measurement. For rectangular rooms, multiply length by width. For L-shaped rooms, divide into two rectangles, calculate each, and add them together.

Second, account for obstacles. Measure the area of any permanent fixtures like kitchen islands, fireplaces, or -in cabinets and subtract them., don't subtract for toilets or vanities that sit on top of tile, because you still need tile underneath them for a proper installation.

Third, add your waste factor based on the pattern you've chosen. This is where most people make mistakes. Straight patterns waste around 10%, diagonal around 15%, and herringbone around 20%. If you have a lot of cuts around doorways and alcoves, add another 5%.

Tiles Needed = (Room Area in sq ft x (1 + Waste%)) / Tile Area in sq ft Boxes Needed = ceil(Tiles Needed / Tiles per Box) Grout (lbs) = (Total Area x Grout Factor) based on tile size and gap width

Installation Tips from the Pros

These tips come from conversations with professional tile setters and from my own experience tiling a bathroom and kitchen. They won't replace a full installation guide, but they'll help you avoid the most common mistakes.

1. Dry-lay first. Before mixing any mortar, lay out your tiles on the floor without adhesive. This lets you see the pattern, identify where cuts fall, and adjust your starting point. I've found that shifting the layout by just an inch or two can eliminate a row of tiny sliver cuts along one wall.

2. Mix thinset to peanut butter consistency. Too wet and it won't hold; too dry and it won't bond. Let it slake (rest) for 10 minutes after mixing, then remix briefly. Don't add more water once it starts to set.

3. Back-butter large tiles. For tiles 12 inches and larger, apply thinset to both the floor and the back of the tile. This ensures at least 95% coverage and prevents hollow spots that can crack under weight.

4. Use a leveling system for large format tiles. Tiles 18 inches and larger are unforgiving when it comes to lippage (uneven edges). A clip-and-wedge leveling system costs about $0.15-0.30 per clip but saves hours of frustration.

5. Don't grout too soon. Wait at least 24 hours after setting tiles before grouting. In humid conditions or for large-format tiles with thick thinset, wait 48 hours. Grouting too early can shift tiles or trap moisture.

6. Seal your grout. Unless you're using epoxy grout, seal cement-based grout 48-72 hours after application. Unsealed grout absorbs stains and moisture, leading to discoloration and potential mold growth. Reseal annually in wet areas.

7. Order extra tiles. Beyond your calculated waste, order 3-5 extra tiles for future repairs. Tile dye lots vary between production runs, so a tile purchased a year later won't match exactly. Store them flat in a dry area.

Grout Coverage Calculation Guide

Grout coverage is one of the trickiest calculations in tile work. It depends on tile dimensions, grout joint width, and tile thickness. Here is the formula the pros use, which is the same one I into this calculator.

Grout (lbs/sq ft) = ((TileW + TileH) / (TileW x TileH)) x GapWidth x TileDepth x 1.87 Where all dimensions are in inches, and 1.87 is the density factor for sanded grout

For unsanded grout (used with gaps under 1/8 inch), the density factor drops to about 1.65. Epoxy grout uses a similar calculation but typically requires about 15% more material due to its different consistency and application technique.

Understanding Tile PEI Ratings

PEI (Porcelain Enamel Institute) ratings classify tile durability. This matters because using a low-rated tile in a high-traffic area leads to premature wear. Here is the scale:

  • PEI 0: Wall tile only. No foot traffic.
  • PEI 1: Very light traffic. Bathrooms with bare or stockinged feet only.
  • PEI 2: Light traffic. Residential bathrooms and bedrooms.
  • PEI 3: Moderate traffic. All residential areas. Most common rating.
  • PEI 4: Heavy traffic. Residential and light commercial (offices, restaurants).
  • PEI 5: Extra heavy traffic. Commercial, industrial, and exterior applications.

For kitchens and entryways, I recommend PEI 3 or higher. For commercial spaces, always use PEI 4 or 5. We've seen PEI 2 tiles in kitchen floors develop visible wear paths within two years.

Tile Layout Starting Points

Where you start your tile layout determines how the pattern looks and where cuts fall. The standard approach is to find the center of the room and snap chalk lines. Start laying tiles at the intersection and work toward the walls. This ensures cut tiles at the edges are roughly equal on opposite sides.

, I've found exceptions. In a narrow hallway, starting from the most visible wall and working toward a closet or less visible area gives a cleaner look. For bathrooms, start at the bathtub or shower curb because that joint is the most visible. In kitchens, align to the most prominent line of cabinets.

Frequently Asked Questions

Common questions about tile estimation, patterns, and installation.

How many tiles do I need for a 10x10 room?+

For a 10x10 foot room (100 sq ft) using 12x12 inch tiles, you need exactly 100 tiles for the base area. With standard 10% waste for straight lay, order 110 tiles (about 10 boxes of 12). For diagonal pattern, order 115 tiles. Always round up to complete boxes.

What waste percentage should I use for tile?+

Standard waste: 10% for straight/grid and brick/offset patterns, 15% for diagonal, 20% for herringbone. Add an extra 5% if you have many obstacles, narrow cuts, or if the room has alcoves. I've found that first-time DIYers should add an extra 5% on top of the standard to account for breakage during cutting.

How much grout do I need per square foot?+

Grout coverage varies dramatically by tile size and gap width. For 12x12 tiles with 1/8" grout lines, you need about 0.04 lbs per square foot. For 3x6 subway tile with 1/8" lines, it jumps to 0.15 lbs per square foot. Use the calculator above for exact amounts based on your specific configuration.

Should I use sanded or unsanded grout?+

Use sanded grout for joints wider than 1/8 inch. Use unsanded grout for joints 1/8 inch or narrower, and always for polished stone tiles where sand could scratch the surface. For wet areas like showers, epoxy grout is the best choice but costs 3-4x more and is harder to apply.

Can I tile over existing tile?+

Yes, if the existing tile is firmly bonded, level, and structurally sound. Clean thoroughly, apply a bonding primer, and use modified thinset. The combined tile height will raise your floor level, so you'll need transition strips at doorways. I don't recommend this for wet areas.

What size tile makes a small room look bigger?+

Larger tiles (18x18 or 24x24) with thin grout lines make small rooms appear bigger because there are fewer grout lines to break up the visual space. Light colors also help. Diagonal layouts create the illusion of more space. I tested this in a 5x8 bathroom and the difference between 6x6 and 18x18 tiles was dramatic.

How do I calculate tiles for a herringbone pattern?+

Calculate the base number of tiles needed for the area, then add 20% for herringbone waste. The interlocking V-pattern creates angled cuts at every wall edge, which is where the extra waste comes from. Use rectangular tiles (like 3x6 or 6x24) for herringbone. Square tiles can't form a true herringbone.

How many tiles come in a box?+

It varies by size and manufacturer. Common quantities: 12x12 tiles come 12-15 per box (12-15 sq ft), 6x6 tiles come 40-44 per box (10-11 sq ft), 18x18 tiles come 6 per box (13.5 sq ft), 24x24 tiles come 4 per box (16 sq ft). Always check the box label for exact coverage.

Is this calculator accurate for metric measurements?+

This calculator uses feet and inches as input. For metric, convert before entering: 1 meter = 3.281 feet, 1 cm = 0.394 inches. The math is the same regardless of unit system. I plan to add native metric input in a future update.

What is the cheapest tile per square foot?+

Basic ceramic tile starts at $0.50 per square foot. Peel-and-stick vinyl tile is even cheaper at $0.30-0.80 per sq ft but isn't real tile. For real tile on a budget, look for 12x12 or 12x24 ceramic in neutral colors. Home improvement store clearance sections often have quality tile under $1/sq ft.
