How CDs Work

A certificate of deposit is one of the simplest financial products available. You deposit a fixed amount of money at a bank or credit union for a predetermined period, and in exchange, the institution pays you a guaranteed interest rate. When the CD matures (reaches the end of its term), you receive your original deposit plus all earned interest.

The guaranteed return is the primary appeal. Unlike stocks, bonds, or even high-yield savings accounts (which can change rates at any time), a CD locks in your rate for the entire term. If you open a 5-year CD at 4.75% today, you will earn 4.75% for all five years regardless of what happens to market interest rates.

The tradeoff is liquidity. Your money is committed for the term. Accessing it early typically incurs a penalty that can reduce or eliminate your earnings. This makes CDs best suited for money you know you will not need during the term.

CD Mechanics Step by Step

1. You deposit a minimum amount (typically $500 to $1,000, though some CDs accept $0 minimum).

2. The bank pays interest at the stated APY, compounded according to the account terms.

3. Interest accrues throughout the term. Some CDs pay interest monthly or quarterly to a linked account; others compound it internally.

4. At maturity, you have a grace period (usually 7 to 14 days) to withdraw, renew, or change terms.

5. If you do nothing, most banks auto-renew the CD at the current rate for the same term.

APY vs APR Explained

These two acronyms look similar but represent different things, and confusing them can lead to incorrect return expectations.

APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is the simple interest rate without compounding. If a CD has a 4.75% APR with monthly compounding, the actual annual return is slightly higher than 4.75% because interest earned in one month begins earning its own interest in subsequent months.

APY (Annual Percentage Yield) accounts for compounding and represents the real annual return. A 4.75% APR with daily compounding produces an APY of approximately 4.87%. Banks are required by the Truth in Savings Act to advertise CDs using APY, which makes comparison shopping straightforward.

Compounding Frequency Explained

Compounding is the process where interest earns interest. The frequency determines how often accrued interest is added to your principal, creating a new, larger base for future interest calculations.

With daily compounding, interest is calculated and added to the balance every day. After day one on a $10,000 CD at 5% APR, you earn $1.37 in interest. On day two, you earn interest on $10,001.37. The effect is small per day but compounds meaningfully over long terms.

With monthly compounding, interest is calculated once per month. After month one on that same $10,000 CD at 5% APR, you earn $41.67 in interest. Month two earns interest on $10,041.67. The fewer compounding periods mean slightly less total return compared to daily compounding.

CD Laddering Strategy in Depth

CD laddering is the most widely recommended strategy for CD investors, and for good reason. It solves the basic tension between wanting higher rates (longer terms) and needing access to your money (shorter terms).

How a Basic 5-Year Ladder Works

Start with $50,000. Split it evenly into five CDs:

CD 1: $10,000 in a 1-year CD at 4.50%

CD 2: $10,000 in a 2-year CD at 4.65%

CD 3: $10,000 in a 3-year CD at 4.75%

CD 4: $10,000 in a 4-year CD at 4.80%

CD 5: $10,000 in a 5-year CD at 4.85%

After year one, CD 1 matures. You reinvest it into a new 5-year CD at whatever the current 5-year rate is. Now all your CDs are 5-year CDs, but one matures every year. You get the benefit of 5-year rates with annual liquidity.

The ladder self-maintains once established. Every year, the shortest CD matures and gets rolled into a new 5-year CD. If rates have risen, you benefit because you are reinvesting at higher rates. If rates have fallen, only one-fifth of your money reinvests at the lower rate while the rest continues earning the older, higher rates.

Variations include a 3-year ladder (more frequent access, slightly lower rates), a mini-ladder with 3-month, 6-month, and 12-month CDs (maximum liquidity), or a barbell strategy with half in short-term and half in long-term CDs (balancing yield and access).

I use a 5-year ladder for my own emergency reserves. The annual maturity cycle means I always have a CD coming due within 12 months, providing a dependable access point without breaking a CD early. The strategy works best in stable or rising rate environments and slightly underperforms a single long-term CD when rates are falling sharply.

This is the CD's greatest advantage: rate certainty.

A penalty that exceeds the interest earned results in a net loss. This can happen when you withdraw very early in the term. For example, withdrawing a 12-month CD after only 2 months with a 6-month interest penalty means you lose 4 months of interest from your principal. The penalty comes out of your original deposit, not just the interest earned.

When does it make sense to break a CD early? If you can reinvest at a significantly higher rate, the math may favor the penalty. If a 1-year CD at 3% has 9 months remaining, and new 1-year CDs pay 5%, breaking the old CD (even with a 3-month penalty) and opening a new one at the higher rate can produce more total interest. Run the numbers using the penalty calculator above before deciding.

Brokered CDs Explained

A brokered CD is purchased through a brokerage account (Fidelity, Schwab, Vanguard, etc.) rather than directly from a bank. The CD itself is still issued by an FDIC-insured bank, but the purchase and management happen through the brokerage platform.

Brokered CDs offer several advantages. Rates are often higher because brokerages aggregate demand and negotiate institutional rates. You can access CDs from hundreds of banks through a single brokerage account, making comparison shopping effortless. And brokered CDs can be sold on the secondary market before maturity, avoiding early withdrawal penalties entirely.

The secondary market feature comes with a caveat: the sale price depends on current interest rates. If rates have risen since you bought the CD, its market value drops (because newer CDs pay more). If rates have fallen, your CD is worth more (because it pays above-market rates). This is the same interest rate risk that affects bonds.

For investors with brokerage accounts, brokered CDs are often the better choice. The rate advantage, access to many issuers, and secondary market liquidity outweigh the minor learning curve. For those who prefer simplicity, bank CDs purchased directly are perfectly fine.

FDIC Insurance Limits

FDIC insurance protects your CD deposits if the bank fails. The standard coverage limit is $250,000 per depositor, per institution, per ownership category. Understanding the ownership category detail is important for savers with large balances.

FDIC Coverage Categories

Joint Account (two co-owners)$500,000 per bank ($250K each)
Revocable Trust (per beneficiary)$250,000 per beneficiary per bank
IRA / Retirement Account$250,000 per bank (separate from individual)

A married couple can insure $1,000,000 at a single bank: $250,000 in Spouse A's individual account, $250,000 in Spouse B's individual account, and $500,000 in a joint account. Adding IRA accounts and revocable trust designations can extend coverage further.

For amounts exceeding FDIC limits, spread deposits across multiple banks. CD laddering across different banks combines the rate strategy with insurance maximization. Credit union CDs (called share certificates) are insured by NCUA under the same $250,000 per depositor limit.

Tax Implications of CD Earnings

CD interest is taxed as ordinary income at your marginal tax rate. This is the same treatment as savings account interest, money market interest, and bond interest. The bank reports your interest on Form 1099-INT.

For multi-year CDs, there is an important timing detail: interest is taxable in the year it accrues, not just the year the CD matures. A 5-year CD earns interest every year, and you owe taxes on that interest each year, even if you do not receive a payout until maturity. The bank reports the annual accrual to the IRS, and you report it on your tax return.

At a 24% federal marginal rate plus 5% state tax, a 5.00% CD effectively yields about 3.55% after taxes. This after-tax return still beats inflation in most years, preserving purchasing power. For higher-income earners in the 32% or 35% federal brackets, the after-tax yield compresses further.

Tax-advantaged accounts change the equation. CDs held inside a traditional IRA grow tax-deferred, meaning you owe no taxes until withdrawal. CDs in a Roth IRA grow tax-free permanently. If you are building a CD ladder for retirement savings, holding it inside an IRA can significantly improve your effective return.

Frequently Asked Questions

Is this CD account calculator free to use?
Yes. This calculator is completely free with no registration, no account creation, and no email required. All calculations run in your browser.
What is the best CD term right now in 2026?
As of March 2026, 6-month to 12-month CDs offer some of the best rates relative to their terms, with top rates between 4.50% and 4.80%. If you believe rates will continue falling, locking in a 2 to 3-year CD at current rates protects your return. A CD ladder across multiple terms hedges your bet regardless of rate direction.
How much can I earn on a $10,000 CD?
At a 4.75% APY with monthly compounding for 12 months, a $10,000 CD earns approximately $486 in interest for a final balance of $10,486. Over 5 years at the same rate, total interest earned is approximately $2,632 for a final balance of $12,632.
Are CDs worth it compared to Treasury bills?
Both are low-risk savings vehicles. Treasury bills (T-bills) are backed by the federal government and exempt from state income tax, which can be advantageous for residents of high-tax states. CDs offer fixed terms and FDIC insurance. The choice depends on state tax rates, current yields, and your preference for bank vs government securities.
Can I lose money on a CD?
You can lose a portion of your principal if you withdraw early and the penalty exceeds the interest earned. Under normal circumstances (holding to maturity), you cannot lose money on an FDIC-insured CD. Even if the bank fails, FDIC insurance covers up to $250,000 per depositor per institution.
What is a bump-up CD?
A bump-up (or step-up) CD allows you to increase your rate once or twice during the term if market rates rise. The initial rate is typically lower than a standard CD of the same term. Bump-up CDs are useful when you expect rates to rise but still want the security of a fixed-term deposit.
Do I report CD interest on my taxes if I do not withdraw it?
Yes. CD interest is taxable in the year it accrues, regardless of whether you withdraw it or the CD has matured. Your bank reports annual interest to the IRS on Form 1099-INT. You include this amount as ordinary income on your tax return.
Is a no-penalty CD a good option?
No-penalty CDs combine the rate lock of a CD with the flexibility of a savings account. The rate is usually slightly lower than a standard CD (0.1% to 0.3% less). They work well for short-term savings where you want rate certainty but may need early access. For longer terms, standard CDs typically offer better returns.
What is the minimum deposit for a CD?
Minimum deposits vary by institution. Many online banks offer CDs with $0 to $500 minimums. Traditional banks may require $1,000 to $5,000. Jumbo CDs (with slightly higher rates) typically require $50,000 to $100,000 minimums. Brokered CDs through major brokerages often start at $1,000.
Should I put my emergency fund in CDs?
A full emergency fund (3 to 6 months of expenses) should remain in a high-yield savings account for immediate access. However, a CD ladder can serve as a secondary emergency reserve. If you have built a 6-month fund in savings, additional reserves can go into a CD ladder for higher returns with staggered access points.
How does inflation affect CD returns?
Your real return on a CD is the APY minus the inflation rate. If your CD earns 4.75% and inflation is 3%, your real return is approximately 1.75%. CDs preserve purchasing power in moderate inflation environments but may not keep pace during periods of high inflation. I-bonds and TIPS are alternatives that directly index to inflation.
Can I hold CDs in an IRA?
Yes. IRA CDs are available at most banks and brokerages. In a traditional IRA, CD interest grows tax-deferred until withdrawal. In a Roth IRA, CD interest grows completely tax-free. Holding CDs in a tax-advantaged account eliminates the annual tax drag on your returns.
