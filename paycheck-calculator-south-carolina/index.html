How South Carolina's State Income Tax Works in 2026

I have tracked South Carolina's tax reform closely over the past several years, and the state has made real progress in simplifying its income tax system. What used to be a seven-bracket progressive structure has been consolidated into just three brackets for 2026. The first $3,640 of taxable income is completely tax-free. Income from $3,641 to $18,230 is taxed at 3%. Everything above $18,230 is taxed at 6%.

The top rate of 6% is itself the result of a recent reduction. South Carolina's top marginal rate was 7% as recently as 2022, and it has been steadily lowered through legislative action. A budget proviso reduced the rate from 6.2% to 6% effective July 2025. This temporary reduction is currently scheduled to revert to 6.2% on July 1, 2026, but there is strong legislative momentum to make the lower rate permanent or reduce it further.

South Carolina does not tax Social Security benefits at the state level. Retirees also benefit from a retirement income deduction of up to $3,000 for those under 65 and up to $10,000 for taxpayers age 65 and older. These provisions, combined with the state's moderate overall tax burden and relatively low cost of living, have made South Carolina one of the most popular retirement destinations in the Southeast.

There are no local income taxes anywhere in South Carolina. The state income tax is the only income-based tax at the state or local level. This is a meaningful simplification compared to states like Ohio, Pennsylvania, and Alabama where local income or occupational taxes can add 1% to 3% on top of state taxes.

South Carolina Standard Deduction and Dependent Exemptions

South Carolina's standard deduction for 2026 is $8,350 for single filers and $16,700 for married filing jointly. These amounts are applied before the tax brackets calculate your state tax liability. In addition, South Carolina allows a dependent exemption of $4,930 per qualifying dependent. A married couple with two children would receive a combined $26,560 reduction in taxable income ($16,700 standard deduction plus $9,860 in dependent exemptions).

South Carolina also allows itemized deductions, and taxpayers can choose whichever method provides the larger benefit. Common itemized deductions include mortgage interest, charitable contributions, and medical expenses exceeding the threshold. The state generally conforms to federal itemized deduction rules with some modifications.

FICA Taxes and How They Affect Your South Carolina Paycheck

FICA taxes apply to every worker in every state. Social Security tax is 6.2% of your wages up to the $168,600 wage base for 2026. Once your earnings exceed that amount, Social Security withholding stops for the remainder of the year. Medicare tax is 1.45% of all wages with no cap. An additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies to earnings above $200,000 for single filers ($250,000 for married filing jointly).

Your employer matches the 6.2% Social Security and 1.45% Medicare contributions. This employer share does not appear on your paycheck and is a separate business expense. The combined employee-employer FICA rate is 15.3% on wages up to the Social Security cap.

South Carolina does not impose any state disability insurance (SDI) or paid family leave (PFL) payroll taxes. Your South Carolina paycheck will not have the SDI or PFL deductions that appear on paychecks in California, New York, New Jersey, Massachusetts, Washington, and several other states. This absence of additional payroll taxes means more of your gross pay reaches your bank account compared to those states.

Pre-Tax Deductions and Their Impact on Your South Carolina Paycheck

Pre-tax deductions are one of the most effective ways to reduce your total tax burden. When you contribute to a traditional 401(k), the contribution is deducted from your gross pay before federal and state income taxes are calculated. In the 22% federal bracket with a 6% South Carolina state rate, every dollar contributed to a 401(k) saves you $0.28 in combined income taxes.

Health insurance premiums through an employer plan are typically pre-tax, reducing your taxable income for all tax types including Social Security and Medicare. Health Savings Accounts (HSAs) provide triple tax benefits. Contributions are pre-tax for both federal and South Carolina purposes, the funds grow tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. The 2026 HSA limits are $4,300 for individual coverage and $8,550 for family coverage.

I encourage South Carolina workers to take full advantage of pre-tax options. The 2026 401(k) contribution limit is $23,500 ($31,000 for those 50 and older). A worker earning $75,000 who contributes 10% to a 401(k) saves approximately $2,100 in annual income taxes. Over a 30-year career, the compounding growth of those pre-tax savings can add hundreds of thousands of dollars to retirement wealth.

South Carolina vs. Neighboring States for Take-Home Pay

When comparing South Carolina to its neighbors, the tax picture varies depending on income level. North Carolina has a flat 4.5% state income tax rate for 2026. For workers earning $50,000 to $70,000, the effective rates in both states are similar. Above $100,000, North Carolina's flat rate starts to look more favorable than South Carolina's 6% top bracket.

Georgia charges 5.49% as its top rate in 2026, which is slightly lower than South Carolina's 6%. However, Georgia reaches its top bracket faster, so middle-income earners may actually pay slightly more in Georgia depending on their deductions.

Tennessee and Florida have no state income tax, making them the clear winners for maximizing take-home pay. However, Tennessee compensates with high sales taxes (up to 9.75%), and Florida's property taxes and insurance costs can be significantly higher than South Carolina's. The overall cost-of-living comparison often narrows the gap between these states.

Virginia's top rate is 5.75%, applied to income above $17,000. When comparing like-for-like salaries, South Carolina and Virginia produce similar effective tax rates for most income levels, though Virginia's higher cost of living in the Northern Virginia and Hampton Roads areas makes South Carolina the better value for the same nominal salary.

Cost of Living in South Carolina

South Carolina's cost of living is approximately 10% to 15% below the national average, which amplifies the value of your take-home pay. The median home price statewide is roughly $310,000, compared to the national median of approximately $420,000. In the Charleston metro area, which has seen significant price appreciation, the median is closer to $400,000. Greenville averages approximately $300,000, Columbia approximately $250,000, and Myrtle Beach approximately $290,000.

Rent in South Carolina is also affordable relative to national averages. A one-bedroom apartment in Charleston averages around $1,500 per month due to the city's popularity. In Greenville, the average is approximately $1,100. In Columbia, which benefits from state government and university employment, the average is roughly $950. Smaller cities and rural areas offer even lower rents.

South Carolina's sales tax is 6% at the state level, with local option taxes adding up to 3% in some jurisdictions. The combined rate can reach 9% in certain areas, which is above average. Property taxes in South Carolina are relatively low, with an effective rate of approximately 0.56% of assessed value for owner-occupied homes due to the 4% assessment ratio applied to primary residences.

The combination of moderate state income taxes, low property taxes on primary residences, affordable housing, and no local income taxes makes South Carolina a financially attractive state for workers at most income levels. The Greenville-Spartanburg corridor in particular has attracted manufacturing and corporate investment, creating higher-paying jobs in a low-cost-of-living environment.

Strategies to increase Your South Carolina Take-Home Pay

There are several practical approaches to putting more money in your pocket each pay period.

  • Max out your employer-sponsored 401(k) at $23,500 per year ($31,000 if 50 or older). The combined federal and state tax savings at the 22% and 6% rates equal $6,580 annually on the full contribution.
  • Contribute to an HSA if enrolled in a high-deductible health plan. The triple tax benefit makes HSAs the most tax-fast savings vehicle available to most workers.
  • Claim all eligible dependent exemptions on your SC withholding form. Each dependent reduces your state taxable income by $4,930, saving you up to $296 per dependent at the 6% rate.
  • Review your W-4 form for accuracy. If you consistently get large federal refunds, you are over-withholding and should adjust to keep more money in each paycheck.
  • If you are approaching the Social Security wage cap ($168,600), anticipate the per-paycheck boost that comes when withholding stops. Plan to direct this extra cash toward savings rather than spending.

South Carolina Payroll Calendar and Pay Frequency

Your pay frequency determines the per-paycheck amount but not your annual take-home pay. Weekly pay produces 52 paychecks, bi-weekly produces 26, semi-monthly produces 24, and monthly produces 12. South Carolina does not mandate a specific pay frequency, but the state does require employers to pay employees on regular paydays and within a reasonable time after work is performed.

Most private-sector employers in South Carolina use bi-weekly or semi-monthly schedules. State government employees are paid on a semi-monthly basis. Bi-weekly pay means two months each year will have three paychecks, providing a budgeting opportunity for additional savings or debt payoff.

Special Considerations for High-Income South Carolina Earners

South Carolina's 6% top bracket begins at $18,230 of taxable income, which means virtually all working adults pay the top rate on the majority of their earnings. This flat-rate behavior at moderate income levels means that South Carolina's tax system functions more like a flat tax with a small exemption than a truly progressive system.

High earners benefit from the Social Security wage cap ($168,600) and should expect a per-paycheck increase after hitting the cap. The Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% applies to wages above $200,000 and begins being withheld once your year-to-date wages exceed that threshold.

South Carolina does not have any additional surcharges or millionaire taxes. The 6% rate is the maximum regardless of income level. This makes South Carolina's top rate lower than California (13.3%), New York (10.9%), New Jersey (10.75%), and many other high-tax states, which is one reason the state has attracted both retirees and high-earning professionals.

South Carolina Tax Credits and Additional Benefits

South Carolina offers several tax credits that can reduce your state tax liability. The two-wage earner credit allows married couples filing jointly to deduct the lesser of $2,120 or the earnings of the lower-earning spouse from their state taxable income. This prevents the marriage penalty that can occur when two working spouses combine their incomes into higher brackets.

The child and dependent care credit allows a credit of up to 7% of eligible expenses for childcare needed to allow the taxpayer to work. South Carolina also offers tax credits for tuition payments to eligible educational institutions, contributions to the Palmetto Fellows and LIFE scholarship programs, and rehabilitation of historic structures.

For taxpayers age 65 and older, South Carolina provides a retirement income deduction of up to $10,000. Combined with the Social Security exemption and the standard deduction, many retirees in South Carolina pay minimal or no state income tax. This is a significant advantage over states like Vermont, Connecticut, and Minnesota that tax most retirement income.

Military and Federal Employee Benefits in South Carolina

South Carolina has a substantial military presence, with major installations including Fort Jackson in Columbia, Joint Base Charleston, Parris Island Marine Corps Recruit Depot, and Shaw Air Force Base. Active-duty military personnel stationed in South Carolina are subject to state income tax on their pay if they are legal residents of the state. Non-resident military personnel stationed in South Carolina are generally exempt from SC state tax on their military income under the Servicemembers Civil Relief Act.

Military retirement pay qualifies for the retirement income deduction. For retirees under 65, the deduction is $3,000. For those 65 and older, it is $10,000. Additionally, military survivors' benefits and disability payments are typically exempt from both federal and state income tax.

Federal civilian employees at the many military bases and government facilities in South Carolina pay state income tax on their earnings. These workers benefit from the same standard deduction and dependent exemptions as all other South Carolina residents.

Understanding Your South Carolina Pay Stub

Your pay stub contains several deduction categories. Federal income tax withholding is based on your W-4 form. South Carolina state income tax withholding is calculated based on the SC W-4 form you filed with your employer. Social Security appears as 6.2% of gross wages up to the annual cap. Medicare appears as 1.45% of all gross wages, with the additional 0.9% applied once year-to-date wages exceed $200,000.

Pre-tax deductions for 401(k), health insurance, and HSA should reduce the wages subject to income tax. Verify that your taxable wages on the pay stub are lower than your gross wages by the total of your pre-tax deductions. If the numbers do not match, your employer may be incorrectly calculating your withholding.

Common Paycheck Mistakes in South Carolina

Several common errors can cost South Carolina workers money over time. Filing incorrect withholding information on the SC W-4 is the most frequent issue. If you never submitted a state withholding form, your employer defaults to single with zero exemptions, resulting in higher withholding than necessary.

Failing to update your withholding after major life events is another common problem. Marriage, divorce, the birth of a child, or a significant income change should all trigger a review of your W-4 and SC W-4 forms. Each dependent exemption ($4,930) reduces your state taxable income, so adding a new dependent without updating your withholding means you are overpaying throughout the year.

Not taking advantage of available pre-tax deductions is a missed opportunity. If your employer offers a 401(k) match, failing to contribute at least enough to receive the full match is leaving free money on the table. The tax savings from pre-tax contributions provide an additional incentive to participate.

Does South Carolina have local or city income taxes?▾

This simplifies the tax picture compared to states like Alabama or Ohio where local occupational taxes can add 1% to 2% on top of state taxes. Your only payroll-based taxes in South Carolina are federal income tax, SC state income tax, and FICA.
How is overtime taxed in South Carolina?▾
Overtime pay is taxed at the same rates as regular wages. There is no special tax rate for overtime in South Carolina or at the federal level. Higher pay during overtime periods may push your per-paycheck income into higher withholding calculations, but the actual tax owed is determined on your annual return. What is the standard deduction in South Carolina?▾

The state also provides a personal exemption of $4,930 per person and a dependent exemption of $4,930 per qualifying dependent. Understanding Your Effective Tax Rate in South Carolina

One of the most useful outputs from this calculator is your effective tax rate, which is your total taxes paid divided by your gross income. This number is always lower than your marginal tax bracket because of the progressive nature of both the federal and state tax systems and the various deductions that reduce your taxable income.

A single South Carolina worker earning $70,000 per year has a marginal federal bracket of 22% and a marginal SC bracket of 6%. But the effective rates are much lower. After accounting for the federal standard deduction ($15,000), the SC standard deduction ($8,350), and the progressive bracket structure, the effective federal rate is approximately 11% to 12%, and the effective SC rate is approximately 4% to 4.5%. The combined effective rate including FICA is roughly 24% to 26%, meaning approximately 74 to 76 cents of every dollar earned reaches the worker's bank account before pre-tax deductions.

Understanding the gap between marginal and effective rates prevents unnecessary alarm when people hear about tax brackets. When someone says South Carolina has a 6% top rate, that rate only applies to income above $18,230 of taxable income (after subtracting the standard deduction). The first $3,640 of taxable income is completely tax-free, and the next $14,590 is taxed at only 3%. For most workers, the effective state rate falls between 3.5% and 5%.

Self-Employment Tax Considerations for South Carolina Workers

Self-employed individuals in South Carolina face the full 15.3% self-employment tax (12.4% Social Security plus 2.9% Medicare) on net self-employment income, in addition to federal and state income tax. This double FICA burden is the biggest financial surprise for people transitioning from traditional employment to freelancing or starting a business.

On $30,000 of net self-employment income, the self-employment tax alone is approximately $4,590. Add federal income tax of roughly $3,600 (at the 12% bracket) and South Carolina state tax of approximately $1,300, and the total tax on that $30,000 is roughly $9,490, an effective rate of about 31.6%. The 50% self-employment tax deduction on the federal return provides some relief, reducing your federal and SC adjusted gross income by half the self-employment tax amount.

South Carolina requires estimated quarterly tax payments if you expect to owe $100 or more in state tax for the year. The quarterly due dates follow the federal schedule: April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15. Failure to make estimated payments can result in penalties and interest charges.

South Carolina Property Tax and Its Interaction with Income

While property tax is not a paycheck deduction, understanding it helps complete the financial picture for South Carolina workers. The state applies a 4% assessment ratio to owner-occupied primary residences, which means a home valued at $300,000 is assessed at $12,000. The effective property tax rate on the full market value is approximately 0.56%, which is among the lowest in the country.

Non-owner-occupied property (rental, commercial, second homes) is assessed at 6%, resulting in a higher effective rate. This differential creates a meaningful tax advantage for homeowners in South Carolina compared to renters, which is worth factoring into your housing decision when evaluating total take-home pay and living expenses.

For workers who itemize deductions on their federal return, South Carolina property taxes are deductible up to the $10,000 SALT cap. However, most South Carolina homeowners pay well under $10,000 in combined state and local taxes, so the SALT cap is less of a constraint here than in high-tax states like New York, New Jersey, or California.

Filing Status and Its Impact on South Carolina Taxes

Your filing status affects both your federal and South Carolina state tax calculations. Single filers receive the smallest standard deduction and the narrowest brackets. Married Filing Jointly provides the largest standard deduction ($16,700 for SC) and generally produces the lowest combined tax for married couples, particularly when there is a significant income disparity between spouses.

Head of Household status is available to unmarried individuals who provide more than half the financial support for a qualifying dependent. This status provides a standard deduction between single and married amounts and slightly wider federal brackets. Head of Household filers in South Carolina receive meaningful tax savings compared to the Single filing status.

The two-wage earner credit in South Carolina is an additional benefit for married couples where both spouses work. This credit allows a deduction of up to $2,120 from state taxable income, helping to mitigate the marriage penalty that can occur when two working spouses combine their incomes. This is a South Carolina-specific benefit that many other states do not provide.

Paycheck Planning for Major Life Events

Major life events can significantly change your paycheck calculations. Marriage typically changes your filing status to Married Filing Jointly, which doubles the standard deduction and widens the federal and state tax brackets. Update your W-4 and SC W-4 promptly after marriage to adjust your withholding.

The birth or adoption of a child adds a dependent exemption of $4,930 to your South Carolina return, saving up to $296 in state tax at the 6% rate. The federal Child Tax Credit of up to $2,000 per child provides additional relief. Update your W-4 after adding a dependent to receive the benefit throughout the year rather than waiting for a larger refund at tax time.

Buying a home in South Carolina creates potential deductions for mortgage interest and property taxes if you itemize. However, with South Carolina's generous standard deduction of $8,350 (single) or $16,700 (married), many homeowners still benefit more from the standard deduction. Run the numbers both ways to determine which approach saves you more.

Job changes require attention to multiple factors. Review the Social Security wage cap ($168,600), as your new employer will restart withholding from zero, potentially causing over-withholding if your combined wages from both employers exceed the cap. Also verify that your W-4 and SC W-4 are filed with the new employer and that pre-tax deductions are set up correctly from the first paycheck.

South Carolina Workers' Compensation and Benefits

Understanding your employer-provided benefits complements the paycheck calculation by showing the full value of your compensation package. South Carolina requires employers with four or more employees to carry workers' compensation insurance. This is an employer-paid benefit that does not appear on your paycheck but provides wage replacement and medical coverage if you are injured on the job.

South Carolina does not mandate paid sick leave, paid family leave, or employer-provided health insurance (beyond the federal ACA requirements for employers with 50 or more full-time employees). Benefits beyond the legal minimums vary widely by employer and industry. The healthcare and technology sectors typically offer the most complete benefits packages, while hospitality and retail often provide fewer benefits.

When evaluating a job offer in South Carolina, consider the total compensation package including health insurance (employer premium contribution), retirement plan match, paid time off, and other benefits. An employer that pays $2,000 per month toward your health insurance premium is effectively adding $24,000 per year to your compensation, though this does not appear in your gross pay calculation.

Biweekly vs. Semi-Monthly Pay in South Carolina

Understanding the practical difference between these two common pay frequencies helps with budgeting. Biweekly pay means 26 paychecks per year, with each paycheck covering exactly two weeks. The payment day is always the same day of the week. Two months per year will have three paychecks instead of two, providing a natural savings opportunity.

Semi-monthly pay means 24 paychecks per year, typically paid on the 1st and 15th or the 15th and last day of the month. Each paycheck is slightly larger than biweekly (because the same annual salary is divided by 24 instead of 26), and the schedule aligns neatly with monthly bills and rent payments.

The per-paycheck tax calculations differ between these frequencies because the same annual tax is spread across a different number of payments. Biweekly paychecks show slightly lower per-period federal and state tax withholding, but the annual total is the same regardless of frequency. South Carolina for Remote Workers and Relocations

South Carolina has become an increasingly popular destination for remote workers relocating from higher-cost states. The combination of a 6% top income tax rate (compared to 13.3% in California or 10.9% in New York), no local income taxes, low property taxes, and affordable housing creates a compelling financial case for workers whose employment is not tied to a specific geographic office.

A remote worker earning $120,000 who relocates from New York City to Charleston saves approximately $6,000 to $8,000 per year in state and city income taxes alone, even after accounting for South Carolina's 6% tax. When you factor in the difference in housing costs (a one-bedroom apartment that costs $3,500 in Manhattan might cost $1,500 in Charleston), the total annual savings can exceed $30,000.

South Carolina taxes remote workers based on where the work is physically performed, not where the employer is located. If you live and work in South Carolina for a New York employer, South Carolina taxes that income and New York generally does not (though New York has been known to assert jurisdiction in certain cases through its convenience-of-the-employer rule). Verify your specific situation with a tax professional, especially if your employer is in a state with aggressive non-resident taxing policies.

The Greenville and Charleston metro areas have been the primary beneficiaries of this remote worker migration. Both cities offer walkable downtowns, access to nature, growing food and cultural scenes, and a lower pace of life compared to major coastal metros. The influx of higher-income remote workers has pushed up housing costs in these areas, but they remain significantly more affordable than the cities most relocators are leaving.

For remote workers evaluating South Carolina specifically, the Greenville area offers the best value. Median home prices are lower than Charleston, traffic congestion is minimal compared to the coast, and the growing tech and manufacturing sectors provide networking and career opportunities. The Spartanburg area, adjacent to Greenville, is even more affordable and has seen increasing investment in arts, dining, and walkable urban spaces.

South Carolina's weather is another draw for relocators from Northern states. Average winter temperatures in Charleston range from 40 to 60 degrees, and Greenville averages 35 to 55 degrees. The warmer climate reduces heating costs and enables year-round outdoor activities, which contributes to quality of life for workers whose income allows location flexibility.

Tax Planning Strategies for South Carolina Residents

South Carolina offers several tax-friendly provisions that residents should factor into their paycheck planning. The state allows a full deduction for contributions to traditional retirement accounts, which directly reduces your taxable income on the SC return. If you are over 65, you may also qualify for an additional retirement income deduction of up to $15,000. I find that many workers overlook these deductions and end up withholding more than necessary throughout the year.

Another consideration is the tuition tax credit. South Carolina provides a credit for contributions to 529 college savings plans, which can offset your state tax liability dollar for dollar up to the annual limit. For families with children approaching college age, this is one of the most effective ways to redirect money that would otherwise go to the state treasury. Planning these contributions in coordination with your payroll withholding ensures your paycheck reflects the true tax burden rather than an inflated estimate.

How South Carolina Compares to Neighboring States

South Carolina's top marginal rate of 6 percent sits in the middle of the pack among southeastern states. North Carolina uses a flat 4.5 percent rate, which is simpler but offers fewer bracket-based savings for lower earners. Georgia applies a graduated structure topping out at 5.49 percent, while Tennessee and Florida impose no state income tax at all. For workers living near the border of any of these states, the difference in annual take-home pay can be meaningful, particularly at higher salary levels.

Where South Carolina distinguishes itself is in property tax and overall cost of living. Housing costs in cities like Greenville, Columbia, and Charleston remain lower than comparable metros in North Carolina and Georgia. When I factor in the combined tax picture alongside living expenses, South Carolina often comes out ahead for workers earning between $50,000 and $100,000 annually. The paycheck calculator above helps you see the income tax piece, but the full comparison requires looking at property taxes, sales taxes, and local costs together.

Community Questions About South Carolina Paycheck Taxes

Q: Why is my South Carolina state tax lower than expected on my first paycheck?

A: South Carolina's first bracket ($0 to $3,640) is taxed at 0%, and the second bracket ($3,641 to $18,230) is taxed at only 3%. Early in the year, payroll systems withhold at a blended rate that accounts for these lower brackets. As the year progresses and your cumulative income enters the 6% bracket, per-paycheck withholding increases. Some employers use annualized withholding that smooths this out, but others use a percentage method that can cause variation.

Q: Does South Carolina tax military retirement pay?

A: South Carolina exempts up to $17,500 of military retirement pay from state income tax for retirees under age 65. For those 65 and older, the exemption increases to $30,000. These exemptions apply to all forms of retirement income, not just military pensions, making SC attractive for veterans and retirees in general.

Q: How do I calculate my South Carolina take-home pay if I also have a side gig?

A: Side gig income (1099) is subject to both self-employment tax (15.3% on 92.35% of net earnings) and regular income tax at both federal and SC state levels.
