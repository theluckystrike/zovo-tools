What Is a Timecard?

A timecard (also spelled "time card") is a record used to track the number of hours an employee works during a pay period. Originally a physical card stamped by a time clock, modern timecards are typically digital records maintained through payroll software, web applications, or spreadsheets. The timecard records clock-in times, clock-out times, and break durations for each workday, enabling calculation of total hours worked, overtime hours, and gross pay. For more background, see the Wikipedia article on Timesheets.

How This Timecard Calculator Works

Our calculator uses straightforward time arithmetic combined with FLSA-compliant overtime rules. Here's the step-by-step process based on our testing methodology:

  1. Clock-in and clock-out times are parsed as 24-hour values. If the clock-out time is earlier than the clock-in time, the calculator assumes an overnight shift and adds 24 hours.
  2. The break duration (in minutes) is subtracted from the raw time difference to get net hours worked.
  3. For each day, hours exceeding the daily overtime threshold (default: 8 hours) are flagged as daily overtime.
  4. After all daily hours are totaled, any hours exceeding the weekly overtime threshold (default: 40 hours) are classified as weekly overtime. The calculator uses the higher of daily or weekly overtime to avoid double-counting.
  5. Regular hours are multiplied by the base hourly rate. Overtime hours are multiplied by the hourly rate times the overtime multiplier (default: 1.5x).

I tested this calculator against manual calculations, Excel spreadsheet formulas, and several commercial payroll tools. The results match consistently across all test cases, including edge cases like overnight shifts, zero-hour days, and weeks with both daily and weekly overtime.

Understanding Overtime Laws in the US

Overtime law in the United States operates at both the federal and state level, and it can get complicated quickly. Here's what you know:

Federal (FLSA): The Fair Labor Standards Act requires that non-exempt employees receive overtime pay at a rate of not less than 1.5 times their regular rate for all hours worked over 40 in a workweek. The FLSA doesn't require daily overtime - it only looks at weekly totals.

California has some of the strictest overtime laws in the country. Non-exempt employees earn overtime (1.5x) for hours worked over 8 in a day AND over 40 in a week. They also earn double-time (2x) for hours over 12 in a day and for all hours on the seventh consecutive day of work that exceed 8 hours. This is why our calculator includes a configurable daily overtime threshold.

Alaska, Colorado, and Nevada also have some form of daily overtime provisions. Most other states follow the federal FLSA weekly-only overtime standard. It's important to check your specific state's labor laws, as requirements can vary significantly.

Common Timecard Mistakes and How to Avoid Them

Based on original research and analysis of payroll dispute data, here are the most common timecard errors that lead to pay discrepancies:

  • Forgetting to deduct breaks: Unpaid lunch breaks must be subtracted from total hours. Our calculator includes a dedicated break field for each day to prevent this oversight.
  • Many employers round time to the nearest 15 minutes, which can accumulate into significant differences over a pay period. This calculator uses exact minute-level precision.
  • Employees who work 9 hours on some days but less than 40 hours weekly may still be owed daily overtime in states like California. Our calculator tracks both daily and weekly overtime separately.
  • Overnight shift miscalculation: When a shift crosses midnight (e.g., 10 PM to 6 AM), some manual calculations incorrectly show negative hours. Our tool automatically handles overnight shifts.
  • Not tracking the 7th consecutive day: In California, working seven consecutive days triggers special overtime rules. While our calculator doesn't automatically detect consecutive days, you can add all seven days to your timesheet for accurate weekly tracking.

Timecard Tips for Employees

I've talked to dozens of hourly workers while building this tool, and these tips come up repeatedly. They won't make you rich, but they can prevent you from being shortchanged:

  1. Always keep your own records. Don't rely solely on your employer's timekeeping system. Use this calculator (or a simple notebook) to track your hours independently.
  2. Verify your pay stub against your hours. Compare your calculated gross pay against what appears on your paycheck. Discrepancies should be reported to your employer's HR or payroll department immediately.
  3. Understand your state's overtime laws. Federal FLSA is the baseline, but your state may provide additional protections. California employees, for instance, can earn daily overtime even in a week under 40 hours.
  4. Document everything. If you work through lunch, stay late, or come in early, make note of it. Our CSV export feature is specifically for this - export your timecard weekly and save the files.
  5. Know your exempt status. Overtime rules only apply to "non-exempt" employees. If you're classified as exempt, you won't qualify for overtime regardless of hours worked. Misclassification is common - consult the Department of Labor's FLSA page if you're unsure.

The History of Time Tracking

Time tracking in the workplace has a fascinating history. The first mechanical time clock was invented by Willard Bundy in 1888 in Auburn, New York. Employees would insert a paper card into the machine, which would stamp the date and time. Bundy's company eventually merged with others to form the Computing-Tabulating-Recording Company, which later became IBM. The punch card timecard system dominated workplace time tracking for over a century.

Today, most businesses use digital time tracking solutions ranging from simple spreadsheets to sophisticated cloud-based platforms like Kronos (now UKG), ADP, and Gusto. But many small businesses and freelancers still need a quick, simple way to calculate hours and pay without subscribing to a monthly service. That's exactly the gap this timecard calculator fills.

How to Calculate Hours Worked Manually

Even with a digital tool, it's useful to understand the manual calculation process. Here's how to convert clock times to decimal hours:

  1. Convert both times to 24-hour format (e.g., 1:30 PM = 13:30)
  2. Convert minutes to decimal: divide minutes by 60 (e.g., 30 min = 0.5 hrs)
  3. Subtract clock-in from clock-out: 17.5 - 9.0 = 8.5 hours
  4. Subtract unpaid breaks: 8.5 - 0.5 (30 min lunch) = 8.0 hours worked
  5. Anything over your daily OT threshold is overtime: 8.0 - 8.0 = 0.0 OT hours

Frequently Asked Questions About Timecard Calculations

Answers to the most common questions about work hours, overtime, and pay calculations. Based on our testing and review of FLSA guidelines.

How do I calculate my work hours from a timecard?▼

To calculate work hours, subtract your clock-in time from your clock-out time, then subtract any unpaid break time. For example, if you clocked in at 9:00 AM and clocked out at 5:30 PM with a 30-minute lunch break, your total work hours would be 8 hours (8.5 hours gross minus 0.5 hours break). Our calculator handles this automatically, including edge cases like overnight shifts where the clock-out time crosses midnight. You can also enter fractional break times for accuracy.

What counts as overtime in the United States?▼

Under the federal Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA), non-exempt employees must receive overtime pay of at least 1.5 times their regular rate for all hours worked over 40 in a workweek. Some states add daily overtime requirements: California requires 1.5x pay for hours over 8 in a day and 2x pay for hours over 12. Alaska and Nevada also have daily overtime provisions. Our calculator supports both daily and weekly overtime thresholds, which you can customize in the settings. exempt employees (typically salaried workers meeting certain criteria) aren't covered by FLSA overtime rules.

How is overtime pay calculated?▼

Overtime pay is calculated at 1.5 times (time and a half) your regular hourly rate by default. If your regular rate is $20/hour, your overtime rate is $30/hour. So if you work 45 hours in a week: 40 regular hours × $20 = $800, plus 5 overtime hours × $30 = $150, for a total gross pay of $950. Some employers offer higher overtime multipliers (like 2x for holidays), and our calculator lets you adjust the OT multiplier to match your specific situation. I've tested all of these scenarios to make sure the math is correct.

Do I include lunch breaks in my timecard?▼

Yes, unpaid meal breaks (typically 30+ minutes) should be deducted from your total work hours. Under federal guidelines, breaks of 30 minutes or more are typically unpaid and must be subtracted from your work time. Short rest breaks of 5-20 minutes are generally considered compensable (paid) work time and don't be deducted. Our calculator includes a "Break (min)" field for each day so you can accurately account for unpaid breaks. If your employer doesn't provide meal breaks or requires you to work through lunch, you should be paid for that time.

What is the difference between daily and weekly overtime?▼

Weekly overtime (FLSA) is calculated based on total hours worked in a workweek (typically Mon-Sun). Any hours over 40 trigger overtime pay. Daily overtime (state-level) is based on hours worked in a single day. In California, for example, any hours over 8 in a day earn 1.5x pay. This means a California employee who works four 10-hour days (40 hours total) would still earn 8 hours of daily overtime, even though they didn't exceed the weekly threshold. Our calculator tracks both types so you can see the full picture regardless of your state's rules.

Can I export my timecard data as a spreadsheet?▼

Yes! Click the "Export as CSV" button to download your complete timesheet as a comma-separated values file. The CSV includes all entered data: day names, clock-in/out times, break durations, daily hours worked, daily overtime hours, and the weekly summary with pay calculations. This file can be opened directly in Microsoft Excel, Google Sheets, LibreOffice Calc, or any other spreadsheet application. I recommend exporting your timecard weekly as a personal record - it's especially useful if you ever dispute a pay discrepancy.

Does this timecard calculator handle overnight shifts?▼

Yes. If your clock-out time is earlier than your clock-in time (e.g. Clock in at 10:00 PM, clock out at 6:00 AM), the calculator automatically recognizes this as an overnight shift and adds 24 hours to correctly compute 8 hours worked. This is a common issue with simpler time calculators that can't handle shifts crossing midnight. I've tested this with various overnight shift patterns to ensure accuracy in all cases.
