Beam Deflection Formulas

The deflection formulas below are derived from the Euler-Bernoulli beam theory, which assumes small deflections, linear elastic material, and plane sections remaining plane. These cover the most common configurations in structural and mechanical design.

Simply Supported Beam - Center Point Load

δ_max = P × L³ / (48 × E × I) at midspan
M_max = P × L / 4 at midspan

Simply Supported Beam - Point Load at Distance a

δ_max = P × a × (L² - a²)^(3/2) / (9 × √3 × E × I × L) at x = √((L² - a²)/3)
M_max = P × a × b / L at load point (b = L - a)

Simply Supported Beam - Uniform Distributed Load

δ_max = 5 × w × L⁴ / (384 × E × I) at midspan
M_max = w × L² / 8 at midspan

Cantilever - Point Load at Free End

δ_max = P × L³ / (3 × E × I) at free end
M_max = P × L at fixed end

Cantilever - Uniform Distributed Load

δ_max = w × L⁴ / (8 × E × I) at free end
M_max = w × L² / 2 at fixed end

Fixed-Fixed Beam - Center Point Load

δ_max = P × L³ / (192 × E × I) at midspan
M_max = P × L / 8 at supports and midspan

Fixed-Fixed Beam - Uniform Distributed Load

δ_max = w × L⁴ / (384 × E × I) at midspan
M_max = w × L² / 12 at supports

Note that the fixed-fixed beam under UDL deflects exactly 1/5 as much as the same simply supported beam. This demonstrates the major stiffness advantage of fixed-end conditions, though they require connections capable of resisting moment.

Beam Support Types

The support conditions at the beam ends determine how loads are carried and how the beam deflects:

Support TypeVertical ReactionMoment ReactionRotationDeflection
Pin (Hinge)YesNoFreeZero
RollerYesNoFreeZero
Fixed (Built-in)YesYesZeroZero
Free EndNoNoFreeFree
GuidedNoYesZeroFree

Simply supported beam: One pin + one roller. This is the most common configuration in building design (steel beams on columns). It is statically determinate, meaning reactions can be found from equilibrium equations alone.

Cantilever beam: One fixed end + one free end. Common in brackets, balconies, aircraft wings, and shelf supports. Higher deflections and moments than simply supported beams of the same span.

Fixed-fixed beam: Both ends fixed. This is statically indeterminate (requires compatibility equations). Much stiffer than simply supported, with smaller midspan moments but negative moments at the supports.

Overhanging beam: Extends past one or both supports. The overhang section behaves like a cantilever. Used in continuous beam design and bridge decks.

Bending Stress Analysis

When a beam bends, one side is in compression and the other is in tension. The bending stress varies linearly from zero at the neutral axis to a maximum at the extreme fibers:

σ = M × y / I

At the extreme fiber (y = c):

σ_max = M_max × c / I = M_max / S

Where S = I/c is the section modulus. A larger section modulus means lower stress for the same bending moment.

For structural steel design per AISC, the allowable bending stress is typically 0.6 × Fy for compact sections (where Fy is the yield strength). For A36 steel (Fy = 250 MPa), the allowable stress is 150 MPa. For A992 steel (Fy = 345 MPa), it is 207 MPa.

Shear stress is also present in beams but is usually much smaller than bending stress except in short, deep beams. Maximum shear stress occurs at the neutral axis:

τ_max = V × Q / (I × b)

Where V is the shear force, Q is the first moment of area above the neutral axis, and b is the width at the neutral axis.

Moment of Inertia

The moment of inertia (second moment of area) quantifies how a cross-section's area is distributed relative to the bending axis. Material far from the neutral axis contributes more to stiffness, which is why I-beams are so effective.

The parallel axis theorem allows you to calculate I for shapes offset from their centroid: I_total = I_centroid + A × d², where d is the distance from the shape's centroid to the reference axis.

Deflection Limits

Building codes limit beam deflection to prevent serviceability problems (visible sagging, cracking of plaster, vibration, water ponding). Common limits per IBC and AISC:

ConditionLive Load OnlyTotal Load
Floor beamsL/360L/240
Roof beams (no plaster ceiling)L/240L/180
Roof beams (plaster ceiling)L/360L/240
CantileversL/180L/120
Equipment-sensitive floorsL/480 to L/600L/360
Crane runway beamsL/600 to L/1000-

L/360 for a 6-meter beam means maximum allowable deflection = 6000/360 = 16.7 mm. If the calculated deflection exceeds this limit, you need a stiffer beam (larger I) or a shorter span.

For timber beams, deflection often controls the design rather than stress, because wood has a relatively low modulus of elasticity. For steel beams, either stress or deflection can control depending on the span and loading.

Load Combinations

Real structures carry multiple types of loads simultaneously. Building codes define load combinations that must be checked. The most common (per ASCE 7):

  • 1.4D (dead load only)
  • 1.2D + 1.6L + 0.5(L_r or S or R) (primary gravity loads)
  • 1.2D + 1.6(L_r or S or R) + (L or 0.5W) (roof loads governing)
  • 1.2D + 1.0W + L + 0.5(L_r or S or R) (wind loads included)
  • 0.9D + 1.0W (uplift check)

For ASD (Allowable Stress Design), the load combinations use factors of 1.0 on all loads. For LRFD (Load and Resistance Factor Design), the combinations above apply. The beam must satisfy strength and deflection limits for every applicable combination.

Understanding Bending Moment Diagrams

A bending moment diagram shows how the internal bending moment varies along the beam length. Key characteristics:

  • Moment is zero at pin and roller supports (unless an external moment is applied there)
  • Under a point load, the moment diagram has a sharp peak or valley
  • Under a distributed load, the moment diagram is a smooth parabola
  • The slope of the moment diagram equals the shear force: dM/dx = V
  • The area under the shear diagram between two points equals the change in moment
  • Maximum moment occurs where the shear force passes through zero

The calculator above draws the bending moment diagram on an HTML canvas element. Positive moments (causing the beam to sag) are drawn below the baseline, following the standard structural engineering convention.

For fixed-end beams, the moment diagram shows negative values at the supports and a smaller positive value at midspan. The sum of the end moment and the midspan moment equals what the moment would be for the same loading on a simply supported beam.

Worked Examples

Example 1: Simply Supported Steel Beam

A W200x46 beam (I = 45.5 × 10⁶ mm⁴, S = 448 × 10³ mm³) spans 6 m and carries a 20 kN point load at midspan. E = 200,000 MPa.

Max Moment: M = PL/4 = 20000 × 6000/4 = 30,000,000 N·mm = 30 kN·m

Max Stress: σ = M/S = 30,000,000/448,000 = 67.0 MPa (well below 207 MPa for A992)

Max Deflection: δ = PL³/(48EI) = 20000 × 6000³/(48 × 200000 × 45.5×10⁶) = 9.89 mm

L/360 Limit: 6000/360 = 16.7 mm. Actual 9.89 mm is OK (59% of limit).

Example 2: Timber Cantilever

A 100×200 mm timber beam projects 1.5 m from a wall with a 5 kN load at the free end. E = 12,000 MPa.

I: 100 × 200³/12 = 66,666,667 mm⁴

Max Moment: M = PL = 5000 × 1500 = 7,500,000 N·mm (at fixed end)

Max Stress: σ = Mc/I = 7,500,000 × 100/66,666,667 = 11.25 MPa

Max Deflection: δ = PL³/(3EI) = 5000 × 1500³/(3 × 12000 × 66,666,667) = 7.03 mm

Design Guidelines

Practical tips for beam selection and design:

  • Start with deflection: For long spans, deflection often controls before stress. Check deflection first and select a beam with sufficient I, then verify stress.
  • Use standard sections: Standard steel shapes (W, S, HSS) are readily available and cost less than custom fabrication. The AISC Steel Construction Manual lists hundreds of standard shapes.
  • Check lateral stability: Narrow beams (large depth-to-width ratio) can buckle laterally before reaching their full bending strength. Provide lateral bracing at the compression flange or use the reduced allowable stress for unbraced length.
  • Consider shear: Shear rarely controls for steel beams but can control for short timber beams and beams with large concentrated loads near supports.
  • Account for self-weight: The beam's own weight is a distributed load. For preliminary design, add 10-15% to the applied load to account for beam self-weight, then verify after selecting a section.
  • Connection design: The connection type determines whether the beam is simply supported or fixed. Simple connections (clip angles, shear tabs) allow rotation. Moment connections (welded flanges, end plates) resist rotation but cost more.
  • Vibration: Floor beams should be checked for vibration sensitivity, especially for long spans or lightweight construction. A natural frequency above 8 Hz is generally acceptable for offices.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is beam deflection?

Beam deflection is the vertical displacement of a beam under load. It depends on the load magnitude, beam length, support conditions, material stiffness (modulus of elasticity E), and cross-section shape (moment of inertia I). Deflection is a serviceability concern: the beam may be perfectly safe structurally but deflect enough to cause visible sagging or cracking of attached finishes.

What is the L/360 rule?

L/360 is the standard deflection limit for floor beams under live load per the International Building Code. L is the beam span. For a 6-meter span, the maximum live load deflection is 6000/360 = 16.7 mm. This limit prevents noticeable floor sag and keeps deflection-sensitive finishes (like drywall) from cracking.

How do I increase beam stiffness?

Increase the moment of inertia (I) by using a deeper beam, adding flanges, or using a stronger material (higher E). Doubling the beam depth approximately increases I by 8 times (since I is proportional to h³). Alternatively, reduce the span with intermediate supports or change support conditions from simply supported to fixed.

Can I add deflections from different loads together?

Yes, if the beam material is linear elastic and deflections are small (less than about L/50). This is the principle of superposition. Calculate deflection for each load case separately, then add the results. Most structural applications are well within the range where superposition is valid.

What is the difference between EI and section modulus?

EI (flexural rigidity) controls deflection. It is the product of the material stiffness (E) and the cross-section's second moment of area (I). Section modulus (S = I/c) controls bending stress. A beam can have adequate strength (sufficient S) but still deflect too much (insufficient EI), or vice versa.

Why does a cantilever deflect more than a simply supported beam?

A cantilever of length L with an end load P deflects PL³/(3EI), while a simply supported beam of the same length with a center load P deflects PL³/(48EI). That is 16 times more deflection for the cantilever. This is because the cantilever has no support at the free end, so the entire load must be carried by the single fixed support, creating a larger moment and curvature along the beam.

What is Euler-Bernoulli beam theory?

Euler-Bernoulli beam theory assumes that plane sections perpendicular to the beam axis remain plane and perpendicular after deformation. This ignores shear deformation, which is valid for slender beams (length much greater than depth). For deep beams (span-to-depth ratio less than about 5), Timoshenko beam theory should be used instead.

How do fixed-end moments affect beam design?

Fixed-end beams have negative bending moments at the supports and smaller positive moments at midspan. The maximum moment is typically at the supports. This means the beam must be strong at the connection points, not just at midspan. Fixed connections require more material and labor but result in a stiffer, lighter beam overall.
