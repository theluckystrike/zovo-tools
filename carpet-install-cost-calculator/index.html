Definition

Carpet installation involves laying fitted carpet over a subfloor, typically with a carpet pad underneath for cushioning and insulation. The total cost depends on carpet grade (fiber type, density, and face weight), pad quality, room dimensions, and labor rates. Professional installation typically includes removal of old flooring, tack strip placement, seaming, and stretching.

Understanding Carpet Installation Costs

The total cost of carpet installation depends on three main components: the carpet itself, the padding underneath, and the labor to install both. Each component has a range of quality levels and price points, and understanding what drives each cost helps you make an informed decision that balances your budget with the performance you need.

For a typical 500 square foot installation (roughly three average bedrooms), total costs range from $1,750 to $6,500 depending on your selections. At the budget end, you are looking at $1,500 to $2,000 for carpet and pad with DIY installation. At the premium end, a professionally installed high-quality nylon or wool carpet with premium padding runs $5,000 to $6,500 for the same area.

I find that the most common mistake homeowners make is choosing carpet based solely on appearance in the showroom without considering the fiber type, face weight, and density that determine how the carpet will perform under real-world conditions. A carpet that looks beautiful in a sample can mat down, show wear paths, and look tired within two years if it does not have the construction quality to match your traffic patterns.

Carpet Fiber Types Compared

The fiber type is the single most important factor in carpet performance. Four fibers dominate the residential market, each with distinct strengths and weaknesses.

Nylon

Nylon is the gold standard for residential carpet durability. It accounts for about 60% of all carpet sold in the United States. Nylon fibers are resilient (they bounce back from compression), wear-resistant, and can be treated for stain resistance. Solution-dyed nylon, where color is built into the fiber during manufacturing rather than applied after, offers the best fade and stain resistance available.

Nylon carpet costs $2 to $8 per square foot for the material alone, with premium brands like Stainmaster, Mohawk SmartStrand (technically triexta), and Shaw Anso running $4 to $8. I recommend nylon for living rooms, hallways, stairs, and any area with moderate to heavy foot traffic. A well-made nylon carpet with proper padding lasts 12 to 15 years in a typical home.

Polyester (PET)

Polyester carpet is softer to the touch than nylon and inherently stain-resistant because polyester does not absorb water-based stains. It is also less expensive, running $1 to $4 per square foot. The downside is durability: polyester fibers do not bounce back from compression as well as nylon, leading to matting and wear paths in high-traffic areas.

Polyester works well in bedrooms and low-traffic living areas where softness matters and foot traffic is limited. It is also a good choice for rental properties where the budget matters more than long-term performance. Expected lifespan is 5 to 10 years depending on traffic.

Olefin (Polypropylene)

Olefin is the most affordable synthetic carpet fiber, typically $1 to $2 per square foot. It is naturally moisture-resistant and mildew-resistant, making it suitable for basements, sunrooms, and outdoor-covered areas. However, olefin is the least durable residential fiber. It crushes easily, creates matting in traffic patterns, and tends to attract oily soils.

I only recommend olefin for basements where moisture resistance is the priority, for indoor/outdoor areas, or when budget constraints are severe. For main living areas, spending the extra $1 to $3 per square foot for nylon or polyester delivers dramatically better performance and longevity.

Wool

Wool is the original carpet fiber and remains the luxury choice. Natural wool carpet costs $8 to $20 per square foot and offers a combination of softness, resilience, natural flame resistance, and aesthetic richness that no synthetic matches. Wool is naturally stain-resistant to water-based spills (its lanolin coating repels liquids) but vulnerable to moths, bleaching, and some chemical stains.

Wool carpet makes sense in formal living rooms, master bedrooms, and spaces where comfort and appearance are the primary concerns. It requires more careful maintenance than synthetic carpet, including professional cleaning with wool-safe products. Lifespan with proper care is 20 to 25 years.

Carpet Padding Guide

Carpet padding sits between the carpet and the subfloor. It absorbs impact, provides insulation, reduces noise, and extends carpet life by absorbing the stress of foot traffic. Choosing the right padding is as important as choosing the right carpet, yet many homeowners overlook it.

Padding Thickness

The most common residential padding thickness is 7/16 inch with 6 to 8 pound density. Thicker padding (1/2 inch or more) feels plusher underfoot but can cause carpet to flex excessively, leading to wrinkles, seam separation, and premature wear. Most carpet manufacturers specify a maximum padding thickness in their warranty requirements, typically 7/16 inch for cut-pile carpet and 3/8 inch for berber or loop-pile carpet.

For stairs, use 3/8-inch padding with 8-pound or higher density. Thinner, firmer padding on stairs provides better footing and reduces the trip hazard of spongy steps. For commercial applications, padding is even thinner and denser.

Padding Materials

Rebond (bonded urethane) is the most common residential padding material, made from recycled foam scraps compressed and bonded together. It offers good performance at $0.50 to $1.00 per square foot. Prime urethane (virgin foam) provides more consistent density and longer life at $0.75 to $1.50 per square foot. Memory foam padding ($1.00 to $2.00 per square foot) offers the most luxurious feel but may be too soft for some carpet types.

Why Padding Quality Matters

Cheap padding breaks down faster than cheap carpet. When padding loses its cushion, the carpet above it takes the full impact of foot traffic and wears out faster. I have seen situations where a homeowner installed premium carpet over economy padding and the carpet showed wear patterns within three years because the padding had already compressed. Spending an extra $100 to $200 on the total padding cost for a room can extend carpet life by 3 to 5 years.

Installation Methods

Stretch-In Installation

Stretch-in is the standard residential carpet installation method. Tack strips (thin wood strips with upward-angled pins) are nailed around the room perimeter. The carpet is cut to size with extra material, laid over the padding, and stretched with a power stretcher to hook onto the tack strips. The edges are trimmed and tucked. This method allows carpet to be removed and replaced without damaging the subfloor.

Proper stretching is critical. Under-stretched carpet develops wrinkles and ripples within months, especially in humid climates. Professional installers use a power stretcher (a long pole with a head that grips the carpet and a lever mechanism that pushes against the opposite wall) rather than a knee kicker alone. Knee kickers are fine for small areas and closets but cannot achieve sufficient tension across a full room.

Glue-Down Installation

Glue-down installation bonds the carpet directly to the subfloor using adhesive. This method is used for commercial carpet tiles, indoor/outdoor carpet, and some low-profile carpet in basements or over concrete. Glue-down carpet does not require tack strips or padding (some thin foam-backed carpets have integral padding). Installation is permanent; removing glued carpet is messy and time-consuming.

DIY vs. Professional Installation

Professional carpet installation costs $1 to $2 per square foot for labor, which on a 500 square foot project adds $500 to $1,000 to the total. Professional installers bring specialized tools (power stretcher, seam iron, stair tool, wall trimmer), experience with seam placement, and warranties on their work. The labor cost is a modest portion of the total project and provides significant value in quality and longevity.

DIY installation saves the labor cost but requires renting tools ($50 to $100 per day for a power stretcher), involves a significant learning curve, and risks visible seams, ripples, and improper edge finishing. I recommend DIY only for experienced homeowners with smaller areas and simple room shapes. Stairs, large open areas, and rooms with multiple seams are best left to professionals.

Cost-Saving Strategies

Shop Remnants and Closeouts

Carpet remnants are leftover pieces from large rolls that are too small for the manufacturer to sell as full rolls. Remnants are typically discounted 30% to 60% off regular prices. If your room fits a standard remnant size (often 12x12 feet or 12x15 feet), you can get premium carpet at budget prices. Check flooring outlets and warehouse stores for the best remnant selection.

Timing Your Purchase

Carpet retailers run their biggest sales during holiday weekends (Memorial Day, Fourth of July, Labor Day, Black Friday) and during January and February (slow season for the flooring industry). Shopping during these periods can save 20% to 40% compared to regular pricing. Some retailers also offer free installation promotions during major sales events.

Negotiate Installation Costs

If buying carpet from a retailer that provides installation services, the installation price is often negotiable, especially on larger jobs. Retailers want to sell carpet, and they may reduce or waive installation fees to close the sale. Ask about free installation promotions, and if none are running, ask the salesperson what they can do on installation cost for your specific project.

Consider Carpet Tiles

Carpet tiles (typically 18x18 or 24x24 inches) offer a cost-effective alternative to broadloom carpet for basements, offices, and modern living spaces. Tiles are easy to install as a DIY project (peel-and-stick or loose-lay), allow replacement of individual damaged tiles without replacing the entire floor, and generate less waste than roll carpet. Quality carpet tiles cost $1 to $5 per square foot.

Room-by-Room Carpet Recommendations

Bedrooms

Bedrooms are low-traffic areas where softness and comfort matter most. A mid-range polyester or soft nylon in a plush or frieze cut works well. Face weight of 30 to 40 ounces is sufficient. Budget $3 to $6 per square foot installed. Choose lighter colors freely since bedrooms see minimal foot traffic and staining risk.

Living Rooms and Family Rooms

These high-traffic areas require durable nylon with a face weight of 40 to 60 ounces. Frieze or textured cut styles hide footprints and vacuum marks better than plush styles. Budget $5 to $9 per square foot installed. Mid-tone or patterned carpet hides soil better than very light or very dark solid colors.

Hallways and Stairs

The highest-traffic areas in most homes. Use the most durable carpet you can afford (nylon, 50+ ounce face weight) in a dense, low-profile construction. Berber or dense loop styles work well for hallways. Budget $6 to $10 per square foot installed, including denser padding for stairs. Pattern or color variation helps hide wear.

Basements

Moisture is the primary concern in basement carpet. Olefin fiber resists moisture and mildew, and carpet tiles allow air circulation between the floor and the carpet. Avoid thick padding that can trap moisture; use a thin synthetic pad or integral foam backing. Budget $2 to $5 per square foot installed. Test for moisture with a plastic sheet taped to the concrete for 48 hours before installing any carpet.

Carpet Maintenance and Care

Proper maintenance extends carpet life by 30% to 50% and maintains appearance throughout its service life. The investment in quality carpet pays off only if you maintain it consistently.

Vacuuming Schedule

Vacuum high-traffic areas (hallways, living rooms, entryways) at least twice per week and bedrooms once per week. The single most damaging thing to carpet fiber is embedded grit and sand that acts like sandpaper when walked on. Regular vacuuming removes this grit before it can abrade the fibers. Use a vacuum with a rotating brush bar (beater bar) for cut-pile carpet and turn off the brush bar for loop-pile and berber carpet to prevent snagging.

A quality vacuum makes a measurable difference. Consumer Reports testing consistently shows that upright vacuums with strong suction and effective brush agitation remove significantly more embedded soil than budget models. The $200 to $400 price range delivers the best performance-to-cost ratio for residential use. Robotic vacuums supplement manual vacuuming but do not replace it because they lack the suction power to extract deeply embedded grit.

Professional Cleaning

Professional steam cleaning (hot water extraction) every 12 to 18 months removes embedded soil, allergens, and oils that vacuuming alone cannot reach. Most carpet manufacturers require professional cleaning at specified intervals to maintain the warranty. A typical professional cleaning costs $0.25 to $0.50 per square foot, or $125 to $250 for a 500 square foot area.

Avoid over-wetting carpet during cleaning. Excessive moisture can seep through the carpet backing into the padding, creating conditions for mold and mildew growth. Professional cleaners use equipment that injects hot water and immediately extracts it, minimizing moisture left in the carpet. Allow 6 to 12 hours of drying time after cleaning, with windows open or fans running if possible.

Spot and Stain Removal

Address spills immediately. Blot (never rub) the spill with a clean white cloth, working from the outside edge toward the center to prevent spreading. For most water-based stains, a solution of one teaspoon of clear dish soap in one cup of warm water works effectively. For grease and oil stains, apply a dry-cleaning solvent to a cloth and blot the area. For pet stains, use an enzyme-based cleaner specifically formulated for pet urine.

Avoid using excessive cleaning solution, which leaves residue that attracts future soil. Rinse the cleaned area with plain water after treatment and blot dry. Place a stack of white paper towels over the spot with a heavy book on top to absorb remaining moisture overnight.

Furniture Protection

Heavy furniture creates permanent compression marks in carpet fiber. Use furniture cups or coasters (available at hardware stores for a few dollars) under all furniture legs and bases. When rearranging furniture, allow the carpet time to recover before deciding whether the indentations are permanent. Placing an ice cube on the indentation and allowing it to melt, then blotting the water and fluffing the fibers with a fork, often restores compressed areas on nylon and polyester carpet.

When to Replace vs. Repair Carpet

Not every carpet problem requires full replacement. Understanding when repair is sufficient saves money, while recognizing when replacement is necessary prevents wasting money on repairs to carpet that should be retired.

Repair Makes Sense When

  • A small area (under 2 square feet) is damaged by burns, stains, or tears, and a patch can be cut from a closet or remnant
  • Seams are separating but the carpet itself is still in good condition (a re-seaming costs $100 to $200)
  • Ripples and wrinkles have developed because the carpet needs re-stretching ($150 to $300 per room)
  • Individual carpet tiles are damaged in a tile installation (replace only the damaged tiles)
  • Carpet is less than 5 years old and the damage is localized

Replacement Is the Better Choice When

  • Matting and wear paths are visible throughout the room and do not recover after professional cleaning
  • The carpet is more than 10 to 12 years old in high-traffic areas
  • Persistent odors (especially pet urine) have penetrated the padding and subfloor
  • Multiple areas show damage, staining, or fiber loss
  • The padding has broken down (carpet feels thin and hard underfoot)
  • Carpet is pulling away from tack strips at walls or transitions
  • Health concerns from allergens trapped deep in old carpet fibers

How to Shop for Carpet

The carpet buying process can be overwhelming because retailers offer hundreds of options with different fiber types, construction methods, brand names, and warranty claims. A systematic approach cuts through the marketing noise and helps you find the best value for your specific situation.

Where to Buy

Home improvement stores (Home Depot, Lowe's) offer convenience, competitive pricing on mid-range options, and installation services. Specialty flooring retailers carry a wider selection including premium and luxury options, offer more knowledgeable sales staff, and often provide more thorough installation services. Carpet wholesale outlets and liquidation stores offer the lowest prices but limited selection and minimal customer service. Online retailers like FlooringInc or BuildDirect offer competitive pricing with shipping to your door, but you cannot see or feel samples before buying.

What to Look For on the Label

Every carpet sample has a specification label listing fiber type, face weight, density, twist level, and construction style. Prioritize in this order for durability: fiber type first (nylon is most durable), then density (higher is better, aim for 2,500+), then twist level (higher means better performance for cut-pile, aim for 5+ twists per inch), then face weight (40+ ounces for high-traffic areas). Brand name matters less than these core specifications.

Understanding Carpet Warranties

Carpet warranties vary from 5 years to lifetime, but the terms and conditions determine their actual value. Most warranties cover manufacturing defects (fiber delamination, backing failure, excessive shedding) but exclude wear, staining, and damage from improper cleaning. Stain warranties typically require prompt treatment (within 24 hours) and professional cleaning at specified intervals. Wear warranties set a threshold (usually 10% to 25% surface fiber loss) before coverage applies. Read the fine print before treating the warranty as a purchasing factor.

Getting Quotes

Obtain quotes from at least three retailers or installers. Specify the same carpet specifications (fiber type, face weight, construction) to each vendor so you are comparing equivalent products. Ask for itemized quotes that separate carpet cost, padding cost, installation labor, old carpet removal, and any additional charges (furniture moving, transition strips, stair installation). Total project quotes without itemization make it impossible to identify where one vendor is more or less expensive than another.

Negotiation Tips

Carpet pricing has significant markup, particularly at specialty retailers. Most salespeople have authority to offer 10% to 20% off the sticker price, and managers can approve deeper discounts. Ask for the "best price" on both carpet and installation. Mention competitor quotes to encourage price matching. If buying for multiple rooms, request a volume discount. Late in the month or quarter, salespeople may be more aggressive on pricing to meet sales targets. Never pay the first quoted price without asking for a discount.

Environmental Considerations

Carpet production and disposal have environmental impacts worth considering when planning your installation. Several choices can reduce your project's ecological footprint without sacrificing quality or performance.

Recycled Content Carpet

Several manufacturers produce carpet from recycled materials. Mohawk's PET polyester carpet is made from recycled plastic bottles. Interface and Shaw produce carpet tiles from recycled nylon and polyester. Recycled-content carpet performs comparably to virgin-material carpet at similar or lower price points. Choosing recycled content diverts plastic waste from landfills and reduces the energy required for fiber production.

Carpet Recycling at End of Life

Approximately 4 billion pounds of carpet are discarded annually in the United States, most of it going to landfills. The Carpet America Recovery Effort (CARE) program facilitates carpet recycling. When replacing carpet, ask your installer about recycling options for the old material. Many recycling centers accept nylon carpet for processing into new products. Some carpet manufacturers offer take-back programs for their products.

Low-VOC Options

New carpet can release volatile organic compounds (VOCs) that contribute to indoor air pollution and the "new carpet smell." The Carpet and Rug Institute's Green Label Plus certification identifies carpet, padding, and adhesives that meet stringent VOC emission standards. If indoor air quality is a concern (particularly for people with respiratory sensitivities), specify Green Label Plus certified products and allow the carpet to off-gas for 48 to 72 hours with windows open before heavy use.

Pre-Installation Preparation Checklist

Proper preparation before the installation crew arrives saves time, reduces costs, and prevents damage to your belongings. Whether you hired professionals or plan to install carpet yourself, these preparation steps ensure the project runs smoothly.

Furniture Removal

Clear all furniture from the rooms being carpeted. Some professional installers will move furniture for an additional charge ($25 to $75 per room), but you save money and protect valuable items by doing it yourself. Small items and breakables should be packed in boxes and stored in another room. Large furniture can often be moved to an adjacent uncarpeted room. If you have a grand piano, pool table, or extremely heavy items, coordinate with the installer in advance because these require specialized moving equipment.

Door and Threshold Preparation

New carpet with padding changes the floor height by 1/2 to 3/4 inch. Doors that currently swing freely over the existing floor may scrape or bind on new carpet. Check the clearance under every door in the installation area. If doors will need trimming, either do it before installation day or have the installer handle it (most charge $15 to $25 per door). Transition strips between carpet and other flooring types (hardwood, tile, vinyl) need to be installed or replaced to accommodate the new carpet height.

Subfloor Inspection

Before old carpet is removed, the subfloor condition is unknown. After removal, inspect for moisture stains (indicating potential water infiltration), soft or rotten spots (which must be replaced before new carpet goes down), squeaky areas (which should be screwed down), and excessive debris or adhesive from previous flooring. Subfloor repairs add $2 to $8 per square foot depending on the scope of work needed, but they are important for a successful installation.

Day-of Logistics

Plan for disrupted access to the rooms being carpeted for the entire installation day. Keep children and pets away from the work area. Ensure the installer has clear access to the driveway or street for delivery of carpet rolls (which can be 12 feet wide and several hundred pounds). Confirm the installation time and expected duration in advance. Have payment ready (most installers accept checks or credit cards, and payment is due upon completion).

Post-Installation Care

After installation, avoid placing heavy furniture on new carpet for 24 hours to allow the tack strips and adhesive to set fully. Run the HVAC system to ventilate any new carpet off-gassing. Expect some shedding (loose fibers working free from the surface) during the first 2 to 4 weeks, particularly with plush cut-pile carpet. This is normal and will stop with regular vacuuming. Do not use a carpet shampooer or apply liquid cleaners for at least 3 months after installation to allow the carpet to settle fully.

Carpet vs. Alternative Flooring

Carpet is one of several flooring options, and understanding how it compares to alternatives helps you decide whether carpet is the right choice for each room in your home.

Carpet vs. Hardwood

Hardwood flooring costs $8 to $15 per square foot installed versus $3 to $11 for carpet. Hardwood lasts 25 to 100+ years and can be refinished 3 to 5 times. Carpet lasts 8 to 15 years and must be replaced when it wears out. Over a 50-year period in a 300 square foot living room, you would replace carpet 3 to 4 times ($4,500 to $13,200 total) versus one hardwood installation ($2,400 to $4,500) plus one refinishing ($600 to $900). Hardwood often has a lower lifetime cost despite the higher upfront investment.

Carpet offers advantages in comfort underfoot, noise reduction (especially between floors), warmth, and affordability for budget-conscious homeowners. Bedrooms are the most common area where carpet is preferred over hardwood for comfort and warmth.

Carpet vs. Luxury Vinyl Plank (LVP)

LVP has become the fastest-growing flooring category because it combines the appearance of hardwood with waterproof performance and easy installation at $3 to $7 per square foot installed. LVP is harder and less comfortable underfoot than carpet but works in bathrooms, kitchens, basements, and high-moisture areas where carpet is not recommended. For open-concept homes, LVP provides a continuous flooring surface through common areas while carpet can be used in bedrooms for comfort.

Carpet vs. Tile

Ceramic and porcelain tile costs $5 to $15 per square foot installed and lasts virtually forever in residential applications. Tile is the best flooring for wet areas (bathrooms, mudrooms, laundry rooms) and high-traffic commercial spaces. Tile is hard, cold, and loud underfoot, making it uncomfortable in bedrooms and living areas. Carpet wins in comfort-critical rooms, while tile wins in moisture-prone and heavy-traffic areas.

Carpet vs. Laminate

Laminate flooring costs $2 to $5 per square foot installed, making it the closest competitor to carpet on price. Laminate offers a hard surface that is easy to clean but is not waterproof (water damage is the primary failure mode), louder underfoot, and less comfortable than carpet. Laminate works well in living areas with moderate traffic and offers a wood-look appearance at a fraction of hardwood cost. Carpet remains the better choice for bedrooms and spaces where softness and quiet matter most.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does carpet installation cost per square foot?
Total carpet installation costs $3 to $11 per square foot including carpet, padding, and labor. Budget carpet with basic pad runs $3 to $4 per square foot total. Mid-range with standard pad runs $5 to $7. Premium nylon or wool with premium pad and professional installation runs $8 to $11 per square foot.
How much carpet do I need for a room?
Multiply the room's length by its width to get square footage, then add 10% for waste from cuts and seams. A 12x15 foot room is 180 square feet, plus 10% waste equals 198 square feet of carpet needed. Carpet is sold in 12-foot or 15-foot wide rolls, and your installer will plan the cut layout.
What carpet type is most durable?
Nylon is the most durable residential carpet fiber. It resists crushing, matting, and abrasive wear better than all other synthetic fibers. Solution-dyed nylon offers the best stain and fade resistance. For maximum durability, choose nylon with face weight above 40 ounces and density rating above 3,000.
How long does carpet installation take?
Professional installation takes 1 to 2 days for an average home. A single room is usually completed in 2 to 4 hours. Timeline depends on total area, number of rooms, furniture moving, old carpet removal, and subfloor condition. Stairs add significant time per step.
Should I install carpet myself or hire a professional?
Professional installation is recommended for most projects. It requires specialized tools and techniques for proper stretching, seaming, and edge finishing. Poorly installed carpet develops wrinkles and premature wear. Professional labor costs $1 to $2 per square foot and is worth the investment for quality results.
What carpet padding should I choose?
For most residential carpet, choose 7/16-inch thick padding with 6 to 8 pound density. Avoid excessively thick padding, which causes wrinkles and uneven wear. For stairs, use thinner and denser padding (3/8-inch, 8-pound) for safety. Check your carpet manufacturer's warranty for padding specifications.
How much does carpeting stairs cost?
Carpeting stairs costs $15 to $45 per step including materials and professional installation. A standard 13-step staircase runs $195 to $585. Stairs require more labor per square foot than flat areas because each step is individually cut, fitted, and secured. Many installers charge a per-step rate.
What is the difference between nylon, polyester, and olefin?
Nylon is the most durable and resilient, ideal for high-traffic areas (12 to 15 year lifespan). Polyester is softer and naturally stain-resistant but less durable (5 to 10 years), and more affordable. Olefin is moisture-resistant and cheapest but crushes easily, best for basements and low-traffic areas.
Does old carpet removal cost extra?
Yes. Most installers charge $0.50 to $1.50 per square foot for old carpet and pad removal and disposal. For 500 square feet, expect $250 to $750 additional. Some retailers include removal in the installation price during promotions. DIY removal saves this cost.
How often should carpet be replaced?
Average carpet lifespan is 8 to 15 years depending on fiber type, quality, traffic, and maintenance. Replace when you see matting that cleaning cannot fix, visible wear paths, persistent odors, deep stains, wrinkles, or carpet pulling away from walls.
Is carpet cheaper than hardwood?
Carpet installation averages $3 to $7 per square foot versus $8 to $15 for hardwood. However, hardwood lasts 25 to 100+ years versus carpet's 8 to 15 years. Over a home's lifetime, carpet may be replaced 3 to 5 times while hardwood needs only refinishing, making their long-term costs comparable.
What is face weight and why does it matter?
Face weight measures carpet fiber weight per square yard in ounces. Higher face weight means more fiber and generally better durability. For residential use, 30 to 40 ounces is standard, 40 to 60 ounces is premium. Face weight alone does not determine quality; fiber type, twist level, and density also matter.

Understanding Carpet Construction

Beyond fiber type, carpet construction determines how the carpet looks, feels, and performs. Understanding the main construction styles helps you match the carpet to the room's aesthetic and traffic demands.

Cut Pile Styles

Cut pile carpet has yarn loops that are cut at the top, creating upright tufts. Plush (or velvet) cut pile has a smooth, even surface that shows footprints and vacuum marks but provides an elegant, formal appearance. Saxony is similar but with slightly longer, more twisted tufts. Frieze (pronounced free-zay) uses highly twisted, kinked fibers that create a textured, casual look that hides footprints and vacuum marks well. Frieze is the most popular residential carpet style because it balances appearance with practical performance.

Loop Pile Styles

Loop pile carpet has uncut yarn loops creating a flat, dense surface. Level loop (also called berber) has loops of uniform height, producing a clean, modern look. Multi-level loop uses loops of varying heights to create patterns and texture. Loop pile is extremely durable because the loop structure resists crushing and matting. It works well in high-traffic areas and commercial applications. The downside is that snags can pull loops and create runs, similar to a knit sweater.

Cut and Loop Combinations

Combining cut and loop tufts creates patterns and visual texture within the carpet surface. These styles offer the durability benefits of loop construction in high-traffic patterns with the softness of cut pile in lower-traffic areas. Cut-and-loop patterns effectively hide wear, soil, and footprints, making them practical choices for active households.
