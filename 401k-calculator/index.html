How a 401(k) Works

A 401(k) is an employer-sponsored retirement savings plan named after Section 401(k) of the Internal Revenue Code. It allows employees to contribute a portion of their paycheck to a tax-advantaged investment account before (Traditional) or after (Roth) income taxes are withheld. The concept was created by benefits consultant Ted Benna in 1980, and 401(k) plans have since become the dominant retirement savings vehicle in the United States, replacing traditional pensions for most private-sector workers.

According to the Investment Company Institute, 401(k) plans held approximately $7.9 trillion in assets as of late 2025, spread across roughly 625,000 plans covering about 70 million active participants. The appeal is straightforward: tax advantages, employer matching (free money), and automated payroll deductions that enforce disciplined saving without requiring willpower each month.

The mechanics are simple. You tell your employer what percentage of your salary to defer into your 401(k). That money is invested in the funds available within your plan. Your employer may match a portion of your contributions. The investments grow tax-deferred (or tax-free in a Roth). At retirement, you withdraw the money as income. The combination of consistent contributions, employer matching, compound investment growth, and tax advantages can turn modest payroll deductions into a substantial retirement fund over a 30-40 year career.

Understanding Employer Match

Employer matching is the closest thing to free money in personal finance. Your employer contributes additional dollars to your 401(k) based on how much you contribute. The most common match formulas are:

  • 50% match up to 6%: Employer adds 50 cents for every dollar you contribute, up to 6% of your salary. If you earn $80,000 and contribute 6% ($4,800), your employer adds $2,400.
  • 100% match up to 3%: Employer matches dollar-for-dollar on the first 3% of salary. On $80,000, that is $2,400 in match money if you contribute at least 3%.
  • Tiered match: 100% on the first 3%, then 50% on the next 2%. On $80,000, that equals $2,400 + $800 = $3,200 total match.

According to the Bureau of Labor Statistics 2024 Employee Benefits Survey, approximately 56% of private industry workers had access to employer matching in their defined contribution plans. The median employer match works out to roughly 3-4% of salary for employees who contribute enough to it.

The single most important rule of 401(k) investing: always contribute at least enough to capture your full employer match. Anything less means you are declining part of your compensation. If your employer matches 50% up to 6% and you only contribute 3%, you are leaving half your match on the table. Over a 30-year career at 7% returns, that missed match could grow to over $100,000 in lost retirement savings.

The Power of Compound Growth

Compound growth is what transforms modest monthly contributions into a retirement fund worth hundreds of thousands or millions of dollars. When your investments earn returns, those returns get reinvested and earn their own returns. Over long time periods, this exponential growth means the majority of your final balance comes from investment gains, not from the money you personally contributed.

Traditional vs Roth 401(k)

Most large employers now offer both Traditional and Roth 401(k) options within the same plan. The fundamental difference is the timing of when you pay taxes.

FeatureTraditional 401(k)Roth 401(k)
ContributionsPre-tax (reduces taxable income)After-tax (no deduction)
GrowthTax-deferredTax-free
WithdrawalsTaxed as ordinary incomeTax-free (if qualified)
RMDsRequired at age 73Not required (SECURE 2.0)
Best forHigh earners expecting lower tax in retirementYounger workers expecting higher future tax
Employer matchGoes to Traditional bucketGoes to Traditional bucket

For someone in the 24% bracket now who expects to be in the 22% bracket in retirement, Traditional saves money because the deduction is at a higher rate than the eventual withdrawal tax. For someone in the 12% or 22% bracket now who expects to be in the same or higher bracket later (due to higher future income, RMDs, or potential tax rate increases), Roth contributions lock in the current lower rate and provide tax-free income for life.

Many financial planners recommend a split strategy: contribute enough to Traditional to capture the employer match, then direct additional contributions to Roth. This gives you both taxable and tax-free buckets in retirement, providing flexibility to manage your tax bill year by year. Withdrawal Strategies

Accumulating a large 401(k) balance is only half the equation. How you withdraw money in retirement determines how long it lasts and how much you keep after taxes.

The 4% Rule

The 4% rule is the most widely cited withdrawal guideline. It states that you can withdraw 4% of your portfolio in the first year of retirement, then adjust that amount for inflation each year, with a high probability of your money lasting at least 30 years. The rule was developed by financial planner William Bengen in 1994 based on historical US stock and bond returns.

Some recent research suggests that 3.5% may be a safer withdrawal rate given current bond yields and market valuations. The right rate depends on your retirement length, asset allocation, other income sources (Social Security, pensions), and flexibility to reduce spending in down markets.

Required Minimum Distributions

Traditional 401(k) plans require you to start taking Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) at a certain age, whether you need the money or not. The SECURE 2.0 Act of 2022 changed the RMD rules significantly:

  • Born 1951-1959: RMDs begin at age 73
  • Born 1960 or later: RMDs begin at age 75 (starting in 2033)
  • Roth 401(k) accounts: No RMDs required as of 2024 (SECURE 2.0 change)
  • Penalty for missed RMD: 25% excise tax (reduced from 50% by SECURE 2.0), or 10% if corrected within 2 years

The RMD amount is calculated by dividing your account balance by a life expectancy factor from IRS Uniform Lifetime Table III. For example, at age 73 the divisor is 26.5, so a $1,000,000 balance would require an RMD of roughly $37,736. At age 80, the divisor drops to 20.2, making the RMD $49,505 on the same balance. RMDs are taxed as ordinary income and can push you into a higher tax bracket if you are not careful with planning.

This is one of the strongest arguments for Roth 401(k) contributions or Roth conversions before RMD age. Roth accounts are not subject to RMDs, allowing the money to continue growing tax-free for as long as you want.

Early Withdrawal Rules

Withdrawing from a 401(k) before age 59.5 generally triggers two costs: a 10% early withdrawal penalty plus regular income taxes on the withdrawn amount. On a $50,000 early withdrawal in the 24% tax bracket, you would owe $5,000 in penalties plus $12,000 in income tax, receiving only $33,000 of the original $50,000. That is a 34% haircut before accounting for lost future growth.

Most 401(k) plans offer a curated menu of 15-30 investment options, typically including target-date funds, index funds, actively managed funds, bond funds, and sometimes company stock. Choosing the right allocation significantly impacts your long-term returns.

Student Loan Matching

One of the most impactful SECURE 2.0 changes for younger workers is the student loan match provision. Employers can now treat an employee's student loan payments as if they were 401(k) contributions for the purpose of calculating the employer match. If you are making $500/month in student loan payments and your employer matches 50% of contributions up to 6% of salary, your loan payments could qualify you for that match even if you cannot afford to contribute directly to the 401(k). This is a significant benefit for the roughly 45 million Americans carrying student loan debt.

Automatic Enrollment and Escalation

Starting in 2025, all new 401(k) plans (not existing plans) must automatically enroll employees at a contribution rate between 3% and 10%, with automatic annual increases of 1% until reaching at least 10% (capped at 15%). This addresses the behavioral inertia that causes many workers to never enroll or to stay at inadequate contribution levels. Research from the National Bureau of Economic Research shows that auto-enrollment increases participation rates from roughly 60% to over 90%.

Target-Date Funds Explained

Target-date funds are the most popular investment option in 401(k) plans, holding roughly 60% of all 401(k) assets for workers in their 20s and 30s according to Vanguard data. They are as a one-decision investment: you choose the fund with the year closest to your expected retirement date, and the fund automatically adjusts its stock-to-bond allocation as you age.

The main advantages of target-date funds are simplicity and automatic rebalancing. You never adjust your allocation or worry about being too aggressive or too conservative for your age. The main disadvantages are slightly higher fees compared to building your own portfolio from index funds (target-date fund expense ratios typically range from 0.08% to 0.75%), and a one-size-fits-all allocation that may not match your specific risk tolerance or retirement timeline.

If you are unsure how to invest your 401(k) and do not learn about asset allocation, a target-date fund is an excellent default choice. If your plan offers a low-cost target-date fund (under 0.15% expense ratio), it is hard to go wrong with this approach.

Mega Backdoor Roth Strategy

The mega backdoor Roth is an advanced strategy that allows high earners to contribute up to $70,000 total to their 401(k) in 2026, far beyond the $23,500 employee deferral limit. It works by making after-tax (non-Roth) contributions up to the combined limit, then converting those contributions to Roth either within the plan or through a rollover to a Roth IRA.

How It Works

  • You max out your pre-tax or Roth employee deferrals ($23,500 in 2026).
  • Your employer contributes their match (say $5,000).
  • The combined limit is $70,000. You have used $28,500 ($23,500 + $5,000).
  • You can make after-tax contributions of up to $41,500 ($70,000 - $28,500) if your plan allows it.
  • You then do an in-plan Roth conversion or roll the after-tax contributions to a Roth IRA.
  • up to $70,000 per year flowing into Roth accounts, growing tax-free forever.

Not all 401(k) plans support this strategy. Your plan must allow after-tax contributions (not the same as Roth) and either in-plan Roth conversions or in-service distributions to an IRA. Check with your plan administrator. If available, this is one of the most tax planning tools for high earners who have already maxed out their standard contributions.

401(k) Rollover Options

When you leave an employer, deciding what to do with your 401(k) is one of the most consequential financial decisions you will make. The wrong choice can cost thousands in unnecessary taxes, penalties, or fees. Here are your options ranked from generally best to worst.

Option 1 Roll Over to an IRA (Usually Best)

A direct rollover from your 401(k) to a Traditional IRA (or Roth IRA for Roth 401(k) funds) is the most popular and often best choice. You gain access to thousands of investment options instead of the 15-30 in a typical 401(k), often with lower expense ratios. Vanguard, Fidelity, and Schwab all offer free IRA accounts with excellent index fund options at expense ratios of 0.03-0.05%. A direct trustee-to-trustee transfer avoids any tax consequences.

Option 2 Roll Over to New Employer's 401(k)

If your new employer's plan has good fund options with low fees, rolling into the new 401(k) keeps everything in one place and maintains the option for 401(k) loans. This is also the only way to preserve eligibility for the Rule of 55 exception on those funds. The main drawback is that 401(k) plans typically offer fewer investment choices than an IRA.

Option 3 Leave It in the Old Plan

If your balance exceeds $7,000, you can leave the money in your former employer's plan. This makes sense if the old plan has exceptional fund options or institutional share classes with very low fees., you lose the ability to contribute, and managing multiple old 401(k) accounts across former employers gets complicated over time. Many people have "orphaned" 401(k)s at previous employers that they eventually forget about.

Option 4 Cash Out (Almost Always Wrong)

Cashing out triggers immediate income taxes plus the 10% early withdrawal penalty if you are under 59.5. On a $100,000 balance in the 24% tax bracket at age 40, you would lose $10,000 to penalties and $24,000 to taxes, receiving only $66,000. That same $100,000 left invested at 7% until age 67 would grow to approximately $588,000. Cashing out should be an absolute last resort.

401(k) vs IRA Which Comes First?

If you have money available to save for retirement, the optimal order of accounts matters. The widely recommended contribution priority is:

  1. 401(k) up to the employer match. This is a guaranteed 50-100% return on your money. Nothing else comes close. If your employer matches 50% on 6%, contribute at least 6%.
  2. Max out a Roth IRA ($7,000 in 2026). A Roth IRA offers more investment choices, lower fees, tax-free growth, no RMDs, and the ability to withdraw contributions (not earnings) at any time without penalty.
  3. Go back and max out the 401(k). With the match captured and IRA maxed, direct remaining savings to the 401(k) up to the $23,500 employee limit.
  4. HSA if eligible ($4,300 individual, $8,550 family in 2026). Health Savings Accounts offer triple tax advantage: deductible contributions, tax-free growth, and tax-free withdrawals for medical expenses. After age 65, non-medical withdrawals are taxed like a Traditional IRA.
  5. Taxable brokerage account. After maxing all tax-advantaged accounts, invest in a regular brokerage account. Lower tax efficiency but unlimited contributions and no withdrawal restrictions.

The True Impact of 401(k) Fees

Fees are the silent killer of 401(k) returns. Unlike market losses, which are visible and eventually recover, fees are a permanent drag on your balance that compounds against you every single year. Understanding and minimizing fees is one of the few variables in retirement planning that you can directly control.

Types of 401(k) Fees

Investment Management Fees

0.03% - 1.5%

Expense ratios charged by the mutual funds in your plan. The largest fee category. Index funds: 0.03-0.10%. Actively managed: 0.50-1.50%.

Plan Administration Fees

0.10% - 0.50%

Charged by the plan provider (Fidelity, Vanguard, etc.) for recordkeeping, compliance, and administration. Sometimes paid by the employer, sometimes deducted from participant accounts.

Individual Service Fees

$25 - $100

Charged for specific actions like taking a loan, processing a hardship withdrawal, or generating a QDRO. Infrequent but can add up.

Revenue Sharing

Hidden

Some fund companies pay the plan provider a portion of expense ratios in exchange for being included on the plan's fund menu. This incentivizes including higher-cost funds.

Common 401(k) Mistakes to Avoid

After years of watching people manage their retirement accounts, I have seen the same costly mistakes repeated over and over. Here are the most impactful ones to avoid.

1. Not Contributing Enough to Get the Full Match

This is the single most expensive mistake. If your employer matches 50% up to 6% and you contribute 3%, you are leaving 1.5% of your salary on the table every year. On an $80,000 salary, that is $1,200 per year in free money declined. Over 30 years at 7% returns, that missed match alone grows to roughly $113,000.

2. Keeping the Default Investment Allocation

Many plans auto-enroll participants into a money market or stable value fund as the default. These are essentially cash-like investments earning 2-4% annually. If you are decades from retirement, you need growth-oriented investments (stock index funds). A 30-year-old in a money market fund instead of a stock index fund could end up with less than half the retirement balance they would have achieved with an age-appropriate allocation.

3. Cashing Out When Changing Jobs

According to data from Alight Solutions, approximately 40% of employees cash out their 401(k) when leaving a job, especially those with smaller balances. This destroys compound growth potential and triggers taxes and penalties. Always roll over to an IRA or new employer plan.

4. Not Increasing Contributions Over Time

Starting at 3% is fine if that is what you can afford, but staying at 3% for your entire career is a problem. As your salary grows, increasing your contribution percentage by 1% each year is painless because the raise more than offsets the additional deferral. Most plans offer automatic escalation features. Turn them on.

5. Ignoring Fees

The difference between a 0.05% expense ratio index fund and a 1.0% actively managed fund seems trivial on paper. But on a $500,000 balance over 20 years, that 0.95% annual drag costs approximately $165,000 in lost returns. Always check the expense ratios of every fund in your lineup and choose the lowest-cost option for each asset class.

6. Taking a 401(k) Loan for Non-Emergencies

401(k) loans let you borrow up to $50,000 or 50% of your vested balance. You pay the interest back to yourself, which sounds great. But the borrowed money misses out on market returns during the loan period, and if you leave your job, the entire loan balance becomes due within 60-90 days. Using a 401(k) loan for a vacation or a car upgrade is a costly decision that most people come to regret.

Understanding 401(k) Loans

Most 401(k) plans allow participants to borrow against their vested balance. While this can be a lifeline in emergencies, it comes with significant risks that are often underestimated.

The Hidden Cost Opportunity Cost

The interest you pay on a 401(k) loan goes back to your own account, which makes it sound like you are paying interest to yourself., the real cost is the opportunity cost of the borrowed amount not being invested in the market. If you borrow $30,000 at 9% interest but the market returns 10% during the loan period, you are losing the spread. Over a 5-year loan period, a $30,000 loan could cost you $8,000-15,000 in lost investment returns versus not taking the loan.

The Job Loss Trap

The biggest risk of a 401(k) loan is involuntary job loss. If you leave your employer (whether voluntarily or through layoff), most plans require full repayment within 60-90 days. If you cannot repay, the outstanding balance is treated as a distribution: you owe income taxes plus the 10% early withdrawal penalty if under 59.5. A $30,000 outstanding loan in the 24% tax bracket would result in a $10,200 tax-and-penalty hit on top of losing your job. The SECURE 2.0 Act extended the repayment deadline to the tax filing deadline for the year of separation, which helps somewhat but does not eliminate the risk.

When a 401(k) Loan Might Make Sense

  • Avoiding high-interest credit card debt (20%+ APR) when you have high job security
  • A true financial emergency after exhausting savings and emergency funds
  • A primary home down payment (15-year repayment period available)

When it almost never makes sense: vacations, car upgrades, funding a business, consolidating low-interest student loans. If you are considering a 401(k) loan for any non-emergency purpose, take a hard look at whether you can cut expenses or find other funding sources first.

Why Brackets Matter for 401(k) Decisions

A Traditional 401(k) contribution saves you tax at your marginal rate today. If you earn $90,000 as a single filer and contribute $10,000 to a Traditional 401(k), that $10,000 is deducted entirely from the 22% bracket, saving you $2,200 in taxes this year. In retirement, your withdrawals fill up the brackets from the bottom: the first $11,600 is taxed at 10%, the next portion at 12%, and so on. If your retirement income is lower than your working income, you effectively move money from a higher bracket today to a lower bracket in retirement.

, consider the TCJA expiration risk. If the current brackets revert to 2017 levels, the 22% bracket becomes 25% and the 24% becomes 28%. Workers currently in the 22-24% range who expect to be in a similar position in retirement may find Roth contributions more valuable, because they lock in today's historically low rates before a potential increase.

When projecting your 401(k) balance decades into the future, it is critical to understand the difference between nominal dollars (the actual number) and real dollars (adjusted for purchasing power). A $2 million balance at age 67 sounds impressive, but if inflation averages 3% over the next 35 years, that $2 million has the purchasing power of roughly $710,000 in today's dollars.

TIPS and I Bonds in Your Portfolio

Treasury Inflation-Protected Securities (TIPS) and Series I Savings Bonds are protect against inflation. TIPS adjust their principal value based on the Consumer Price Index, so your investment keeps pace with inflation automatically. Some 401(k) plans include a TIPS bond fund, which can be a useful component of the bond allocation for participants within 10-15 years of retirement. I Bonds are purchased directly through TreasuryDirect.gov (not available in 401(k) plans) and currently offer a competitive real yield that makes them an excellent addition to your overall retirement strategy outside the 401(k).

Healthcare Costs in Retirement

Healthcare is often the largest expense category in retirement that people underestimate. According to Fidelity's 2025 Retiree Health Care Cost Estimate, an average retired couple age 65 should expect to need approximately $340,000 in after-tax savings to cover healthcare expenses throughout retirement (not including long-term care). This figure has increased roughly 4-5% annually, outpacing general inflation.

Medicare Coverage Gaps

  • Medicare Part A (hospital insurance) is free for most retirees, but covers only hospital stays, not routine care.
  • Medicare Part B (medical insurance) costs approximately $185/month per person in 2026 and covers doctor visits and outpatient care, but with a 20% coinsurance for most services.
  • Medicare Part D (prescription drug) premiums average $35-55/month with variable copays.
  • Medigap supplemental insurance covers Part A and B gaps but adds $150-400/month per person.
  • Dental, vision, and hearing are generally not covered by original Medicare.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much should I contribute to my 401(k)?

At minimum, contribute enough to capture your full employer match. Beyond that, aim for 10-15% of pre-tax income including employer contributions. If you can afford to max out the 2026 limit of $23,500 (or $31,000 if 50+), that positions you well for retirement. Start where you can and increase by 1% each year. Many plans offer automatic escalation that raises your contribution percentage annually, which removes the friction of making the decision each time.

What is the 401(k) contribution limit for 2026?

The 2026 employee elective deferral limit is $23,500. If you are age 50 or older, you can add a $7,500 catch-up contribution for a total of $31,000. Workers aged 60-63 qualify for a SECURE 2.0 super catch-up of $11,250, bringing their total to $34,750. The combined employee plus employer limit is $70,000 ($77,500 with standard catch-up). These limits apply across all 401(k) plans you participate in during the same tax year.

What is the difference between a Traditional and Roth 401(k)?

Traditional 401(k) contributions reduce your taxable income today and grow tax-deferred, but withdrawals in retirement are taxed as ordinary income. Roth 401(k) contributions are made with after-tax dollars with no upfront deduction, but qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free. The right choice depends on whether you expect your tax rate to be higher or lower in retirement. Many advisors recommend splitting between both types for tax flexibility.

How does employer matching work in a 401(k)?

Your employer contributes additional money based on your contributions, up to a formula specified in your plan. A common formula is 50% match on the first 6% of salary. On $80,000 salary, contributing 6% ($4,800) gets you an additional $2,400 from your employer. Some companies use tiered formulas or dollar-for-dollar matches. Check your plan document or ask HR for your exact formula. The match is the highest-return investment available to you because it is an immediate 50-100% guaranteed return before any market gains.

What happens if I withdraw from my 401(k) before age 59.5?

Early withdrawals incur a 10% penalty plus regular income taxes. On $20,000 in the 24% bracket, that means $2,000 penalty plus $4,800 in tax, netting you only $13,200. Exceptions include the Rule of 55 (leaving job at 55+), 72(t) SEPP distributions, disability, and SECURE 2.0 emergency provisions ($1,000/year without penalty). Beyond the immediate tax hit, you lose decades of compound growth on the withdrawn amount.

What is the 4% rule for retirement withdrawals?

The 4% rule says you can withdraw 4% of your portfolio in year one of retirement, then adjust annually for inflation, with a high probability of your money lasting 30+ years. For $1 million, that means $40,000 in year one (~$3,333/month). The rule was developed from historical return data and is a starting point, not a guarantee. Some researchers now suggest 3.5% as safer given current market conditions. Flexibility to reduce spending in down markets significantly improves outcomes.

When do Required Minimum Distributions (RMDs) start?

Under SECURE 2.0, RMDs from Traditional 401(k) accounts begin at age 73 for those born 1951-1959, and age 75 for those born 1960 or later. Roth 401(k) accounts are no longer subject to RMDs as of 2024. The penalty for missing an RMD has been reduced from 50% to 25% (or 10% if corrected within 2 years). RMD amounts are calculated using IRS life expectancy tables and increase as a percentage of your balance each year.

What rate of return should I assume for my 401(k)?

A reasonable long-term assumption for a diversified stock-heavy portfolio is 7-8% nominal return (before inflation) or 4-5% real return. A conservative 60/40 stock/bond split might return 6-7%. This calculator defaults to 7%, which is a prudent middle-ground for long-term projections. Remember that actual returns will vary year to year, and past performance does not guarantee future results. The 7% figure is based on roughly 100 years of US stock market history.

Roth Conversion Strategies

A Roth conversion involves moving money from a Traditional 401(k) or Traditional IRA to a Roth account. You pay income taxes on the converted amount in the year of conversion, but the money then grows tax-free forever. Strategic Roth conversions can save significant money over a lifetime, especially when done during low-income years.

When Roth Conversions Make Sense

  • Gap years between jobs: If you leave one job and have a period with reduced or no income, your tax bracket drops. Converting Traditional money to Roth at this lower rate locks in a permanent tax advantage.
  • Early retirement before If you retire at 55-62 before claiming Social Security, your taxable income may be very low. Fill up the lower brackets (10% and 12%) with Roth conversions each year.
  • If your 401(k) balance drops 30% in a crash, converting during the downturn means you pay tax on the depressed value. When the market recovers, the growth is all tax-free in the Roth.
  • Before If current low tax brackets expire after 2025 as scheduled, converting now at current rates may be cheaper than paying higher rates on future withdrawals.

Roth Conversion Ladder for Early Retirees

The Roth conversion ladder is a strategy used by early retirees to access Traditional 401(k)/IRA money before age 59.5 without penalties. You convert a year's worth of living expenses from Traditional to Roth each year. After a 5-year seasoning period, the converted amount (not earnings) can be withdrawn from the Roth tax-free and penalty-free at any age. This requires planning ahead, as you need 5 years of living expenses accessible from other sources (taxable accounts, Roth contributions) before the first conversion becomes available.

Conversion Tax Impact Example

Convert $50K at 22% Bracket

$11,000 tax

Pay $11,000 now. If $50K grows to $200K over 20 years at 7%, the $150K in growth is completely tax-free. Effective tax rate on the full $200K: 5.5%.

No Conversion, Withdraw at 24%

$48,000 tax

Same $50K grows to $200K in the Traditional account. Withdraw at 24%: $48,000 in taxes. The conversion saved $37,000 in this example.

Always pay the conversion taxes from outside the retirement account (use a taxable savings or checking account). Paying the tax from the converted amount itself reduces the amount that grows tax-free and may trigger a 10% early withdrawal penalty on the portion used for taxes.

401(k) and Divorce

Divorce introduces complex decisions around 401(k) accounts. In most states, 401(k) balances accumulated during the marriage are considered marital property subject to division. Understanding the process and tax implications can prevent costly mistakes during an already difficult time.

Qualified Domestic Relations Order (QDRO)

A QDRO is a court order that directs the 401(k) plan administrator to divide the account between the plan participant and an "alternate payee" (typically the ex-spouse). This is the only legal way to split a 401(k) in divorce without triggering taxes or penalties. Key points:

  • The QDRO must be drafted by an attorney familiar with retirement plan law and approved by both the court and the plan administrator.
  • QDRO preparation costs typically run $500-2,000 through a specialized QDRO attorney.
  • The alternate payee can receive their share as a direct rollover to their own IRA (no tax or penalty), take a lump-sum distribution (income tax but no 10% penalty even if under 59.5), or leave it in the plan if the plan allows.
  • Only the portion earned during the marriage is typically subject to division, though this varies by state law.

Common Division Methods

Percentage Method

50%

Each spouse receives a fixed percentage of the account balance. Simpler but the non-participant bears market risk between the divorce filing and actual distribution.

Fixed Dollar Amount

Specified $

A specific dollar amount is transferred. Provides certainty but may not reflect account growth or losses between valuation date and transfer date.

Never attempt to informally divide a 401(k) without a proper QDRO. Taking a distribution to pay your ex-spouse directly triggers full income tax plus the 10% early withdrawal penalty, potentially costing 30-40% of the transferred amount in taxes. The QDRO process avoids all of this.

Self-Employed Retirement Options

If you are self-employed, a freelancer, or a small business owner, you do not have access to an employer-sponsored 401(k)., several retirement plan options are available that offer similar or even better contribution limits.

Solo 401(k)

A Solo 401(k), also called an Individual 401(k), is the closest equivalent to a regular 401(k) for self-employed individuals with no employees (other than a spouse). You can contribute as both the employee and the employer:

  • Up to $23,500 in 2026 ($31,000 if 50+)
  • Up to 25% of net self-employment income
  • $70,000 ($77,500 with catch-up)
  • Employee deferrals can be designated as Roth

The Solo 401(k) is generally the best option for self-employed individuals earning over $60,000 because it allows the highest contribution levels. For side-hustle income alongside a W-2 job, a Solo 401(k) lets you make employer contributions on the self-employment income even if you have already maxed out the employee deferral at your day job.

SEP IRA

A Simplified Employee Pension (SEP) IRA allows contributions of up to 25% of net self-employment income, capped at $70,000 for 2026. The setup is simpler than a Solo 401(k) (just fill out IRS Form 5305-SEP), but there is no employee deferral component and no Roth option. This means you need roughly $280,000 in net self-employment income to max out the SEP at $70,000, whereas a Solo 401(k) lets you reach higher contribution levels at lower income through the employee deferral component.

SIMPLE IRA

A SIMPLE IRA is for small businesses with 100 or fewer employees. The 2026 employee contribution limit is $16,500 ($20,000 if 50+), which is lower than a 401(k). The employer must either match contributions dollar-for-dollar up to 3% of salary or make a flat 2% contribution for all eligible employees regardless of whether they contribute. SIMPLE IRAs are administratively simpler and cheaper to run than 401(k) plans but offer lower limits and fewer features.

401(k) Beneficiaries and Estate Planning

Your 401(k) beneficiary designation determines who inherits the account when you die. This designation overrides your will, making it one of the most important and frequently neglected aspects of retirement planning.

Key Rules

  • Federal law (ERISA) requires that your spouse is the automatic beneficiary of your 401(k). To name someone else as primary beneficiary, your spouse must sign a written waiver.
  • Inherited 401(k) rules (SECURE Act): Non-spouse beneficiaries must empty an inherited 401(k) within 10 years of the original owner's death (the "10-year rule"). This replaced the old "stretch IRA" provision that allowed distributions over the beneficiary's lifetime.
  • Spouses have more flexibility. They can roll the inherited 401(k) into their own IRA and treat it as their own, delaying RMDs until their own RMD age.
  • Eligible designated beneficiaries: Minor children, disabled individuals, chronically ill individuals, and beneficiaries less than 10 years younger than the deceased can still stretch distributions over their lifetime.

Common Beneficiary Mistakes

Outdated Beneficiaries

Most Common

After divorce, remarriage, or a death, the beneficiary form may still list an ex-spouse or deceased person. Review and update every 2-3 years or after any major life event.

Naming Your Estate

Tax Problem

Naming your "estate" as beneficiary forces the 401(k) through probate and eliminates the 10-year stretch for individual beneficiaries. Always name specific people or a properly drafted trust.

No Contingent Beneficiary

Risky

If your primary beneficiary predeceases you and there is no contingent, the 401(k) goes to your estate by default, losing tax advantages.

Minor Children Directly

Complicated

Minors cannot directly inherit. A court-appointed guardian manages the funds. Use a trust as beneficiary instead to maintain control over how the money is used for your children.

Review your 401(k) beneficiary designation at least once every two years or whenever you experience a major life change (marriage, divorce, birth of a child, death of a beneficiary). Log into your plan's website or contact HR to verify and update as needed.

Your 401(k) Action Plan by Age

Knowing the theory is one thing. Putting it into practice requires different strategies at different life stages. Here is a practical action plan based on where you are in your career.

Age 22-30 Foundation Phase

  • Enroll immediately and contribute at least enough for the full employer match from day one.
  • Target 10-15% total contribution (including match) by age 30.
  • Choose an aggressive allocation: 90-100% stocks via a target-date fund or a simple S&P 500 index fund.
  • Favor Roth contributions if your income is below $60,000, since you are likely in a lower tax bracket now.
  • Turn on automatic escalation (1% increase per year).
  • Goal: 1x your salary saved in retirement accounts by age 30.

Age 30-40 Accumulation Phase

  • Increase contributions as salary grows. Aim to max out the employee deferral limit ($23,500) if possible.
  • Consolidate old 401(k) accounts from previous employers into an IRA for better fund choices and lower fees.
  • Maintain a growth allocation: 80-90% stocks.
  • Start or max out a Roth IRA alongside the 401(k) for tax diversification.
  • If married, coordinate contributions with your spouse to household tax benefits.
  • Goal: 3x salary by age 40.

Age 40-50 Peak Earning Phase

  • This is typically when income peaks and children's education costs rise. Maintain 401(k) contributions at maximum even if budgets feel tight.
  • Consider the mega backdoor Roth strategy if your plan allows after-tax contributions.
  • Begin thinking about Traditional vs Roth balance. If in a high bracket, Traditional may make more sense now.
  • Review asset allocation. Consider shifting to 70-80% stocks as retirement becomes visible on the horizon.
  • Goal: 6x salary by age 50.

Age 50-59 Catch-Up Phase

  • Make full use of catch-up contributions ($7,500 extra, or $11,250 during ages 60-63).
  • Run the retirement calculator (above) seriously. Are you on track for your target?
  • If behind, aggressively cut expenses and redirect to 401(k). This is your last big window.
  • Consider Roth conversions if you have a low-income year (job transition, sabbatical) to move Traditional money to Roth at a low tax rate.
  • Begin planning your withdrawal strategy: which accounts to draw from first, Social Security timing, etc.
  • Goal: 8-10x salary by age 60.

Age 60-67 Pre-Retirement Phase

  • Use the ages 60-63 super catch-up ($11,250) to make final large contributions.
  • Shift allocation toward 50-60% stocks / 40-50% bonds. Protect against sequence-of-returns risk.
  • Build a cash reserve of 1-2 years of expenses outside the 401(k) to avoid selling stocks in a downturn during early retirement.
  • Delay Social Security to age 70 if possible (8% increase per year of delay from 62 to 70).
  • Create a detailed withdrawal plan: 401(k) withdrawals, Social Security, other income sources, tax bracket management.
  • Goal: 10-12x final salary by retirement age.

401(k) and the FIRE Movement

The Financial Independence, Retire Early (FIRE) movement focuses on aggressive saving and investing to achieve financial independence decades before the traditional retirement age. A 401(k) plays a central but nuanced role in FIRE strategies, because while it offers tax advantages, accessing the money before 59.5 requires careful planning.

How FIRE Practitioners Use 401(k) Plans

  • Max out the 401(k) first: The tax deduction effectively gives you a 22-37% immediate "return" on every dollar contributed by reducing your tax bill. FIRE savers this benefit even though the money is technically locked until 59.5.
  • Roth conversion ladder: Convert Traditional 401(k) money to Roth IRA each year after early retirement. After a 5-year waiting period, the converted principal can be withdrawn tax-free and penalty-free at any age. This is the primary bridge strategy for accessing 401(k) money before 59.5.
  • Rule of 55: If you can time your departure from your last employer at age 55, you can access that employer's 401(k) without the 10% penalty. Some FIRE practitioners plan around this milestone.
  • 72(t) Substantially Equal Periodic Payments allow penalty-free access at any age, but the payment schedule is inflexible and must continue for 5 years or until 59.5, whichever is longer.

The 401(k) is the cornerstone of the FIRE savings plan because of its outsized contribution limit ($23,500) compared to IRAs ($7,000). A married couple maxing two 401(k) plans at $23,500 each saves $47,000 per year in tax-advantaged space before even touching IRA contributions. The tax deduction on Traditional contributions further accelerates savings by reducing your tax bill and increasing the amount available to invest.

FIRE Withdrawal Order

For early retirees, the withdrawal order matters enormously for tax efficiency. The generally recommended sequence is:

  1. Taxable brokerage accounts first: These have no age restrictions and benefit from lower long-term capital gains rates (0-20%).
  2. Roth You can withdraw your Roth IRA contributions (not earnings) at any time without tax or penalty, since you already paid tax on them.
  3. Roth conversion ladder: After the 5-year seasoning period, converted amounts become available tax-free and penalty-free.
  4. Traditional 401(k)/Access after 59.5 without penalty, or via 72(t) SEPP or Rule of 55 before that.
  5. Last, since these grow tax-free indefinitely and have no RMDs.

This order reduces the total tax paid over your retirement by drawing from taxable sources first (at favorable capital gains rates), then from pre-tax accounts (filling lower brackets), while allowing Roth accounts to grow tax-free for as long as possible.
