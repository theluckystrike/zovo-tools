How to Measure Your Lawn

precise measurements are the foundation of any sod project. For rectangular or square lawns, simply measure the length and width and multiply them together. For L-shaped or irregular lawns, divide the area into separate rectangles, calculate each section individually, and add the totals together.

Use a measuring tape, measuring wheel, or a GPS-based lawn measurement app for larger properties. If you have triangular sections, calculate them as half the base times the height. Subtract any areas you do not need to sod, such as driveways, patios, flower beds, and swimming pools.

Professional Tip - Walk the perimeter of your yard with a measuring wheel and sketch the shape on graph paper. This visual reference helps you identify odd corners and obstacles that add to waste.

Waste Factor Guide

Every sod installation generates some waste due to cutting, fitting around obstacles, and overlapping at seams. Choosing the right waste percentage saves money while ensuring you have enough sod to finish the job.

Waste %Best ForDescription
5%Simple rectanglesStraight edges, no curves, no obstacles
10%Moderate complexitySome curves, a few flower beds or walkways
15%Irregular shapesMultiple angles, circular beds, tree rings
20%+Complex layoutsMany obstacles, steep slopes, intricate borders

Running out of sod mid-installation is a serious problem because fresh sod must be laid the same day it is harvested. If the next delivery takes a day or more, the partially installed lawn develops visible seam lines where old and new sod meet at different growth stages.

Soil Preparation Checklist

Proper soil preparation accounts for roughly 80% of your new lawn's long-term health. Skipping or rushing this step is the most common cause of sod failure within the first year.

Step-by-Step Soil Prep

1. Remove existing vegetation. Kill or strip old grass, weeds, and debris. If using herbicide, wait the manufacturer's recommended period before installing sod. For mechanical removal, use a sod cutter set to 1-inch depth.

2. Grade and slope. Ensure the soil surface slopes away from your home's foundation at a minimum of 1 to 2 percent grade. This prevents water pooling against structures and promotes natural drainage.

3. Till the soil. Rototill the top 4 to 6 inches to break up compaction. This step is critical for clay-heavy soils. For sandy soils, tilling is less critical but still beneficial for mixing in amendments.

4. Amend the soil. Spread 1 to 2 inches of quality compost over the tilled area and mix it into the top 4 inches. If a soil test indicates low pH, add lime. For high pH, add sulfur. Target a pH between 6.0 and 7.0 for most grass types.

5. Apply starter fertilizer. Use a phosphorus-rich starter fertilizer (e.g., 18-24-6) according to package directions. Phosphorus promotes root establishment, which is the priority for new sod.

6. Level and firm. Rake the surface smooth and roll with a half-filled lawn roller. The finished grade should sit about 1 inch below adjacent sidewalks and driveways to account for sod thickness.

Sod Installation Tips

Professional-quality installation does not require professional tools. Following a few key principles makes a significant difference in the final result.

Start along a straight edge. Lay your first row against a driveway, sidewalk, or string line. This gives you a clean reference line for all subsequent rows. Stagger joints like bricks, never aligning seams between adjacent rows.

Push seams tight. Butt each piece firmly against its neighbor without overlapping or leaving gaps. Even small gaps dry out quickly and leave brown lines. Use a knife to trim pieces that overlap.

Roll after installation. Use a lawn roller filled one-third with water to press the sod into firm contact with the soil. This eliminates air pockets that prevent root establishment.

Install the same day. Sod begins deteriorating within hours of harvest. The rolls on a pallet generate heat, and interior rolls can reach temperatures that kill the grass within 24 hours. Install sod the day it arrives, and keep uninstalled pallets in shade.

When to Lay Sod

Timing your sod installation to match the grass type's natural growth cycle dramatically improves establishment rates and reduces the risk of failure.

Warm-season grasses (Bermuda, St. Augustine, Zoysia): Install from late April through mid-June when soil temperatures consistently exceed 65 degrees Fahrenheit. The grass needs the entire warm season to establish roots before winter dormancy.

Cool-season grasses (Fescue, Bluegrass): Early September through mid-October is the ideal window. Cooler air temperatures reduce water stress on the sod while soil remains warm enough for root growth. Early spring (March through April) is a secondary window.

Avoid extreme conditions. Do not install sod during heat waves, drought, or when daytime temperatures exceed 95 degrees Fahrenheit. Frozen ground or ground that will freeze within 6 weeks is also unsuitable. Mid-summer installations in southern climates require nearly double the irrigation.

Common Sod Installation Mistakes

After working through hundreds of lawn projects, these are the errors that cause the most problems.

Ordering too little sod. This forces a second delivery, often a day later, which creates visible transition lines between the fresh and day-old sod. Always round up and add your waste factor.

Skipping soil preparation. Laying sod over compacted or poorly graded soil leads to drainage problems, shallow rooting, and premature failure. The time spent on soil prep pays dividends for years.

Leaving gaps between pieces. Even quarter-inch gaps dry out rapidly, creating brown lines that take weeks to fill in. Push each piece firmly against its neighbor.

Overwatering or underwatering. Both extremes are equally damaging. Overwatering causes root rot and fungal disease. Underwatering causes edges to curl and die. Use the tug test at 10 to 14 days to assess root development.

Mowing too soon. Wait until the sod roots are established (typically 2 to 3 weeks) before the first mow. Set your mower to its highest setting and never remove more than one-third of the grass blade length at a time.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much sod do I need for a 1,000 square foot lawn?

For 1,000 square feet with a 10% waste factor, you need 1,100 square feet of sod, which is 110 rolls or approximately 2.2 pallets. Most suppliers sell full pallets, so you would order 3 pallets and have some extra for patching and repairs.

Can I lay sod over existing grass?

This is strongly discouraged. Sod laid over existing vegetation rarely establishes properly because the old grass creates a barrier between the new roots and the soil. The decomposing layer also creates heat and fungal problems. Always remove old vegetation first.

How long does sod last on a pallet?

Sod on a pallet begins declining within 6 to 8 hours in warm weather and can last up to 24 hours in cool conditions. Interior rolls on a pallet heat up faster than exterior ones. Plan your installation so all sod is down the same day it arrives.

Does sod need to be rolled after installation?

Yes, rolling eliminates air pockets between the sod and soil, which is important for root-to-soil contact. Use a lawn roller filled one-third to one-half with water. Roll in two directions perpendicular to each other for complete coverage.
