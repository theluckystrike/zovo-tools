Definition

Sod, also known as turf, is grass and the part of the soil beneath it held together by its roots or another piece of thin material. In horticulture, it is used to establish a lawn quickly where seeding would be too slow or impractical, such as on slopes or in areas with high erosion risk.

How to Measure Your Lawn

precise measurements are the foundation of any sod project. For rectangular or square lawns, simply measure the length and width and multiply them together. For L-shaped or irregular lawns, divide the area into separate rectangles, calculate each section individually, and add the totals together.

Use a measuring tape, measuring wheel, or a GPS-based lawn measurement app for larger properties. If you have triangular sections, calculate them as half the base times the height. Subtract any areas you do not need to sod, such as driveways, patios, flower beds, and swimming pools.

Professional Tip - Walk the perimeter of your yard with a measuring wheel and sketch the shape on graph paper. This visual reference helps you identify odd corners and obstacles that add to waste.

Soil Preparation Checklist

Proper soil preparation accounts for roughly 80% of your new lawn's long-term health. Skipping or rushing this step is the most common cause of sod failure within the first year.

Step-by-Step Soil Prep

1. Remove existing vegetation. Kill or strip old grass, weeds, and debris. If using herbicide, wait the manufacturer's recommended period before installing sod. For mechanical removal, use a sod cutter set to 1-inch depth.

2. Grade and slope. Ensure the soil surface slopes away from your home's foundation at a minimum of 1 to 2 percent grade. This prevents water pooling against structures and promotes natural drainage.

3. Till the soil. Rototill the top 4 to 6 inches to break up compaction. This step is critical for clay-heavy soils. For sandy soils, tilling is less critical but still beneficial for mixing in amendments.

4. Amend the soil. Spread 1 to 2 inches of quality compost over the tilled area and mix it into the top 4 inches. If a soil test indicates low pH, add lime. For high pH, add sulfur. Target a pH between 6.0 and 7.0 for most grass types.

5. Apply starter fertilizer. Use a phosphorus-rich starter fertilizer (e.g., 18-24-6) according to package directions. Phosphorus promotes root establishment, which is the priority for new sod.

6. Level and firm. Rake the surface smooth and roll with a half-filled lawn roller. The finished grade should sit about 1 inch below adjacent sidewalks and driveways to account for sod thickness.

Sod Installation Tips

Professional-quality installation does not require professional tools. Following a few key principles makes a significant difference in the final result.

Start along a straight edge. Lay your first row against a driveway, sidewalk, or string line. This gives you a clean reference line for all subsequent rows. Stagger joints like bricks, never aligning seams between adjacent rows.

Push seams tight. Butt each piece firmly against its neighbor without overlapping or leaving gaps. Even small gaps dry out quickly and leave brown lines. Use a knife to trim pieces that overlap.

Roll after installation. Use a lawn roller filled one-third with water to press the sod into firm contact with the soil. This eliminates air pockets that prevent root establishment.

Install the same day. Sod begins deteriorating within hours of harvest. The rolls on a pallet generate heat, and interior rolls can reach temperatures that kill the grass within 24 hours. Install sod the day it arrives, and keep uninstalled pallets in shade.

When to Lay Sod

Timing your sod installation to match the grass type's natural growth cycle dramatically improves establishment rates and reduces the risk of failure.

Warm-season grasses (Bermuda, St. Augustine, Zoysia): Install from late April through mid-June when soil temperatures consistently exceed 65 degrees Fahrenheit. The grass needs the entire warm season to establish roots before winter dormancy.

Cool-season grasses (Fescue, Bluegrass): Early September through mid-October is the ideal window. Cooler air temperatures reduce water stress on the sod while soil remains warm enough for root growth. Early spring (March through April) is a secondary window.

Avoid extreme conditions. Do not install sod during heat waves, drought, or when daytime temperatures exceed 95 degrees Fahrenheit. Frozen ground or ground that will freeze within 6 weeks is also unsuitable. Mid-summer installations in southern climates require nearly double the irrigation.

Common Sod Installation Mistakes

After working through hundreds of lawn projects, these are the errors that cause the most problems.

Ordering too little sod. This forces a second delivery, often a day later, which creates visible transition lines between the fresh and day-old sod. Always round up and add your waste factor.

Skipping soil preparation. Laying sod over compacted or poorly graded soil leads to drainage problems, shallow rooting, and premature failure. The time spent on soil prep pays dividends for years.

Leaving gaps between pieces. Even quarter-inch gaps dry out rapidly, creating brown lines that take weeks to fill in. Push each piece firmly against its neighbor.

Overwatering or underwatering. Both extremes are equally damaging. Overwatering causes root rot and fungal disease. Underwatering causes edges to curl and die. Use the tug test at 10 to 14 days to assess root development.

Mowing too soon. Wait until the sod roots are established (typically 2 to 3 weeks) before the first mow. Set your mower to its highest setting and never remove more than one-third of the grass blade length at a time.

Complete Guide to Sod Installation and Lawn Establishment

Understanding Sod Measurements and Coverage

A standard sod roll measures 2 feet wide by 5 feet long, covering 10 square feet. Rolls typically weigh 35 to 45 pounds when freshly harvested and properly watered. A full pallet contains 50 rolls and covers 500 square feet, weighing approximately 2,000 to 2,500 pounds. Some suppliers offer "big rolls" (42 to 48 inches wide) for commercial applications, which reduce installation time but require mechanical handling equipment.

Calculating the amount of sod needed starts with measuring your lawn area. For rectangular areas, multiply length by width in feet. For irregular shapes, divide the area into rectangles and triangles, calculate each section, and sum the results. Circular areas use the formula: Area = pi x radius squared. This calculator handles these calculations automatically, including waste factor adjustments.

The waste factor accounts for cutting, fitting around obstacles, and trimming edges. A 5% waste factor is adequate for simple rectangular areas with few obstacles. A 10% factor is standard for most residential lawns with moderate complexity (walkways, garden beds, trees). A 15% factor is appropriate for highly irregular shapes, steep slopes, or areas with many obstacles requiring extensive cutting. I recommend using 10% as your default and increasing to 15% for complex layouts.

Soil Preparation for Sod Installation

Proper soil preparation is the most important factor in long-term sod success. Begin by removing all existing vegetation, debris, and rocks. For existing lawns, you can use a sod cutter (available at equipment rental stores for $50 to $100 per day) to strip the old grass, or apply a non-selective herbicide (glyphosate) and wait 7 to 10 days for complete kill before removing the dead vegetation.

Grade the soil to ensure proper drainage away from buildings. A slope of 1 to 2 inches per 10 feet away from the foundation prevents water accumulation against the structure. Fill low spots with topsoil and compact gently. The finished grade should sit approximately 1 inch below the level of adjacent sidewalks, driveways, and patios to account for the thickness of the sod layer.

Soil testing through your local county extension service (typically $10 to $25) reveals pH, nutrient levels, and organic matter content. Most turfgrasses thrive in soil with a pH between 6.0 and 7.0. If pH is too low (acidic), apply pelletized limestone at the rate recommended by the soil test. If pH is too high (alkaline), apply elemental sulfur or iron sulfate. Correct pH before installing sod, as adjusting it afterward is slower and less effective.

Amend the soil with 2 to 3 inches of quality compost tilled into the top 4 to 6 inches. Compost improves soil structure, water retention, nutrient availability, and microbial activity. For clay soils that drain poorly, compost loosens the structure and improves drainage. For sandy soils that drain too quickly, compost increases water and nutrient retention. Apply a starter fertilizer (high phosphorus, such as a 10-20-10 or 18-24-12 formula) at the recommended rate and rake it into the top inch of soil.

Final grade by raking the surface smooth, removing any remaining debris, rocks, or clumps larger than a golf ball. A field rake works well for this final smoothing. The surface should be firm enough to walk on without sinking more than half an inch. Lightly water the prepared soil the day before sod installation to ensure good moisture contact when the sod is laid.

Sod Installation Step by Step

Begin installation along the longest straight edge, typically a sidewalk, driveway, or fence line. Lay the first row with the sod edges tight against the straight reference line. For the second row, stagger the end joints like bricks, with end joints offset by at least 12 inches from the row below. This staggering prevents visible seam lines and distributes stress more evenly as the sod establishes.

On slopes, lay sod perpendicular to the slope (across the hill, not up and down). This orientation prevents individual pieces from sliding downhill and helps retain moisture. On steep slopes (greater than 3:1 ratio), secure sod with biodegradable sod staples (wooden or bamboo stakes) placed every 2 to 3 feet along each piece. The staples hold the sod in place until roots anchor it naturally.

Trim sod pieces to fit around curved edges, sprinkler heads, trees, and garden beds using a sharp utility knife or serrated knife. Cut on a flat surface rather than while the sod is in place for cleaner edges. For curved borders, make a series of short, straight cuts that approximate the curve rather than trying to cut a continuous curve, which tends to tear the sod.

Water immediately and thoroughly after installation. The goal is to moisten the soil beneath the sod to a depth of 3 to 4 inches. Lift a corner of the sod periodically to check that water has penetrated through to the soil below. The first watering is the most critical; sod that dries out within the first 24 hours is very difficult to recover.

Watering Schedule for New Sod

Week 1 to 2: Water twice daily (early morning and late afternoon) for 15 to 20 minutes per zone, keeping the sod and underlying soil consistently moist but not waterlogged. The sod should never feel dry or crispy. Reduce watering if you see standing water or the soil becomes squishy underfoot.

Week 3 to 4: Reduce watering to once daily in the morning for 20 to 30 minutes. By this point, roots should be beginning to establish into the underlying soil. Perform the "tug test" by gently pulling on a piece of sod; if it resists, roots are developing. If it lifts easily, continue the twice-daily schedule.

Week 5 to 6: Transition to watering every other day for 30 to 40 minutes. Deeper, less frequent watering encourages roots to grow deeper into the soil, building drought tolerance for the future. Shallow, frequent watering trains roots to stay near the surface, making the lawn vulnerable to heat and drought stress.

Week 7 and beyond: Establish a regular watering schedule of 2 to 3 times per week, providing approximately 1 inch of total water per week (including rainfall). Most established lawns need 1 to 1.5 inches per week during the growing season. Place a rain gauge or tuna can in the sprinkler zone to measure actual water delivery. Adjust based on rainfall, temperature, and soil type (clay retains water longer; sand requires more frequent irrigation).

Grass Type Selection Guide

Cool-season grasses (Kentucky bluegrass, tall fescue, perennial ryegrass, fine fescue) grow actively in temperatures between 60 and 75 degrees Fahrenheit. They are ideal for USDA zones 3 through 6 (northern United States, upper Midwest, Northeast, Pacific Northwest). These grasses stay green longer into fall and green up earlier in spring but may go dormant (turn brown) during hot, dry summer periods.

Kentucky bluegrass produces a dense, fine-textured, dark green lawn that is considered the gold standard for residential turf. It spreads aggressively through rhizomes, filling in damaged areas naturally. It requires moderate to high maintenance (regular watering, fertilization, and mowing) and prefers full sun with at least 6 hours of direct sunlight daily.

Tall fescue is a low-maintenance alternative for cool-season areas. It tolerates drought, heat, shade, and poor soil better than Kentucky bluegrass. Modern turf-type tall fescue varieties produce a fine-textured lawn that rivals bluegrass in appearance. It does not spread through rhizomes, so damage repair requires overseeding. I recommend tall fescue for homeowners who want a good-looking lawn with less maintenance input.

Warm-season grasses (Bermuda, Zoysia, St. Augustine, centipede, bahia) grow actively in temperatures between 80 and 95 degrees Fahrenheit. They are ideal for USDA zones 7 through 10 (southern United States, Gulf Coast, Southeast, Southwest). These grasses go dormant and turn brown in winter when temperatures drop below 55 degrees consistently.

Bermuda grass is the most widely used warm-season turf, known for exceptional heat and drought tolerance, rapid growth and recovery from damage, and tolerance of heavy foot traffic. It requires full sun and does not perform well in shade. Common Bermuda is less expensive but coarser; hybrid Bermuda (Tifway 419, Celebration, Latitude 36) produces a finer-textured, more attractive lawn but must be established from sod (not seed).

Zoysia grass provides a dense, carpet-like lawn with moderate maintenance requirements. It tolerates both heat and moderate cold (further north than other warm-season grasses), handles shade better than Bermuda, and has excellent wear tolerance. The primary drawback is slow establishment. Zoysia may take 2 to 3 growing seasons to fully cover from plugs, which is why sod installation is strongly preferred for Zoysia lawns.

The transition zone (USDA zones 6b to 7a, roughly from Washington DC to Memphis to Denver) presents the greatest challenge because summers are too hot for cool-season grasses to thrive and winters are too cold for warm-season grasses. Tall fescue is often the best choice for the transition zone, supplemented by overseeding with perennial ryegrass in fall for year-round color. Zoysia can also perform well in the transition zone, accepting winter dormancy as a trade-off for summer performance.

Premium varieties (hybrid Bermuda, Zoysia, fine fescue blends) may cost $0.50 to $1.25 per square foot. Pallet pricing offers volume discounts, with most suppliers selling pallets of 500 square feet for $150 to $450.

Specify delivery location and access requirements when ordering.

DIY installation eliminates labor cost but requires a full day of physical work (plan for 500 to 1,000 square feet per person per day).

For a 5,000 square foot DIY project, total cost including sod, soil prep, and materials typically runs $1,200 to $3,000.

Long-Term Lawn Care After Sod Installation

First mowing should occur 2 to 3 weeks after installation, when roots have established sufficiently that the sod resists pulling. Set the mower to the highest recommended height for your grass type (3 to 4 inches for fescue and bluegrass, 1.5 to 2.5 inches for Bermuda and Zoysia). Never remove more than one-third of the blade height in a single mowing. A sharp mower blade produces clean cuts; dull blades tear grass tissue, creating entry points for disease.

Fertilization for newly sodded lawns begins 4 to 6 weeks after installation with a balanced fertilizer (such as 16-4-8 or 15-5-10) at the rate specified on the product label for your grass type. Cool-season lawns benefit most from fall fertilization (September through November). Warm-season lawns benefit most from spring and summer fertilization (April through August). Avoid fertilizing warm-season grasses in fall, as it can interfere with natural dormancy preparation.

Core aeration after the first growing season relieves soil compaction, improves water and nutrient penetration, and encourages deeper root growth. Aerate when the grass is actively growing (fall for cool-season, late spring for warm-season). An aerator pulls small soil plugs from the lawn, creating channels for air, water, and nutrients to reach the root zone. Leave the plugs on the surface; they break down naturally within 2 to 3 weeks.

Weed prevention in the first year relies primarily on maintaining dense, healthy turf that crowds out weed establishment. Avoid pre-emergent herbicides for the first 60 to 90 days after sod installation, as they can inhibit new root development. Once the lawn is fully established, a fall pre-emergent application prevents winter and spring annual weeds, while a spring application prevents summer annual weeds including crabgrass.

Regional Sod Considerations

Northeast and Upper Midwest (zones 3 to 5): Kentucky bluegrass and fine fescue blends dominate. Short growing seasons (May through September) limit the sod installation window. Fall installation (late August through September) is ideal because cool temperatures reduce transplant stress and fall rains provide natural irrigation. Spring installation works well from late April through May. Summer installation is feasible but requires aggressive watering during heat stress periods.

Southeast and Gulf Coast (zones 7 to 10): Bermuda, St. Augustine, Zoysia, and centipede are the primary options. The long growing season (March through November) provides an extended installation window. Late spring (April to May) is best for warm-season sod because the grass enters its peak growth phase immediately after installation. Avoid late fall installation because the grass cannot establish roots before winter dormancy.

Pacific Northwest (zones 7 to 8b): Cool, moist conditions favor perennial ryegrass, fine fescue, and Kentucky bluegrass. The mild climate allows nearly year-round installation, though fall (September to October) produces the best results due to warm soil temperatures combined with increasing rainfall. Summer installation requires irrigation to compensate for the characteristically dry Pacific Northwest summers.

Southwest and arid regions (zones 8 to 10): Bermuda grass dominates due to its exceptional heat and drought tolerance once established. Water conservation is a critical consideration; many municipalities restrict or incentivize alternatives to traditional turfgrass. If installing sod in arid regions, consider reducing the total sodded area and using drought-tolerant landscaping for non-functional areas. Drip irrigation edges and fast sprinkler heads reduce water waste during establishment.

Troubleshooting Common Sod Problems

Yellowing or browning patches within the first two weeks usually indicate watering issues. Check that sprinkler coverage reaches all areas uniformly. Corners and edges receive less water from most sprinkler patterns. Hand-water problem areas temporarily while adjusting sprinkler heads for complete coverage.

Edges lifting or curling indicate insufficient watering at the perimeter of each sod piece. Edges dry faster than the center because they have more exposed surface area. Increase watering duration and consider hand-watering edges specifically during the first 10 days. Push lifted edges back into contact with the soil and apply additional water.

Visible seams between sod pieces are normal initially and should fill in as the grass spreads over 4 to 8 weeks. For grasses that spread through rhizomes or stolons (Kentucky bluegrass, Bermuda, Zoysia), seams close naturally. For bunch-type grasses (tall fescue, perennial ryegrass), seams may persist longer and can be filled with a thin layer of topsoil and grass seed.

Fungal disease (brown patches, dollar spot, pythium blight) can occur when new sod is kept too wet, especially during warm, humid weather. Reduce watering frequency if you notice circular brown patches with a wet or slimy appearance. Improve air circulation by avoiding evening watering (morning watering allows the surface to dry during the day). In severe cases, a fungicide application may be necessary; consult your local extension service for product recommendations specific to your grass type and disease.

Grub damage appears as irregular brown patches that can be peeled back easily from the soil because grubs have consumed the roots. White grubs (larvae of Japanese beetles, June bugs, and chafers) are the most common lawn pest. A threshold of 8 to 10 grubs per square foot typically warrants treatment. Beneficial nematodes or milky spore disease provide biological control, while imidacloprid or chlorantraniliprole provide chemical control with lower environmental impact than older insecticides.

Sustainable Sod and Lawn Practices

Water-fast lawn management reduces consumption by 30 to 50% compared to traditional practices. Deep, infrequent watering (1 to 1.5 inches per week in two to three applications) encourages deeper root development than daily light watering. Cycle-and-soak irrigation (running each zone for half the total time, waiting 30 minutes, then running the second half) reduces runoff on clay soils and slopes.

Grasscycling (leaving clippings on the lawn instead of bagging) returns nitrogen, phosphorus, and potassium to the soil, reducing fertilizer needs by approximately 25%. Clippings decompose rapidly (within 1 to 2 weeks) and do not contribute to thatch when mowed at appropriate intervals. Use a mulching mower or mulching blade attachment to cut clippings finely for faster decomposition.

Soil health practices improve long-term lawn performance while reducing input requirements. Annual compost topdressing (a thin, quarter-inch layer spread across the lawn in fall) feeds soil microorganisms, improves soil structure, and increases water retention. Core aeration combined with compost topdressing accelerates soil improvement by depositing organic matter directly into the aeration holes.

Integrated Pest Management (IPM) minimizes pesticide use by combining cultural practices (proper mowing height, appropriate watering, healthy soil), biological controls (beneficial insects, microorganisms), and targeted chemical applications only when pest populations exceed treatment thresholds. A healthy, well-maintained lawn resists most pest and disease pressure without chemical intervention.

Native and low-input grass alternatives are gaining popularity as homeowners seek reduced-maintenance lawns. Buffalo grass, blue grama grass, and fine fescue blends require less water, fertilizer, and mowing than traditional lawn grasses. These options work particularly well for areas of the lawn that receive light foot traffic, where the dense, manicured appearance of traditional turf is less important than reduced maintenance and environmental impact.

Professional vs DIY Sod Installation Decision Guide

DIY sod installation makes economic sense for projects under 3,000 square feet where the homeowner is physically able to handle the work and has basic tools available. Savings of $0.75 to $2.00 per square foot on labor translate to $750 to $2,000 saved on a 1,000 square foot project. The work is physically demanding but requires no specialized skills. Expect to spend a full day on soil preparation and a full day on sod laying for a typical residential project.

Professional installation is recommended for projects over 5,000 square feet, steep slopes requiring grading equipment, sites with drainage issues requiring re-grading, commercial or high-visibility installations where appearance quality is critical, and homeowners with physical limitations. Professionals bring experience with local soil conditions, fast crews (a team of 3 to 4 can install 5,000 square feet in one day), and usually guarantee their work for 30 to 90 days.

Hybrid approaches work for many homeowners. Do the soil preparation yourself (removing old vegetation, adding compost, grading, fertilizing) and hire a crew for the sod installation only. This captures labor savings on the less skilled prep work while ensuring professional execution of the critical installation phase. Alternatively, have the sod delivered and installed by professionals but handle all post-installation watering, mowing, and care yourself.

When hiring a professional, get three written quotes that itemize soil preparation, sod type and quantity, delivery, installation labor, and any warranty or guarantee. Ask for references from recent local projects and verify insurance coverage. A reputable installer will walk the site with you before quoting, discuss soil preparation requirements, recommend appropriate grass varieties, and provide a written care guide for the establishment period.

Drainage and Grading Before Sod Installation

Proper drainage is the single most overlooked factor in sod project planning. A lawn graded at 1 to 2% slope away from the house foundation prevents water pooling against the structure and protects against basement moisture issues. To check your current grade, place a 10-foot straight board on the ground near the foundation. A level placed on the board should show the far end 1.2 to 2.4 inches lower than the foundation end. If the grade slopes toward the house, you will need to add soil and re-grade before laying sod.

French drains solve subsurface drainage problems that grading alone cannot address. A French drain consists of a perforated pipe wrapped in filter fabric and surrounded by gravel, installed in a trench that slopes at 1% grade toward a discharge point. Typical installations use 4-inch perforated PVC pipe in a trench 12 inches wide and 18 to 24 inches deep. Install French drains before laying sod, as retrofitting requires removing established turf. The cost of a French drain ($10 to $25 per linear foot for DIY, $25 to $75 per linear foot for professional installation) is far less than repairing water damage to foundations or replacing sod that failed due to saturated soil.

Catch basins collect surface water from low spots and redirect it through underground pipes to a discharge point. A 12-inch catch basin with grate costs $30 to $60 and connects to 4-inch solid (non-perforated) PVC pipe. Position catch basins at the lowest points in your yard where water tends to pool. The discharge pipe should run to a dry well, storm drain, or daylight outlet at the property edge. Plan pipe routes before sod installation to avoid disturbing new turf later.

Raised beds, berms, and swales provide natural drainage solutions that complement traditional infrastructure. A shallow swale (a gently sloped depression 6 to 12 inches deep and 2 to 4 feet wide) can redirect surface water across a lawn without requiring underground pipes. Line the swale with sod for a natural appearance, or use river rock for a decorative dry creek bed effect. Swales are particularly effective on large properties where pipe runs would be prohibitively long.

Understanding Sod Quality Grades

Sod quality varies significantly between suppliers, and understanding grading standards helps you make informed purchasing decisions. The Turfgrass Producers International (TPI) recommends standards based on purity (percentage of desired grass species), density (plants per square inch), root development, soil thickness, and absence of weeds, disease, and insects. Premium-grade sod typically contains 95%+ purity of the labeled variety with fewer than 1 weed per 500 square feet, dense and uniform coverage, and a minimum root depth of 1.5 inches.

Certified sod from licensed producers carries a guarantee of genetic purity and adherence to state certification standards. Certified seed stock is inspected during production, and the harvested sod is sampled and tested before sale. While certified sod costs 10 to 20% more than uncertified alternatives, the investment pays off through more uniform appearance, better disease resistance, and fewer weed problems. Ask your supplier whether their sod is certified and by which state program.

Inspect sod upon delivery before accepting the shipment. Look for consistent green color (no yellowing, which indicates heat damage from stacking), uniform thickness (0.5 to 0.75 inches of soil), moist but not soggy roots, absence of visible weeds or weed seed heads, and no signs of insect activity. Pull gently on the grass blades. They should hold firmly in the soil, indicating healthy root attachment. Reject any pallets showing excessive yellowing, dry or crumbling soil, a sour or ammonia smell (indicating overheating), or significant weed contamination.

Sod thickness affects establishment speed and handling. Thinner-cut sod (0.5 inch of soil) is lighter and easier to handle but dries out faster and has less root mass. Thicker-cut sod (0.75 to 1 inch) retains moisture better and has more established roots but is heavier (50 to 70 pounds per roll vs 30 to 40 pounds for thin-cut). Big-roll sod (48 to 60 inches wide, installed with specialized equipment) provides fewer seams and faster coverage for large commercial projects, but requires machinery that makes it impractical for residential use.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much sod do I need for a 1,000 square foot lawn?

For 1,000 square feet with a 10% waste factor, you need 1,100 square feet of sod, which is 110 rolls or approximately 2.2 pallets. Most suppliers sell full pallets, so you would order 3 pallets and have some extra for patching and repairs.

Can I lay sod over existing grass?

This is strongly discouraged. Sod laid over existing vegetation rarely establishes properly because the old grass creates a barrier between the new roots and the soil. The decomposing layer also creates heat and fungal problems. Always remove old vegetation first.

How long does sod last on a pallet?

Sod on a pallet begins declining within 6 to 8 hours in warm weather and can last up to 24 hours in cool conditions. Interior rolls on a pallet heat up faster than exterior ones. Plan your installation so all sod is down the same day it arrives.

Does sod need to be rolled after installation?

Yes, rolling eliminates air pockets between the sod and soil, which is important for root-to-soil contact. Use a lawn roller filled one-third to one-half with water. Roll in two directions perpendicular to each other for complete coverage.

What is the best time of year to install sod?

The ideal time depends on your grass type. Cool-season grasses (Kentucky bluegrass, fescue, ryegrass) establish best in early fall (September to October) or early spring (March to April). Warm-season grasses (Bermuda, Zoysia, St. Augustine) establish best in late spring through early summer (May to June) when soil temperatures are consistently above 65 degrees Fahrenheit. Avoid installing any sod during extreme heat (above 95 degrees) or when frost is expected.

How many square feet of sod are on a pallet?

A standard pallet of sod covers 400 to 500 square feet depending on the supplier and region. In the Southeast US, pallets often hold 504 sq ft (50 yards of sod at 2 ft x 5 ft each). In the Midwest, 450 sq ft is typical. Always confirm pallet coverage with your local sod farm.

Q

What waste factor should I use when ordering sod?

For rectangular lawns, add 5% for waste. For irregular shapes with curves, flower beds, or walkways, add 10%. Complex fields with many obstacles may need 15% extra. Sod cannot be returned once delivered, so a small surplus is better than running short mid-project.

Q

Is it better to sod or seed a new lawn?

Sod provides instant results and prevents erosion on slopes, but costs 3-5x more than seed. Seeding is cheaper and offers more grass variety options, but takes 2-3 months to establish and is vulnerable to washout and weed competition during germination. For slopes and high-traffic areas, sod is usually the better choice.
