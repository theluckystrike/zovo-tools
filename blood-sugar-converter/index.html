Where Your Reading Falls

How the Conversion Works

The conversion between mg/dL and mmol/L is straightforward math. The molecular weight of glucose is 180.16 g/mol, and since 1 deciliter equals 0.1 liters, the conversion factor works out to 18.0156. In practice, most clinical sources round this to 18.018 or simply 18.

To convert from mg/dL to mmol/L, you divide by 18.018. To go the other direction, you multiply by 18.018. I've seen some converters round to 18, which introduces a small but unnecessary error. This tool uses the precise factor of 18.018 for accuracy to one decimal place, which matches the precision of most glucose meters.

The reason different countries use different units goes back to the history of clinical chemistry. The mg/dL convention was established in the US before the international community adopted SI units. There have been periodic calls to standardize on mmol/L globally, but the US hasn't made the switch. You can find extended discussion about measurement standardization on Stack Overflow's unit conversion threads.

Normal Blood Sugar Ranges

Understanding what constitutes a "normal" reading depends entirely on when the measurement was taken. The three most common contexts are fasting (at least 8 hours without food), post-meal (typically 2 hours after eating), and random (any time of day). When to Test Blood Sugar

Testing timing matters enormously. A fasting reading of 110 mg/dL and a post-meal reading of 110 mg/dL tell very different stories. The fasting value suggests prediabetes, while the post-meal value is well within normal range.

For most people monitoring their blood sugar, these are the key testing times and what they reveal:

Fasting (Morning)

This is the gold standard for diabetes screening. You should have gone at least 8 hours without eating. The fasting level reflects your baseline glucose regulation and how well your body manages blood sugar overnight. If you consistently see fasting values above 100 mg/dL, it is worth discussing with your doctor.

Post-Meal (2 Hours After Eating)

Post-prandial testing shows how your body handles a glucose load. In a healthy person, blood sugar rises after eating and returns to near-normal within 2 hours. Persistently elevated post-meal readings can indicate insulin resistance even when fasting values are still normal. This is often the earliest sign of metabolic issues.

Before Bed

Testing before bed helps you understand your glucose trajectory going into the overnight period. For people on insulin, this reading helps determine if a dose adjustment is needed to avoid overnight lows.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the conversion factor between mg/dL and mmol/L?
The conversion factor is 18.018 (or precisely, 180.156/10). To convert from mg/dL to mmol/L, divide by 18.018. To convert from mmol/L to mg/dL, multiply by 18.018. Some sources round this to 18, which is close enough for most practical purposes but introduces a small error at higher values.
Why does the US use mg/dL while other countries use mmol/L?
The US adopted the mg/dL convention before the international scientific community standardized on SI units (which include mmol/L). Switching would require recalibrating millions of glucose meters, reprinting all clinical reference materials, and retraining healthcare professionals. The cost and confusion of such a transition has kept the US on mg/dL.
What is a normal fasting blood sugar level?
A normal fasting blood sugar is below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) according to the American Diabetes Association. Values between 100 and 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L) indicate prediabetes. A fasting level of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher on two separate tests indicates diabetes.
Can a single high reading mean I have diabetes?
No. A diabetes diagnosis requires at least two separate fasting blood glucose tests showing 126 mg/dL or higher, or an A1C of 6.5 percent or above, or a random blood glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher with symptoms. A single elevated reading could be caused by stress, illness, medications, or recent food intake. However, if you get a high reading, it is definitely worth following up with your healthcare provider.
What does A1C measure and how does it relate to daily readings?
A1C measures the percentage of hemoglobin in your blood that has glucose attached to it. Since red blood cells live about 90 to 120 days, A1C reflects your average blood sugar over the past 2 to 3 months. An A1C of 5.7 percent roughly corresponds to an average glucose of 117 mg/dL (6.5 mmol/L).
Is this tool precise enough for medical decisions?
The mathematical conversion is precise. However, this tool should not replace clinical laboratory testing for medical decisions. Home glucose meters themselves have a margin of error of plus or minus 15 percent. The risk categorizations follow ADA guidelines but individual circumstances vary. Always consult a healthcare provider for diagnosis and treatment decisions.
What should I do if my reading is in the prediabetic range?
Prediabetes is a critical intervention window. Research shows that lifestyle changes, including modest weight loss (5 to 7 percent of body weight), regular physical activity (150 minutes per week), and dietary improvements, can reduce the risk of progressing to diabetes by 58 percent. Discuss your results with your doctor and consider joining a diabetes prevention program. The Hacker News community has discussed various approaches to glucose monitoring and management.

The Science of Blood Glucose Regulation

Understanding what your blood sugar numbers mean requires some knowledge of how glucose regulation works in the body. Glucose is the primary fuel source for cells throughout the body, and the brain in particular is almost entirely dependent on glucose for energy. The body maintains blood glucose within a relatively narrow range through a complex hormonal feedback system.

When you eat carbohydrates, they are broken down into glucose in the digestive tract and absorbed into the bloodstream. This triggers the pancreas to release insulin, a hormone that signals cells to take up glucose from the blood. Insulin essentially acts as a key that unlocks cell membranes to allow glucose entry. As cells absorb glucose, blood sugar levels drop back toward the fasting baseline.

In a healthy person, this system keeps fasting blood glucose between about 70 and 99 mg/dL (3.9 to 5.5 mmol/L). After a meal, blood sugar typically rises to 120 to 140 mg/dL (6.7 to 7.8 mmol/L) and returns to near-fasting levels within 2 to 3 hours. The system is remarkably precise, maintaining glucose within this range despite wide variations in food intake, physical activity, and stress levels.

Diabetes develops when this system breaks down. In Type 1 diabetes, the immune system destroys the insulin-producing beta cells of the pancreas, eliminating the body's ability to produce insulin. In Type 2 diabetes, cells become resistant to insulin's effects, requiring more and more insulin to achieve the same glucose uptake. Over time, the pancreas can't keep up with demand, and blood sugar levels rise chronically.

Prediabetes represents the intermediate stage where insulin resistance has begun but hasn't progressed to full diabetes. This is the critical intervention window. Research from the Diabetes Prevention Program showed that lifestyle modifications (modest weight loss plus regular exercise) reduced the risk of progressing from prediabetes to diabetes by 58 percent, which was more effective than the medication metformin (31 percent reduction).

The Role of Glucagon

While insulin gets most of the attention, its counterpart hormone glucagon is equally important. When blood sugar drops too low (below about 70 mg/dL or 3.9 mmol/L), the pancreas releases glucagon, which signals the liver to convert stored glycogen back into glucose and release it into the bloodstream. This is why fasting blood sugar doesn't drop to zero overnight. The liver continuously releases small amounts of glucose to maintain baseline levels.

In people with diabetes, both the insulin and glucagon systems can be impaired. Type 1 diabetics often have reduced glucagon responses, which increases their risk of severe hypoglycemia. Type 2 diabetics often have elevated glucagon levels even when blood sugar is high, which contributes to the hyperglycemia. Newer diabetes medications, like GLP-1 receptor agonists, work partly by reducing inappropriate glucagon secretion.

Dawn Phenomenon and Somogyi Effect

Two phenomena can cause unexpectedly high morning blood sugar readings. The dawn phenomenon occurs when the body releases cortisol and growth hormone in the early morning hours (typically 4 to 8 AM), which stimulate glucose release from the liver. This is a normal physiological process that affects everyone, but it can cause fasting blood sugar to be higher than bedtime readings in people with diabetes.

The Somogyi effect (rebound hyperglycemia) occurs when blood sugar drops too low during the night (often due to too much insulin), triggering a counter-regulatory hormone response that overshoots and causes high morning readings. Distinguishing between these two causes requires checking blood sugar at 2 to 3 AM. If the overnight reading is low, it is likely the Somogyi effect; if it is normal or high, it is the dawn phenomenon.

Understanding Glucose Meter Accuracy

If you are using this converter with readings from a home glucose meter, it is important to understand the limitations of those devices. The FDA requires that home glucose meters be precise within plus or minus 15 percent of a laboratory reference for 95 percent of readings at glucose levels at or above 75 mg/dL. For readings below 75 mg/dL, the allowed deviation is plus or minus 15 mg/dL.

This means a meter reading of 100 mg/dL could represent an actual blood glucose anywhere from 85 to 115 mg/dL. This is good enough for day-to-day diabetes management, but it means you shouldn't obsess over small differences between readings. A reading of 102 mg/dL versus 98 mg/dL is well within the meter's margin of error and shouldn't be interpreted as a meaningful change.

Several factors affect meter accuracy beyond the inherent measurement tolerance. Test strip storage matters; strips exposed to heat, humidity, or expired strips give unreliable results. The testing site matters; fingertip readings are generally more precise and responsive than alternative site testing (palm, forearm). Hand washing is critical; even trace amounts of food residue on the finger can significantly inflate readings. I've seen cases where someone tested after handling fruit and got a reading 50 mg/dL higher than their actual blood sugar.

Continuous glucose monitors (CGMs) like those from Dexus and Abbott measure interstitial fluid glucose, not blood glucose directly. There is a 5 to 15 minute lag between blood glucose changes and interstitial fluid changes. CGM readings are calibrated against fingerstick readings but can diverge during rapid glucose changes (after meals, during exercise). The conversion between mg/dL and mmol/L applies equally to CGM readings.

International Blood Sugar Standards

While this tool converts between the two most common blood sugar units, it is worth knowing which countries use which system. The United States, Japan, and a few other countries use mg/dL as their primary unit. Most of Europe, Australia, Canada, and the rest of the world use mmol/L. Some countries use both, depending on the clinical context or the era when their medical standards were established.

The diagnostic thresholds are the same regardless of which unit system is used, just expressed differently. The ADA fasting glucose cutoff of 126 mg/dL equals the WHO cutoff of 7.0 mmol/L. The difference in the last decimal place (126 / 18.018 = 6.993, which rounds to 7.0) is why these thresholds align so cleanly. The organizations coordinated on the thresholds and then expressed them in their respective units.

If you are traveling internationally and need to communicate blood sugar readings to healthcare providers, this converter becomes important. I've heard from travelers with diabetes who had emergency room visits abroad where the doctors were working in a different unit system. Having a quick conversion reference can literally be a lifesaver in those situations.

For researchers and developers working with glucose data from international studies, unit conversion errors are a common source of mistakes. Always verify which unit system a dataset uses before performing analysis. A study reporting a mean fasting glucose of 5.5 is using mmol/L (normal range); if you mistakenly interpret it as mg/dL, you would conclude the subjects have dangerously low blood sugar.

How Diet Affects Blood Sugar Readings

The food you eat has a direct and significant impact on blood sugar levels, but the relationship is more complex than simply "sugar makes blood sugar go up." The glycemic index (GI) measures how quickly a food raises blood sugar on a scale of 0 to 100, where pure glucose is 100. Foods with a high GI (above 70) cause rapid blood sugar spikes, while low GI foods (below 55) produce a more gradual rise.

However, the glycemic index doesn't tell the whole story. The glycemic load (GL) accounts for both the GI and the amount of carbohydrate in a typical serving. Watermelon has a high GI of 76 but a low GL of 4 because a typical serving contains relatively little carbohydrate. Conversely, white rice has a moderate GI but a high GL because servings tend to be large.

Protein and fat slow down carbohydrate absorption, which is why a mixed meal produces a different blood sugar response than the same carbohydrates eaten alone. Eating a piece of bread by itself will spike blood sugar faster than eating that same bread with butter and cheese. This is the basis of dietary advice to pair carbohydrates with protein and healthy fats.

Fiber is another important factor. Soluble fiber forms a gel-like substance in the digestive tract that slows glucose absorption. Whole grains, legumes, and vegetables contain significant fiber, which is why they have lower glycemic responses than their refined counterparts. The difference between white bread (GI 75) and whole grain bread (GI 51) is largely due to fiber content.

For people monitoring blood sugar, meal timing consistency matters more than most people realize. Eating meals at roughly the same time each day helps the body maintain a regular insulin response pattern. Skipping meals and then eating a large meal can cause larger blood sugar swings than eating smaller, more frequent meals at consistent times.

Exercise and Blood Sugar

Physical activity has both immediate and long-term effects on blood sugar. During exercise, muscles take up glucose from the bloodstream without needing insulin (through a separate glucose transporter called GLUT4). This can cause blood sugar to drop during and after exercise. For people with diabetes on insulin or certain oral medications, this creates a risk of exercise-induced hypoglycemia.

Paradoxically, very intense exercise (like sprinting or heavy weightlifting) can temporarily raise blood sugar. This happens because the stress hormones adrenaline and cortisol trigger the liver to release stored glucose, and the release can exceed the rate at which muscles are taking up glucose. This is why someone might see a blood sugar reading of 180 mg/dL (10.0 mmol/L) immediately after an intense workout, even though the overall effect of exercise is blood sugar lowering.

Regular exercise improves insulin sensitivity, meaning your cells respond better to insulin and need less of it to maintain normal blood sugar. This effect persists for 24 to 48 hours after a single exercise session and accumulates over weeks and months of consistent activity. This is one reason why exercise is so effective at preventing and managing Type 2 diabetes.
