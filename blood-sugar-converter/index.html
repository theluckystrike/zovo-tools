How the Conversion Works

The conversion between mg/dL and mmol/L is straightforward math. The molecular weight of glucose is 180.16 g/mol, and since 1 deciliter equals 0.1 liters, the conversion factor works out to 18.0156. In practice, most clinical sources round this to 18.018 or simply 18.

To convert from mg/dL to mmol/L, you divide by 18.018. To go the other direction, you multiply by 18.018. I've seen some converters round to 18, which introduces a small but unnecessary error. This tool uses the precise factor of 18.018 for accuracy to one decimal place, which matches the precision of most glucose meters.

The reason different countries use different units goes back to the history of clinical chemistry. The mg/dL convention was established in the US before the international community adopted SI units. There have been periodic calls to standardize on mmol/L globally, but the US hasn't made the switch. You can find extended discussion about measurement standardization on Stack Overflow's unit conversion threads.

Normal Blood Sugar Ranges

Understanding what constitutes a "normal" reading depends entirely on when the measurement was taken. The three most common contexts are fasting (at least 8 hours without food), post-meal (typically 2 hours after eating), and random (any time of day). Here are the clinical reference ranges used by the American Diabetes Association:

These thresholds aren't arbitrary. They were established through large-scale epidemiological studies that identified the blood glucose levels at which the risk of diabetic complications increases significantly. The fasting cutoff of 126 mg/dL, for example, corresponds to the point where retinopathy risk begins to rise sharply.

When to Test Blood Sugar

Testing timing matters enormously. A fasting reading of 110 mg/dL and a post-meal reading of 110 mg/dL tell very different stories. The fasting value suggests prediabetes, while the post-meal value is well within normal range.

For most people monitoring their blood sugar, these are the key testing times and what they reveal:

Fasting (Morning)

This is the gold standard for diabetes screening. You should have gone at least 8 hours without eating. The fasting level reflects your baseline glucose regulation and how well your body manages blood sugar overnight. If you consistently see fasting values above 100 mg/dL, it is worth discussing with your doctor.

Post-Meal (2 Hours After Eating)

Post-prandial testing shows how your body handles a glucose load. In a healthy person, blood sugar rises after eating and returns to near-normal within 2 hours. Persistently elevated post-meal readings can indicate insulin resistance even when fasting values are still normal. This is often the earliest sign of metabolic issues.

Before Bed

Testing before bed helps you understand your glucose trajectory going into the overnight period. For people on insulin, this reading helps determine if a dose adjustment is needed to avoid overnight lows.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the conversion factor between mg/dL and mmol/L?
The conversion factor is 18.018 (or precisely, 180.156/10). To convert from mg/dL to mmol/L, divide by 18.018. To convert from mmol/L to mg/dL, multiply by 18.018. Some sources round this to 18, which is close enough for most practical purposes but introduces a small error at higher values.
Why does the US use mg/dL while other countries use mmol/L?
The US adopted the mg/dL convention before the international scientific community standardized on SI units (which include mmol/L). Switching would require recalibrating millions of glucose meters, reprinting all clinical reference materials, and retraining healthcare professionals. The cost and confusion of such a transition has kept the US on mg/dL.
What is a normal fasting blood sugar level?
A normal fasting blood sugar is below 100 mg/dL (5.6 mmol/L) according to the American Diabetes Association. Values between 100 and 125 mg/dL (5.6 to 6.9 mmol/L) indicate prediabetes. A fasting level of 126 mg/dL (7.0 mmol/L) or higher on two separate tests indicates diabetes.
Can a single high reading mean I have diabetes?
No. A diabetes diagnosis requires at least two separate fasting blood glucose tests showing 126 mg/dL or higher, or an A1C of 6.5 percent or above, or a random blood glucose of 200 mg/dL or higher with symptoms. A single elevated reading could be caused by stress, illness, medications, or recent food intake. However, if you get a high reading, it is definitely worth following up with your healthcare provider.
What does A1C measure and how does it relate to daily readings?
A1C measures the percentage of hemoglobin in your blood that has glucose attached to it. Since red blood cells live about 90 to 120 days, A1C reflects your average blood sugar over the past 2 to 3 months. An A1C of 5.7 percent roughly corresponds to an average glucose of 117 mg/dL (6.5 mmol/L).
Is this tool accurate enough for medical decisions?
The mathematical conversion is precise. However, this tool should not replace clinical laboratory testing for medical decisions. Home glucose meters themselves have a margin of error of plus or minus 15 percent. The risk categorizations follow ADA guidelines but individual circumstances vary. Always consult a healthcare provider for diagnosis and treatment decisions.
What should I do if my reading is in the prediabetic range?
Prediabetes is a critical intervention window. Research shows that lifestyle changes, including modest weight loss (5 to 7 percent of body weight), regular physical activity (150 minutes per week), and dietary improvements, can reduce the risk of progressing to diabetes by 58 percent. Discuss your results with your doctor and consider joining a diabetes prevention program. The Hacker News community has discussed various approaches to glucose monitoring and management.
