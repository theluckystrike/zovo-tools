How Pennsylvania's Flat Tax Actually Works

Pennsylvania's 3.07% flat income tax applies to eight classes of income: compensation, interest, dividends, net business income, net rental income, net gains from property disposition, income from estates and trusts, and gambling/lottery winnings. For most W-2 employees, only "compensation" matters - that's your wages, salaries, tips, and bonuses.

Here's the critical detail that distinguishes PA from other flat-tax states: Pennsylvania does NOT have a standard deduction. There is no personal exemption. The 3.07% rate applies to virtually all of your earned income. Compare this to North Carolina, which has a flat 4.5% rate but offers a $12,750 standard deduction - NC's effective rate on a $75,000 salary is actually lower than 4.5% because of that deduction. PA's 3.07% is the true effective rate on nearly every dollar.

The math for a single PA filer earning $75,000 with 6% 401(k) and $200/month health insurance:

  1. Start with gross compensation: $75,000
  2. Pre-tax deductions (PA allows 401k reduction): $75,000 - $4,500 = $70,500
  3. PA taxable compensation: $70,500 (no standard deduction available)
  4. $70,500 x 3.07% = $2,164.35
  5. Local EIT (assuming 1% suburban rate): $70,500 x 1.0% = $705.00
  6. PA employee unemployment: $75,000 x 0.13% = $97.50
  7. $52.00
  8. Total PA state/local taxes: $3,018.85

For a Philadelphia resident, replace the 1% local EIT with the 3.75% wage tax, and the total PA state/local jumps to $4,802.10 - an increase of nearly $1,800 just from living in Philly. This is why municipality selection matters so much in this calculator.

Philadelphia Wage Tax The Tax Within a Tax

Philadelphia's wage tax deserves its own section because it's one of the highest local income taxes in the entire country. At 3.75% for residents and 3.44% for non-residents, it exceeds the state income tax rate itself. Combined with the 3.07% state tax, Philadelphia residents pay a staggering 6.82% combined income tax rate before federal taxes are even considered.

The Philadelphia wage tax has been gradually decreasing - it was 3.8398% as recently as 2020 and has come down to 3.75% for 2025 (the non-resident rate dropped from 3.4481% to 3.44%). The city has a stated goal of continuing reductions, but progress has been slow. For comparison, the next-highest city wage taxes in the state are in Reading (3.6%) and Scranton (3.4%).

Here's the annual Philadelphia tax impact on various salary levels:

  • $50,000 salary: $3,410 combined PA + Philly tax (6.82%), plus FICA = effective total rate of ~31%
  • $75,000 salary: $5,115 combined PA + Philly tax, plus FICA = effective total rate of ~31%
  • $100,000 salary: $6,820 combined PA + Philly tax, plus FICA = effective total rate of ~32%
  • $150,000 salary: $10,230 combined PA + Philly tax, plus FICA = effective total rate of ~33%

For Philadelphia workers considering moving to the suburbs, the savings are substantial. Moving from Center City Philadelphia (3.75% wage tax) to King of Prussia in Montgomery County (1.0% local EIT) saves a $75,000 earner approximately $2,063 per year in local taxes alone. That doesn't include the non-resident wage tax credit - if you work in Philadelphia but live outside, you'll still owe the 3.44% non-resident wage tax on Philly-sourced income.

Pittsburgh's Local Tax Structure

Pittsburgh's local tax structure is different from Philadelphia's but still significant. Pittsburgh residents pay a combined 3.0% local earned income tax (1.0% to the city, 2.0% to the Pittsburgh School District). Unlike Philadelphia's wage tax system, Pittsburgh uses the standard PA EIT framework administered by Jordan Tax Service.

, Pittsburgh levies a Local Services Tax (LST) of $52 per year on all workers earning over $12,000. This is a flat dollar amount divided across pay periods, not a percentage. Non-residents working in Pittsburgh may also owe Pittsburgh EIT, but they can credit taxes paid to their home municipality, and vice versa.

The Pittsburgh metro area outside the city proper generally has lower local EIT rates - many Allegheny County suburbs have combined rates of 1.0%-2.0%. Moving from the city to a suburb like Cranberry Township or Mt. Lebanon can save $750-$1,500 per year in local taxes on a $75,000 salary.

Understanding PA's SUI/SUTA System

Pennsylvania's State Unemployment Insurance (SUI/SUTA) system has both employer and employee components. While most of the cost falls on employers (rates vary based on the employer's experience rating, ranging from 1.2905% to 9.9333% on the first $10,000 of wages per employee), employees also contribute:

  • UC (Unemployment Compensation): 0.06% of gross wages - employee contribution to the UC fund
  • Additional UC Contribution: 0.07% of gross wages - supplemental employee contribution
  • Total employee SUI: 0.13% of gross wages

On a $75,000 salary, the employee SUI contribution is $97.50 annually - a small amount, but it does appear on your pay stub and reduces your take-home pay. This calculator includes these deductions for accuracy. Most other PA salary calculators I tested omit the employee SUI portion.

PA's Retirement Income Exemption The Silver Lining

Despite PA's complex tax structure for active workers, the state offers one of the most generous retirement income exemptions in the country. Pennsylvania does NOT tax any of the following:

  • Social Security benefits
  • 401(k) and IRA distributions (once you reach eligible retirement age)
  • Pension income (public and private)
  • Railroad retirement benefits

This makes PA one of the most tax-friendly states for retirees, despite being moderately complex for working-age adults. A retiree receiving $60,000 per year in pension and Social Security pays $0 in PA state tax - compared to $2,700 in NC (4.5% minus Social Security exemption) or over $3,000 in Virginia. If you're planning for retirement, PA's exemption is a major factor that I've found most people undervalue.

The Local EIT System How It Actually Works

Pennsylvania's local earned income tax (EIT) system is governed by Act 32, which consolidated local tax collection starting in 2012. Here's how the system works in practice:

Every municipality (borough, township, or city) sets a municipal EIT rate. Every school district sets a school district EIT rate. Your combined local EIT rate is the sum of both. These rates are set independently by local governing bodies and can change annually, though most remain stable year to year.

When you work in a different municipality than where you live, things get complicated. PA uses a "nonresident credit" system. You owe your resident municipality's EIT rate, but you get a credit for taxes paid to your work municipality, up to the lesser of the two rates. In practice, this means you always pay at least your home municipality's rate. If your work location's rate is higher, you also owe the difference to the work location.

This system creates interesting tax arbitrage opportunities. If you work in Philadelphia (3.75% wage tax) and live in a suburban municipality with a 1% EIT, you'll owe the full 3.44% Philadelphia non-resident wage tax plus your home 1% EIT minus credit. The net result is more complex than most people realize. I this calculator to handle the most common scenarios, but if you have an unusual work/home municipality combination, you may verify with your local tax collector.

PA Property Tax Context and Total Tax Burden

Understanding PA income taxes in isolation doesn't tell the complete story. Pennsylvania has some of the highest property taxes in the country, with an average effective rate of approximately 1.58% - well above the national average of 1.07%. In some counties (like Monroe and Luzerne), effective rates exceed 2%. This means a $300,000 home in PA costs about $4,740 in annual property taxes, compared to a national average of $3,210.

When I calculate total tax burden - income tax (state + local) plus property tax plus sales tax - Pennsylvania ranks as a moderately high-tax state overall, despite its low 3.07% state income tax rate. The combination of local EIT, high property taxes, and a 6% state sales tax pushes PA's total burden higher than the state income tax rate alone would suggest. This is one of those findings from our testing that I think is really important for people comparing job offers across states.

PA for Remote Workers Tax Implications

Remote work has created interesting tax dynamics for Pennsylvania workers. If you live in PA and work remotely for an out-of-state employer, you generally owe PA state tax (3.07%) and your local EIT on all compensation. Your employer may or may not withhold PA taxes - if they don't, you're responsible for estimated payments.

If you live in PA and occasionally travel to another state for work, reciprocal tax agreements matter. PA has reciprocity agreements with Indiana, Maryland, New Jersey, Ohio, Virginia, and West Virginia. Under these agreements, you only owe income tax to your state of residence, not the state where you work. This simplifies things significantly for border-area workers.

New York does NOT have a reciprocity agreement with PA, which creates potential double taxation for PA residents working in New York. New York may try to tax your income under the "convenience of the employer" rule. This is an active area of tax law dispute. If you're in this situation, I'd strongly recommend consulting a tax professional.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Pennsylvania state income tax rate for 2025?
Pennsylvania has a flat state income tax rate of 3.07% for 2025. This is one of the lowest state income tax rates in the country. Unlike most states, PA does not offer a standard deduction or personal exemptions, so the 3.07% effectively applies to all earned income. The rate has remained at 3.07% for many years and there are no scheduled changes.
What is the Philadelphia wage tax and how does it work?
The Philadelphia wage tax is 3.75% for residents and 3.44% for non-residents who work in Philadelphia, as of 2025. This is levied on all gross wages, salaries, and self-employment income. It's collected by the Philadelphia Revenue Department and is separate from the PA 3.07% state tax. Combined, Philadelphia residents pay 6.82% in state + local income tax. The wage tax has been gradually declining from about 3.84% in 2020.
Does Pennsylvania have local income taxes?
Yes, Pennsylvania has one of the most complex local income tax systems in the nation. Nearly every municipality and school district levies a local earned income tax (EIT). Combined rates typically range from 1.0% in suburban areas to 3.6% in cities like Reading and Scranton. Philadelphia uses a separate wage tax system. The local EIT is to the 3.07% state rate. You can look up your specific rate on the DCED's EIT rate finder website.
How much is the Pittsburgh local income tax?
Pittsburgh residents pay a combined local EIT of 3.0% - split as 1.0% municipal tax and 2.0% Pittsburgh School District tax. Combined with the PA state 3.07%, Pittsburgh residents pay 6.07% in state/local income tax. There's also a $52 annual Local Services Tax (LST) for workers earning over $12,000. Non-residents working in Pittsburgh may owe part of this depending on their home municipality's rate and credit arrangements.
Does Pennsylvania tax retirement income?
No, Pennsylvania does not tax retirement income. This includes 401(k) and IRA withdrawals, pension income, Social Security benefits, and railroad retirement. This makes PA one of the most tax-friendly states for retirees despite its complexity for working adults. The retirement income exemption applies once you reach eligible retirement age for the specific plan. This is a significant advantage over most other states in the region.
What is the PA SUI/SUTA employee contribution?
Pennsylvania employees pay a small unemployment insurance contribution of 0.06% (UC) plus 0.07% (Additional UC) of gross wages, totaling 0.13%. On a $75,000 salary, this amounts to about $97.50 annually. These deductions appear on your pay stub as UC/SUI contributions. The employer pays a much larger share of unemployment insurance based on their experience rating, but the employee portion is a mandatory payroll deduction.
Does PA have reciprocal tax agreements with other states?
Yes, Pennsylvania has reciprocal tax agreements with Indiana, Maryland, New Jersey, Ohio, Virginia, and West Virginia. Under these agreements, if you live in PA and work in a reciprocal state (or vice versa), you only owe income tax to your state of residence. This prevents double taxation for border-area commuters. Notably, PA does NOT have reciprocity with New York or Delaware, which can create complex situations for workers in those border areas.

Understanding Pennsylvania's Tax Structure

Pennsylvania uses a flat income tax rate of 3.07% on all taxable compensation, making it one of only eight U.S. states with a flat personal income tax. Unlike progressive-tax states where higher earners pay a larger percentage, every Pennsylvania resident pays the same rate regardless of income level. This simplifies calculation but means lower-income households bear a proportionally higher overall tax burden when considering sales and property taxes.

What makes Pennsylvania uniquely complex is its system of local earned income taxes (EIT). Nearly every municipality and school district levies its own EIT, typically ranging from 1% to 2% combined, though rates can reach 3.5% in some areas. Philadelphia stands apart with a wage tax of 3.75% for residents (3.44% for non-residents who work in the city), one of the highest local income taxes in the nation. Pittsburgh residents pay a combined local rate of approximately 3%, including the city's earned income tax and the local services tax. These local taxes are collected by regional tax officers and are separate from your state return, so workers who live in one municipality and commute to another may need to file with multiple jurisdictions and claim credits for taxes paid to their work location.
