How Car Depreciation Works

Depreciation is the decline in a vehicle's market value over time. It isn't linear. New cars depreciate fastest in the first year, losing an average of 20 to 25 percent of their purchase price. By year three, most vehicles have lost about 40 to 45 percent of their original value. After five years, the average car retains only about 40 percent of its new price.

The mathematics behind this calculator use a declining balance method, where each year's depreciation is a percentage of the remaining value rather than the original price. This models real-world market behavior more accurately than straight-line depreciation. I've calibrated the rates using data from automotive valuation services and established depreciation principles documented on Wikipedia.

Year one typically sees the steepest drop because a "new" car becomes "used" the moment it is registered. That new-to-used transition accounts for a significant portion of the first-year loss. After that, the rate of depreciation gradually slows as the vehicle approaches its floor value, the point below which market demand and scrap value support the price.

The Depreciation Curve Explained

The classic depreciation curve follows an exponential decay pattern. This tool models depreciation using vehicle-type-specific annual rates that decrease over time. A luxury vehicle might lose 30 percent in year one but only 8 percent in year ten. A truck, which typically holds value better, might lose 15 percent in year one and 6 percent in year ten.

Notice how trucks and SUVs hold their value better than luxury vehicles and sports cars. This is partly driven by demand dynamics. Trucks have strong utility value and a robust used market, while luxury vehicles suffer from high maintenance costs and rapid technology obsolescence that discourage used buyers.

Factors Affecting Depreciation Rate

Several factors influence how quickly your specific vehicle depreciates. Understanding these can help you make smarter buying and selling decisions.

Mileage

Mileage is one of the strongest predictors of a vehicle's value. The industry standard is about 12,000 to 15,000 miles per year. Vehicles significantly above this average depreciate faster because higher mileage implies more wear on the engine, transmission, and chassis components. This calculator adjusts the depreciation rate based on your annual mileage input. Developers interested in mileage-based valuation algorithms can find related discussions on Stack Overflow.

Vehicle Type

As shown in the chart above, vehicle type dramatically affects depreciation. Trucks and SUVs generally hold value best due to high demand and utility. Luxury vehicles depreciate fastest because their appeal is tied to having the latest features and technology. Electric vehicles have been a mixed bag; early models depreciated very quickly due to battery concerns and rapid technology improvements, but newer EVs with longer range and better battery warranties are holding value better.

Condition

A well-maintained vehicle can be worth 10 to 20 percent more than a comparable vehicle in poor condition. Regular maintenance records, no accident history, and clean interior/exterior all contribute to higher resale value. This calculator includes a condition adjustment that modifies the base depreciation rate.

Brand and Model

Some brands depreciate much less than others. Historically, Toyota and Honda have the best value retention, while certain European luxury brands lose value quickly. This tool uses broad category averages rather than brand-specific data, which makes it a general estimation tool rather than a precise valuation service.

Best Time to Sell Analysis

I've found that the optimal selling window for most vehicles is between years 3 and 5. Here is the reasoning: the steepest depreciation happens in years 1 through 3, meaning you have already absorbed the biggest losses. After year 5, maintenance costs start increasing significantly, and the cost of ownership per mile begins to rise.

The sweet spot depends on your vehicle type. For trucks and SUVs that hold value well, you can wait longer, perhaps years 5 through 7. For luxury vehicles that depreciate rapidly, selling at year 3 before the warranty expires is often the best financial move. The warranty factor is significant because buyers will pay more for a vehicle still under manufacturer warranty.

This concept has been discussed extensively in personal finance communities, including this Hacker News thread on car ownership economics. The general consensus is that buying a 2 to 3 year old vehicle and selling it at 6 to 7 years is the most cost-effective approach if you are optimizing for total ownership cost.

Understanding Cost Per Mile

Cost per mile from depreciation alone gives you a clearer picture of what vehicle ownership truly costs. If you paid $35,000 for a car and sell it five years later for $15,000, your depreciation cost is $20,000 over those five years. At 12,000 miles per year, that is 60,000 total miles, making your depreciation cost roughly $0.33 per mile.

This figure doesn't include fuel, insurance, maintenance, or financing costs. The total cost of ownership per mile is typically 2 to 3 times the depreciation cost alone. The IRS standard mileage rate of $0.67 per mile (2024) is a reasonable estimate of total cost, and depreciation accounts for roughly half of that.

Understanding cost per mile helps you compare vehicles objectively. A more expensive vehicle that holds value well might have a lower cost per mile than a cheaper vehicle that depreciates rapidly. This is why trucks, despite higher purchase prices, can sometimes be cheaper to own over time than economy sedans when measured on a per-mile depreciation basis.

Our Testing Methodology

I've calibrated this calculator using original research that cross-references depreciation rates from multiple automotive valuation sources. The annual depreciation percentages used in this tool are derived from averaging data points across vehicle categories. The model uses year-specific rates for the first five years and then transitions to a slower steady-state rate for years six through fifteen.

Edge cases tested include: very old vehicles (1990 models), brand new vehicles (2026), extremely high mileage (300,000+ miles), and all six vehicle type categories. The mileage adjustment was validated against published per-mile depreciation factors. Condition adjustments follow the four-tier system (excellent/good/fair/poor) used by major valuation services.

For transparency, this is a general estimation tool. For an actual vehicle valuation, services like Kelley Blue Book or specialized npm packages for automotive calculations would provide make/model-specific data. This tool gives you a solid ballpark based on vehicle category averages.

Frequently Asked Questions

How much does a new car depreciate in the first year?
On average, a new car loses 20 to 25 percent of its value in the first year. This varies significantly by vehicle type. Trucks might lose only 15 percent, while luxury vehicles can lose 30 percent or more. The first-year drop is the largest single-year depreciation hit you will take as a car owner.
Do electric vehicles depreciate faster than gas cars?
Historically yes, but the gap is narrowing. Early EVs depreciated rapidly due to battery degradation concerns and fast-improving technology. Newer EVs with longer range and better battery warranties are holding value much better. Tesla models in particular have shown stronger value retention than the EV average, though this fluctuates with pricing changes.
What vehicles hold their value the best?
Trucks (especially full-size pickups like the Toyota Tacoma and Ford F-150) and certain SUVs consistently top value retention rankings. The Toyota 4Runner, Jeep Wrangler, and Porsche 911 are also known for exceptional resale value. In general, vehicles with strong utility value, loyal followings, and limited supply hold value best.
How does mileage affect car value?
As a rough guideline, every 10,000 miles above the average annual mileage (12,000 to 15,000) reduces the car's value by approximately 1 to 2 percent relative to a comparable lower-mileage example. Very high mileage (over 100,000 miles) can trigger a more significant drop because buyers anticipate major maintenance needs like timing belt/chain replacement and transmission service.
When is the best time to sell a car?
For most vehicle types, the optimal selling window is between years 3 and 5. By this point, you have absorbed the steepest depreciation, and the vehicle still has enough life left (and possibly remaining warranty) to command a decent resale price. Selling before major maintenance milestones (60K, 100K miles) can also help maximize value.
Is straight-line depreciation or declining balance more accurate for cars?
Declining balance (also called reducing balance) is more accurate for vehicles because cars don't lose a fixed dollar amount each year. They lose a percentage of their current value, which naturally decreases the dollar amount of depreciation over time. This tool uses a declining balance method with year-specific rates that reflect the steeper early depreciation.
Does color affect a car's depreciation?
Surprisingly, yes. Studies have shown that mainstream colors (white, black, gray, silver) tend to depreciate less because they appeal to a broader buyer pool. Unusual colors (orange, purple, bright green) can depreciate faster in the general market, though they can sometimes command a premium in specific enthusiast communities.
