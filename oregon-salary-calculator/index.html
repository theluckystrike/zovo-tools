How to Use This Tool

This Oregon salary calculator gives you a clear picture of your actual take-home pay after all mandatory deductions. Here is how to get the most precise results:

  1. Enter your annual gross salary in the first field. This is your total compensation before any taxes or deductions. If you know only your hourly rate, multiply it by 2,080 for a standard full-time year (40 hours per week times 52 weeks).
  2. Choose your pay frequency from the dropdown. Biweekly (26 pay periods) is the most common schedule in Oregon, but weekly, semi-monthly, and monthly options are also available. The annual tax amounts remain the same regardless of frequency.
  3. Select your filing status as it appears on your W-4 form. This affects both your federal tax brackets and your Oregon state tax brackets, as the state uses different bracket thresholds for each filing status.
  4. Add any extra withholding if you have requested additional federal tax withheld per pay period on your W-4. Leave at zero if you have no extra withholding.
  5. Include pre-tax deductions such as 401(k) contributions, HSA deposits, or health insurance premiums. These reduce your taxable income for both federal and Oregon state tax calculations.
  6. Press Calculate to see your full paycheck breakdown, including per-period amounts, annual totals, effective tax rate, and a visual chart showing where each dollar goes.

Oregon's progressive tax structure means your effective state tax rate varies based on income. The calculator applies all four state brackets correctly, along with Oregon's separate standard deduction. Remember that Oregon has no sales tax, so your income tax burden is partially offset by savings on everyday purchases.

Oregon has its own standard deduction that is much lower than the federal standard deduction. For 2024, the Oregon standard deduction is approximately $2,745 for single and head of household filers, and $5,495 for married filing jointly. Oregon does not allow you to claim the federal standard deduction on your state return. Instead, you use Oregon's own deduction amounts.

Oregon also does not tax Social Security benefits, which can be a significant advantage for retirees. However, other forms of retirement income (pensions, 401(k) distributions, IRA withdrawals) are fully taxable at Oregon's progressive rates. The Oregon Department of Revenue publishes updated brackets and standard deduction amounts each year in Publication OR-17.

One important distinction: Oregon's taxable income starts with your federal adjusted gross income (AGI) and then applies Oregon-specific additions, subtractions, and the Oregon standard deduction (or Oregon itemized deductions). This means your Oregon taxable income can differ substantially from your federal taxable income due to the different standard deduction amounts.

Statewide Transit Tax

Oregon's Statewide Transit Tax (STT) is a payroll tax of 0.1% (one-tenth of one percent) on all wages earned in Oregon. Enacted by the Oregon Legislature in 2018 through House Bill 2017, this tax funds public transportation improvements across the state.

Key details about the Statewide Transit Tax:

  • Rate: 0.1% of gross wages, with no wage cap
  • Who pays: All employees who perform services in Oregon, regardless of where they live
  • Withholding: Employers are required to withhold the tax from each paycheck
  • Self-employed: Self-employed individuals earning $1,000 or more must also pay the tax
  • No employer match: Unlike FICA, the transit tax is entirely an employee obligation. Employers withhold it but do not contribute a matching amount

On a $65,000 salary, the Statewide Transit Tax amounts to $65 per year, or about $2.50 per biweekly paycheck. While small individually, this tax generates hundreds of millions of dollars statewide for transit improvements including bus service, light rail, and pedestrian and cycling infrastructure.

The STT is reported on Oregon Form OQ (Quarterly Combined Tax Report) and is separate from the Oregon income tax withholding. Employees see it as a distinct line item on their pay stubs. It applies to all wages with no minimum threshold and no maximum cap, making it a simple flat tax on all Oregon-source wage income.

Portland-area workers may also be subject to the TriMet Transit District excise tax, which is an employer-paid tax and does not directly reduce employee paychecks. Also, the Lane Transit District (Eugene-Springfield area) imposes a similar employer-paid transit tax.

Your employer matches the 6.2% Social Security and 1.45% Medicare contributions but does not match the Additional Medicare Tax. For someone earning $65,000 per year in Oregon, FICA totals $4,030 for Social Security and $942.50 for Medicare, equaling $4,972.50 annually.

Combined with Oregon's progressive state tax (roughly 7-8% effective for a $65,000 earner), the Statewide Transit Tax at 0.1%, and federal income tax, the total deduction rate for most Oregon workers falls between 25% and 38%. Oregon's lack of sales tax partially offsets this higher income tax burden, but the take-home pay impact is still significant compared to states with lower or no income tax.

The Social Security wage base for 2024 is $168,600. Wages above that amount are exempt from the 6.2% Social Security tax but remain subject to Medicare at 1.45%. The Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% kicks in at $200,000 for single filers and $250,000 for married filing jointly, adding to the total FICA obligation for higher earners.

Common Deductions Guide

Understanding the deductions on your Oregon paycheck helps you plan effectively. Here is what to expect:

Mandatory Deductions

  • Federal Income Tax: Withheld based on your W-4 form and the IRS withholding tables. Typically the largest deduction for higher earners, though Oregon state tax can rival it for moderate incomes.
  • Oregon State Income Tax: Progressive rates from 4.75% to 9.9%. Oregon uses its own withholding tables (Form OW-4) and its own standard deduction ($2,745 for single filers).
  • Social Security Tax: 6.2% of gross wages up to $168,600. Funds your future Social Security retirement benefits.
  • Medicare Tax: 1.45% of all gross wages with no cap. Additional 0.9% on earnings over $200,000 (single).
  • Statewide Transit Tax: 0.1% of all wages. A flat-rate tax with no cap or exemption threshold.

Common Pre-Tax Deductions

  • 401(k) / 403(b) contributions: Reduce both federal and Oregon state taxable income. The 2024 contribution limit is $23,000 ($30,500 if age 50 or older).
  • Health insurance premiums: Employer-sponsored plan contributions are typically pre-tax, reducing taxable income for all purposes.
  • HSA contributions: Pre-tax through payroll. Oregon follows federal HSA treatment. The 2024 limits are $4,150 individual and $8,300 family.
  • FSA contributions: adaptable Spending Accounts reduce taxable income. The 2024 medical FSA limit is $3,200.
  • Oregon Saves: If your employer does not offer a retirement plan, Oregon Saves is the state-facilitated Roth IRA program. Contributions are after-tax (Roth) by default.

Post-Tax Deductions

  • Roth 401(k) / Roth IRA contributions: After-tax contributions that grow and are withdrawn tax-free in retirement.
  • Wage garnishments: Court-ordered deductions for child support, alimony, student loans, or other debts.
  • Union dues: Oregon has a significant union presence, particularly in education, healthcare, and public sector jobs.
  • Voluntary benefits: Supplemental insurance, legal plans, and other optional coverage.

Pre-tax deductions are especially valuable in Oregon because of the high state tax rates. A $500 monthly 401(k) contribution saves approximately $43.75 per month in Oregon state tax at the 8.75% bracket, plus your federal marginal rate savings. This makes tax-advantaged savings even more rewarding for Oregon workers than for those in lower-tax states.

Oregon Department of Revenue

The Oregon Department of Revenue administers the state income tax system. Here are key resources and information for Oregon taxpayers:

  • Form OR-40: The Oregon individual income tax return for full-year residents. Due April 15, aligned with the federal deadline.
  • Form OR-W-4: Oregon's withholding allowance certificate. While many employers use the federal W-4 to calculate Oregon withholding, you can file a separate OR-W-4 to customize your state withholding.
  • Publication OR-17: The Oregon Individual Income Tax Guide, published annually with current brackets, deductions, credits, and filing instructions.
  • Revenue Online: Oregon's electronic filing and payment system at revenueonline.dor.oregon.gov for tax returns, estimated payments, and account management.
  • Estimated payments: If you owe $1,000 or more in Oregon tax after withholding and credits, you may make quarterly estimated payments using Form OR-40-V.

Oregon does not participate in the Sales Tax Project because it has no sales tax. However, Oregon does enforce a use tax that technically applies to goods purchased from out-of-state sellers for use in Oregon, though enforcement on individual purchases is limited.

The Oregon Department of Revenue also administers several tax credits that can reduce your Oregon tax liability, including the Earned Income Credit (12% of the federal credit for most filers), the Working Family Household and Dependent Care Credit, the Political Contribution Credit, and the Retirement Income Credit for lower-income seniors.

Tax Saving Tips for OR Workers

Oregon's high income tax rates make tax-saving strategies particularly valuable. Here are practical approaches that work within Oregon's tax framework:

  1. retirement account contributions. The 2024 401(k) limit is $23,000 ($30,500 if 50+). At Oregon's 8.75% bracket, a full $23,000 contribution saves you $2,012 in state tax plus your federal savings. This is one of the highest state-level savings rates in the country.
  2. Fund your HSA fully. If you have a high-deductible health plan, contribute the maximum ($4,150 individual, $8,300 family in 2024). HSA contributions reduce federal, state, and FICA taxable income when made through payroll deduction.
  3. Claim the Oregon Earned Income Credit. If you qualify for the federal EITC, Oregon adds 12% of your federal credit (9% for filers without children). This is a refundable credit that can reduce your Oregon tax below zero.
  4. Take the Oregon political contribution credit. Oregon allows a tax credit (not just a deduction) of up to $50 per person ($100 for joint filers) for contributions to qualifying political organizations.
  5. Use Oregon 529 deduction. Contributions to an Oregon 529 plan (Oregon College Savings Plan) qualify for an Oregon tax deduction of up to $300 per contributor ($600 joint) for 2024.
  6. Consider itemizing on Oregon. Even if you take the federal standard deduction, you may benefit from itemizing on your Oregon return if your Oregon itemized deductions exceed $2,745 (single) or $5,495 (joint). Oregon's low standard deduction makes itemizing worthwhile at lower income levels than federal.
  7. Review the Working Family Household and Dependent Care Credit. This Oregon credit helps working families with child care costs and can be worth up to 40% of qualifying expenses for lower-income households.
  8. If self-employed, deduct business expenses fully. Oregon follows federal treatment of business deductions. Every legitimate business expense reduces your taxable income at your marginal state rate (up to 9.9%).

Key considerations for self-employed workers in Oregon:

  • Self-employment tax: You pay both employee and employer shares of Social Security (12.4%) and Medicare (2.9%), totaling 15.3% on 92.35% of your net SE income.
  • Deductible half: You can deduct half of SE tax as an above-the-line deduction, reducing both federal and Oregon taxable income.
  • Quarterly estimated payments: Both federal (Form 1040-ES) and Oregon (Form OR-40-V) estimated payments are required if you expect to owe $1,000+ in either. Due dates are April 15, June 15, September 15, and January 15.
  • Statewide Transit Tax: Self-employed individuals earning $1,000 or more in Oregon must pay the 0.1% transit tax on Schedule SE-compatible income.
  • Section 199A QBI deduction: Qualified business income may be eligible for a 20% deduction, reducing both federal and Oregon taxable income.
  • Oregon business credits: Certain Oregon tax credits are available to self-employed individuals, including the business energy tax credit and the rural practitioner tax credit for healthcare providers in underserved areas.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the top Oregon income tax rate?

Oregon's top marginal income tax rate is 9.9%, which applies to taxable income over $125,000 for single filers and over $250,000 for married filing jointly. This is among the highest state income tax rates in the country, behind only California (13.3%), Hawaii (11%), and New Jersey (10.75%).

Why does Oregon have such high income taxes?

Oregon relies heavily on income tax revenue because it has no sales tax. The state made a deliberate choice decades ago to fund government services primarily through income and property taxes rather than consumption taxes. This approach is seen by supporters as more progressive since income taxes are based on ability to pay.

Does Oregon tax Social Security benefits?

No. Oregon does not tax Social Security benefits. If you receive Social Security retirement, disability, or survivor benefits, they are not included in Oregon taxable income, even if a portion is taxable at the federal level. This can be a significant benefit for retirees living in Oregon.

What is the Portland Metro tax?

The Portland Metro area has a Supportive Housing Services (SHS) personal income tax of 1% on taxable income over $125,000 for single filers and $200,000 for joint filers. This is a local tax to Oregon state income tax. Multnomah County also has a Preschool For All personal income tax with similar thresholds. These local taxes are not included in this calculator.

Is there a kicker refund in Oregon?

Yes. Oregon has a unique "kicker" law. When actual state revenue exceeds the official forecast by more than 2%, the excess is returned to taxpayers as a credit on their next tax return. The kicker has been triggered multiple times in recent years, providing significant refunds to Oregon taxpayers. The amount varies based on your prior year tax liability.

Do I file Oregon taxes if I work remotely?

If you physically work in Oregon, you owe Oregon income tax on that income regardless of where your employer is located. If you are an Oregon resident working remotely for an out-of-state company, you owe Oregon tax on all your income. Oregon may also claim taxing authority on income earned by nonresidents if the work was performed in Oregon.

What is Oregon Saves?

Oregon Saves (OregonSaves) is a state-facilitated retirement savings program for employees whose employers do not offer a qualified retirement plan. It automatically enrolls eligible employees in a Roth IRA with a default 5% contribution rate. Contributions are after-tax and do not reduce your Oregon income tax liability, but the account grows tax-free.

How does the Oregon standard deduction compare to federal?

Oregon's standard deduction is much lower than the federal amount. For 2024, the Oregon standard deduction is approximately $2,745 for single filers versus $14,600 at the federal level. This means a larger portion of your income is subject to Oregon tax, which partly explains why effective state tax rates feel high even at moderate incomes. Married filing jointly gets $5,495 in Oregon versus $29,200 federally.
