Understanding Business Loan Types

The business lending field offers several distinct loan products, each designed for different needs, timelines, and borrower profiles. I've analyzed dozens of loan programs in building this calculator, and the differences in total cost can be substantial. Choosing the wrong loan type can cost you tens of thousands in unnecessary interest, so it is worth understanding your options before applying.

The key insight I can't stress enough is that the cheapest loan isn't always the best loan. SBA loans typically offer the lowest rates, but the application process takes months and requires extensive documentation. If you need capital quickly, a slightly higher-rate conventional loan that funds in two weeks might save more money through faster revenue generation than the savings from a lower SBA rate. Timing matters as much as rates in business financing.

As discussed on Wikipedia's SBA article, the Small Business Administration doesn't directly lend money. Instead, it guarantees a portion of loans made by participating lenders, reducing the lender's risk and allowing them to offer better terms. This guarantee structure is what makes SBA loans possible for businesses that might not qualify for conventional bank financing.

SBA Loans in Detail · 7(a) vs 504

SBA loans represent the gold standard for small business financing, but the two main programs serve fundamentally different purposes. Understanding the distinction will save you time in the application process and ensure you apply for the right program.

SBA 7(a) Loans

The 7(a) program is the SBA's most popular and adaptable loan option. You can use it for virtually any legitimate business purpose: working capital, equipment, inventory, real estate, business acquisition, or debt refinancing. The maximum loan amount is $5 million, and terms extend up to 25 years for real estate or 10 years for equipment and working capital.

Interest rates on 7(a) loans are tied to the prime rate. For loans over $50,000 with terms under 7 years, lenders can charge up to prime + 2.25%. For longer terms, the maximum is prime + 2.75%. On loans of $50,000 or less, lenders can add up to 4.75% above prime. The SBA also charges a guarantee fee of 2-3.75% depending on loan amount and maturity, which is typically rolled into the loan.

SBA 504 Loans

The 504 program is specifically designed for major fixed assets: real estate, land, buildings, or heavy equipment with a useful life of 10+ years. The unique structure involves three parties: the borrower contributes 10-20% down payment, a conventional lender provides about 50% of the project cost, and a Certified Development Company (CDC) provides up to 40% at a below-market fixed rate.

The CDC portion is where the real advantage lies. Because CDCs fund their portion through SBA-guaranteed debentures sold on the bond market, the rate is fixed and typically lower than what any bank would offer. This makes 504 loans the cheapest financing option for qualifying real estate and equipment purchases. Current CDC rates run between 5.5% and 7%, depending on term and market conditions.

Important distinction: SBA 7(a) loans are more adaptable but typically carry variable rates. SBA 504 loans offer fixed rates on the CDC portion but can only be used for specific fixed-asset purchases. For general working capital or inventory, the 7(a) is your only SBA option. For buying commercial real estate, the 504 usually wins on total cost.

Detailed comparisons of SBA programs have been discussed extensively on Hacker News, where startup founders and small business owners share their experiences navigating the application process. The consensus is that having your documentation fully prepared before applying (3 years of tax returns, business plan, financial projections, and personal financial statements) can cut approval time significantly.

How Loan Amortization Works

Understanding amortization is important for making informed borrowing decisions. An amortized loan spreads payments evenly across the loan term, but the composition of each payment changes over time. Early payments are heavily weighted toward interest, while later payments pay down more principal. I've found this pattern surprises many first-time borrowers who don't realize how slowly they build equity in the early years.

The standard amortization formula is: M = P[r(1+r)^n] / [(1+r)^n - 1], where M is the monthly payment, P is the principal loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate / 12), and n is the total number of payments. For a $250,000 loan at 8% over 10 years, this yields a monthly payment of $3,033.19.

In the first month of that example loan, $1,666.67 goes to interest and only $1,366.52 goes to principal. By month 60 (the halfway point), interest drops to $1,020.55 and principal rises to $2,012.64. In the final month, only $20.05 is interest and $3,013.14 goes to principal. This front-loading of interest is why refinancing early in a loan term saves more money than refinancing later.

Interest-only periods, common in SBA 7(a) loans and lines of credit, delay principal repayment entirely. During an interest-only period, you pay only the interest accruing each month. This keeps payments low but means the full principal remains outstanding. Once the interest-only period ends, payments increase substantially because the same principal must be repaid in fewer remaining months.

For detailed mathematical analysis of amortization schedules, Stack Overflow's amortization tag contains numerous implementations in various programming languages. The underlying math is straightforward but the edge cases (variable rates, extra payments, interest-only periods) add complexity that this calculator handles automatically.

Debt Service Coverage Ratio (DSCR) is often the deciding factor. It measures whether your business generates enough cash flow to cover loan payments. DSCR = Net Operating Income / Total Debt Service. A DSCR of 1.25 means your business earns $1.25 for every $1.00 in debt payments. Most lenders require at least 1.25, and stronger ratios improve your chances of approval and may qualify you for better rates.

For SBA loans specifically, the business must meet SBA size standards (generally fewer than 500 employees for most industries), operate for profit, be based in the United States, and have exhausted other financing options. The Wikipedia article on SBA loan programs provides additional context on eligibility requirements and the historical evolution of these programs.

How to Choose the Right Business Loan

Selecting the right loan product requires balancing multiple factors: total cost, speed of funding, flexibility of use, repayment terms, and your business's qualification strength. I've developed a decision framework through our testing methodology that simplifies this process.

If you are buying commercial real estate or heavy equipment and can wait 2-3 months for funding, the SBA 504 loan almost always wins on total cost. The fixed rate on the CDC portion and the longer terms produce the lowest total interest expense for fixed-asset purchases. Don't overlook this option just because the process seems complicated.

If you need general-purpose capital (working capital, inventory, marketing, hiring) and can wait 1-2 months, the SBA 7(a) is your best bet. The lower rates and longer terms keep monthly payments manageable. The guarantee fee is a worthwhile tradeoff for the interest savings over the life of the loan.

If you need money within days rather than months, online lenders or a business line of credit provide speed at the cost of higher rates. Use these for short-term bridge financing or genuine emergencies, but don't rely on them as long-term capital. The interest costs on a 20%+ online loan can devastate profitability if the funds aren't deployed into high-return activities.

Lines of credit are ideal for managing cash flow volatility. Rather than borrowing a lump sum, you draw only what you need and pay interest only on the outstanding balance. This makes them perfect for seasonal businesses, companies with lumpy receivables, or any business that needs adaptable access to capital without the commitment of a term loan.

Discussions on Hacker News about startup financing highlight that many founders overlook SBA loans because they associate them with traditional brick-and-mortar businesses. In reality, SBA 7(a) loans can fund software companies, consultancies, and service businesses just as effectively. The key qualifier is that the business must be generating revenue.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the difference between SBA 7(a) and SBA 504 loans?

SBA 7(a) loans are the most adaptable, used for working capital, equipment, inventory, and real estate up to $5 million. SBA 504 loans are specifically for major fixed assets like real estate and heavy equipment, up to $5.5 million. The 504 typically offers lower rates because part of the loan is funded by a CDC at a fixed rate, but it has stricter use requirements. I'd recommend 7(a) for general needs and 504 for real estate purchases.

What credit score do I need for a business loan?

SBA loans generally require 680-700+. Bank term loans typically need 700+. Online lenders may approve 580+ but charge significantly higher rates. Business credit scores from Dun and Bradstreet or Experian Business are also considered for established companies. The calculator above lets you model different rate scenarios to see how credit quality affects total cost.

How long does it take to get an SBA loan?

SBA 7(a) loans typically take 30-90 days from application to funding. SBA 504 loans can take 60-120 days due to CDC involvement. Conventional bank loans fund in 2-6 weeks. Online lenders can fund in 1-7 business days. Having all documentation ready before applying (3 years of tax returns, business plan, financial statements) can significantly reduce timelines.

What are typical business loan interest rates in 2026?

SBA 7(a) rates run prime + 2.25% to prime + 4.75%. SBA 504 fixed rates are 5.5-7%. Bank term loans range 6-13%. Online lenders charge 8-30%+. Lines of credit typically fall between 7-25%. Rates depend heavily on credit profile, collateral, business age, and revenue. Use the comparison feature above to see how rates affect your specific loan scenario.

Should I choose a fixed or variable rate business loan?

Fixed rates provide payment predictability for budgeting. Variable rates start lower but carry the risk of increases. Fixed rates work best for long-term loans (5+ years) and when rates are expected to rise. Variable rates suit short-term borrowing or falling-rate environments. SBA 504 is always fixed. SBA 7(a) is usually variable. Conventional loans offer both options.

Can I pay off a business loan early?

Most business loans allow early payoff, but some charge prepayment penalties. SBA 7(a) loans penalize prepayment only on 15+ year terms during the first 3 years (5%/3%/1%). SBA 504 loans have a declining 10-year prepayment penalty. Conventional loan terms vary by lender. Always review the prepayment clause before signing. Early payoff can save significant interest, especially in the early years of a loan.

What collateral is required for a business loan?

SBA loans require collateral for amounts over $25,000 (7a) or $50,000 (504), typically business assets or real estate. The SBA won't decline a loan solely for insufficient collateral but requires lenders to collateralize to the maximum extent possible. Bank loans almost always need collateral. Online lenders may offer unsecured options at higher rates. Lines of credit can be secured or unsecured.
