How to Use This Paycheck Calculator

This paycheck calculator helps you estimate your take-home pay based on your gross salary, tax filing status, state of residence, and pre-tax deductions. Understanding your net pay is essential for budgeting, financial planning, and evaluating job offers.

To get started, enter your gross annual salary in the field above. Next, select your pay frequency. Most employees in the United States are paid bi-weekly (every two weeks), resulting in 26 paychecks per year. Some employers pay weekly (52 paychecks), semi-monthly (24 paychecks, typically on the 1st and 15th), or monthly (12 paychecks).

Choose your filing status: Single, Married Filing Jointly, or Head of Household. This determines the tax brackets and standard deduction used for your federal income tax calculation. Then select your state to include state income tax in the estimate.

If you contribute to a 401(k) retirement plan, an HSA (Health Savings Account), or pay health insurance premiums through your employer, enter those annual amounts in the pre-tax deductions section. These reduce your taxable income before federal and state taxes are calculated, potentially lowering your tax burden.

Understanding Federal Income Tax Brackets (2024/2025)

The United States uses a progressive federal income tax system. This means your income is taxed at increasing rates as it passes through each bracket. The 2024/2025 federal tax brackets for single filers are:

  • 10% on taxable income up to $11,600
  • 12% on taxable income from $11,601 to $47,150
  • 22% on taxable income from $47,151 to $100,525
  • 24% on taxable income from $100,526 to $191,950
  • 32% on taxable income from $191,951 to $243,725
  • 35% on taxable income from $243,726 to $609,350
  • 37% on taxable income over $609,350

For married filing jointly, the bracket thresholds are roughly doubled. For example, the 10% bracket covers taxable income up to $23,200, and the 12% bracket extends to $94,300. Head of Household filers have brackets that fall between Single and Married Filing Jointly.

It is important to understand that only the income within each bracket is taxed at that rate. If you earn $60,000 as a single filer, you do not pay 22% on the entire amount. Instead, you pay 10% on the first $11,600, 12% on the next portion up to $47,150, and 22% only on the remaining income above $47,150.

Social Security and Medicare

FICA (Federal Insurance Contributions Act) taxes fund Social Security and Medicare programs. Every worker in the United States pays these taxes on their earned income.

Social Security tax is 6.2% of your gross wages, applied to earnings up to the wage base limit of $168,600 for 2024. Any income above that threshold is not subject to Social Security tax. Your employer also pays a matching 6.2%, but that does not come out of your paycheck.

Medicare tax is 1.45% on all earned income with no cap. If your earnings exceed $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly), an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies to the income above that threshold. This surtax is paid only by the employee.

How FICA Affects Your Paycheck

For most workers, FICA taxes represent a combined 7.65% reduction from gross pay (6.2% Social Security plus 1.45% Medicare). On a $75,000 salary, that amounts to $5,737.50 per year, or roughly $220.67 per bi-weekly paycheck. These taxes are not reduced by standard deductions or pre-tax retirement contributions (except for certain Section 125 cafeteria plan deductions).

State Income Tax Overview

State income taxes vary dramatically across the country. Nine states levy no income tax on wages: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. Living in one of these states means your take-home pay will be noticeably higher compared to states with steep income taxes.

On the other end of the spectrum, California has the highest marginal state income tax rate at 13.3%, though that rate applies only to income over $1 million. Other high-tax states include Hawaii (11%), New Jersey (10.75%), Oregon (9.9%), and Minnesota (9.85%). Many states use progressive brackets similar to the federal system, while others such as Colorado (4.4%), Illinois (4.95%), and Pennsylvania (3.07%) use a flat rate.

This calculator uses simplified effective rates for each state to provide a reasonable estimate. Your actual state tax liability may differ based on specific state deductions, credits, and local taxes.

Pre-Tax Deductions and How They Save You Money

Pre-tax deductions are amounts taken from your gross pay before income taxes are calculated. Common pre-tax deductions include:

  • 401(k) or 403(b) retirement contributions (up to $23,000 in 2024, or $30,500 if age 50+)
  • Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions ($4,150 for self-only or $8,300 for family coverage in 2024)
  • Employer-sponsored health insurance premiums
  • Flexible Spending Account (FSA) contributions
  • Commuter benefits

By reducing your taxable income, pre-tax deductions lower your federal and state tax obligations. For example, if you earn $75,000 and contribute $10,000 to your 401(k), your federal taxable income drops to $65,000 (before the standard deduction). At the 22% marginal rate, that $10,000 contribution saves you approximately $2,200 in federal taxes alone, plus additional savings on state taxes.

Practical Example

Consider a single filer in Texas earning $80,000 annually, contributing $6,000 to a 401(k) and $2,000 to health insurance. Their taxable income for federal purposes would be $80,000 minus $6,000 minus $2,000 minus the $14,600 standard deduction, yielding $57,400. Federal tax on that amount would be approximately $7,616. FICA taxes would be $6,120 (Social Security on the full $80,000 at 6.2% equals $4,960, plus Medicare at 1.45% equals $1,160). With no state income tax, the total annual deductions and taxes come to approximately $21,736, leaving a net annual pay of roughly $58,264, or about $2,241 per bi-weekly paycheck.

Tips for Maximizing Your Take-Home Pay

  • Contribute enough to your 401(k) to get the full employer match. This is free money that increases your total compensation.
  • Use an HSA if eligible. HSA contributions are pre-tax, grow tax-free, and can be withdrawn tax-free for qualified medical expenses.
  • Review your W-4 withholding annually. Over-withholding gives you a large refund but reduces your paycheck throughout the year. Adjusting your W-4 can put more money in each paycheck.
  • Consider the total compensation package, not just salary. Benefits like health insurance, retirement matching, and paid time off have real financial value.
  • If you are choosing between job offers in different states, factor in state income tax differences. A $90,000 salary in Texas takes home more than $90,000 in California.

Understanding Your Effective Tax Rate

Your effective tax rate is the total percentage of your gross income that goes to all taxes combined. It is different from your marginal tax rate, which is the rate on your last dollar of income. Most people have an effective tax rate well below their top marginal bracket. This calculator displays your effective rate in the pay stub summary, giving you a clear picture of your overall tax burden.

For example, a single filer earning $75,000 might have a marginal federal rate of 22%, but their effective federal rate could be around 12-14% after the standard deduction. When you add FICA and state taxes, the total effective rate typically ranges from 20% to 35% for most workers.

Married Filing Jointly roughly doubles both, resulting in lower taxes on the same income. Head of Household falls between the two and requires you to be unmarried and pay more than half the cost of maintaining a home for a qualifying dependent.

Frequently Asked Questions

Q: How do I calculate my take-home pay from my salary?

To calculate your take-home pay, start with your gross salary and subtract federal income tax, state income tax, Social Security tax (6.2%), Medicare tax (1.45%), and any pre-tax deductions like 401k contributions or health insurance premiums. The result is your net pay or take-home pay.

Q: What are the 2024-2025 federal income tax brackets?

For 2024-2025, the federal tax brackets for single filers are: 10% on income up to $11,600, 12% on $11,601 to $47,150, 22% on $47,151 to $100,525, 24% on $100,526 to $191,950, 32% on $191,951 to $243,725, 35% on $243,726 to $609,350, and 37% on income over $609,350.

Q: How much is FICA tax in 2024?

FICA tax consists of Social Security tax at 6.2% on earnings up to $168,600 and Medicare tax at 1.45% on all earnings. An additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies to earnings over $200,000 for single filers or $250,000 for married filing jointly.

Q: What is the difference between gross pay and net pay?

Gross pay is your total earnings before any deductions. Net pay (take-home pay) is what you actually receive after subtracting federal taxes, state taxes, FICA taxes, and any voluntary deductions like retirement contributions or health insurance.

Q: How do pre-tax deductions affect my paycheck?

Pre-tax deductions like 401k contributions, HSA contributions, and health insurance premiums are subtracted from your gross pay before taxes are calculated. This reduces your taxable income and lowers the amount of federal and state taxes you owe, effectively saving you money on taxes.

Q: Which states have no income tax?

Nine states have no state income tax: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. New Hampshire and Tennessee only tax interest and dividend income, not wages.

Q: How does filing status affect my paycheck?

Your filing status (Single, Married Filing Jointly, or Head of Household) determines which tax brackets and standard deduction amounts apply. Married Filing Jointly typically has wider tax brackets and a higher standard deduction, which can result in lower taxes and higher take-home pay.

Q: How often should I check my paycheck calculations?

You should review your paycheck calculations at least once per year, especially after tax bracket changes, salary increases, changes in filing status, or adjustments to your deductions. It is also wise to check after major life events like marriage, having a child, or buying a home.
