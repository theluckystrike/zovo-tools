Understanding Compound Interest

Compound interest is the interest calculated on both the initial principal and the accumulated interest from previous periods. It is one of the most fundamental concepts in personal finance and investing.

Compound Interest Formula
A = P(1 + r/n)nt
A = future value | P = principal | r = annual rate (decimal) | n = compounds per year | t = years
With Regular Contributions
A = P(1 + r/n)nt + PMT × [((1 + r/n)nt - 1) / (r/n)]
PMT = periodic contribution amount

Example Calculation

If you deposit $10,000 at a 5% annual interest rate compounded monthly for 10 years:

A = $10,000 × (1 + 0.05/12)12×10 = $10,000 × (1.004167)120 = $10,000 × 1.6470 = $16,470.09

Your $10,000 would grow to $16,470.09 - earning $6,470.09 in interest. With an additional $500 monthly contribution, the total would grow to approximately $94,128.

Simple Interest vs Compound Interest

Simple interest is calculated only on the original principal: I = P × r × t. With simple interest, $10,000 at 5% for 10 years earns exactly $5,000. With monthly compounding, the same deposit earns $6,470.09 - that is $1,470.09 more thanks to compounding.

Quick Facts About Interest Rates

  • The Federal Reserve sets the federal funds rate, which influences all other interest rates in the economy
  • APY (Annual Percentage Yield) always accounts for compounding, making it the best metric for comparing savings products
  • The Rule of 72: divide 72 by the interest rate to estimate how many years it takes to double your money
  • Higher compounding frequency (daily vs annually) results in higher effective returns, though the difference narrows at lower rates
  • Interest rates and bond prices move inversely - when rates rise, existing bond values decrease

Frequently Asked Questions

Q What is the difference between APR and APY?

APR (Annual Percentage Rate) is the stated annual interest rate without accounting for compounding. APY (Annual Percentage Yield) includes the effect of compounding within the year. APY is always equal to or higher than APR. The formula is APY = (1 + APR/n)^n - 1, where n is the number of compounding periods per year.

Q How does compounding frequency affect interest rates?

More frequent compounding results in higher effective returns. Daily compounding yields more than monthly, which yields more than quarterly, which yields more than annually. For example, a 5% APR compounded daily gives an APY of about 5.13%, while compounded annually it stays at exactly 5%.

Q What is a good savings account interest rate?

As of 2026, high-yield savings accounts offer rates between 4.00% and 5.25% APY. Traditional bank savings accounts may offer as low as 0.01% to 0.50%. Online banks and credit unions typically offer the most competitive rates. Always compare APY rather than APR for an accurate comparison.

Q How do I calculate monthly loan payments?

The monthly payment formula is M = P[r(1+r)^n] / [(1+r)^n - 1], where P is the principal loan amount, r is the monthly interest rate (annual rate divided by 12), and n is the total number of monthly payments. This calculator handles the math automatically.

Q What factors affect the interest rate I receive?

Key factors include your credit score, loan term length, loan amount, the Federal Reserve's benchmark rate, economic conditions, the type of loan or account, and the financial institution. For savings, online banks typically offer higher rates due to lower overhead costs.
