Washington Tax Overview

Washington is one of nine states that does not impose a personal income tax on wages and salaries. This single fact is the biggest reason Washington workers take home more per dollar earned than workers in most other states.

Here is a summary of what Washington does and does not tax:

The absence of a state income tax means Washington relies heavily on sales tax, property tax, and business taxes (B&O tax) for revenue. For employees, this results in noticeably larger paychecks compared to neighboring Oregon, which has no sales tax but a top income tax rate of 9.9%.

FICA: Social Security and Medicare

FICA taxes are federal and apply in every state, including Washington:

  • Social Security: 6.2% on wages up to $176,100 (2025 wage base). Your employer pays an equal 6.2%.
  • Medicare: 1.45% on all wages with no cap. An additional 0.9% surtax applies to earnings above $200,000 for single filers ($250,000 for joint filers).
  • Total FICA: 7.65% for most workers, making it the largest payroll deduction for Washington employees after federal income tax.

FICA is calculated on gross wages after certain pre-tax deductions like 401(k) contributions, but before the standard deduction. These contributions fund Social Security retirement benefits and Medicare health coverage.

WA Cares Fund (Long-Term Care)

The WA Cares Fund is Washington's first-in-the-nation long-term care insurance program. Key details:

  • Premium Rate: 0.58% of gross wages, paid entirely by the employee
  • No Wage Cap: Unlike Social Security, the WA Cares premium applies to all wages with no upper limit
  • Lifetime Benefit: Eligible workers can access up to $36,500 for long-term care services (home care, assisted living, nursing facilities)
  • Vesting: Workers need to contribute for at least 10 years (without a gap of 5+ years) or 3 of the last 6 years to qualify
  • Exemptions: Workers who purchased qualifying private long-term care insurance before November 1, 2021 could apply for a permanent exemption. Veterans with service-connected disabilities and certain non-immigrant visa holders may also be exempt.

For a worker earning $85,000 per year, the WA Cares premium costs $493 annually. While this is a new deduction, it remains small relative to federal taxes and FICA.

Learn more at the WA Cares Fund official website.

Paid Family and Medical Leave

Washington's Paid Family and Medical Leave (PFML) program provides up to 12 weeks of paid leave for qualifying events such as a new child, serious illness, or military family needs. The cost is split between employees and employers:

  • Total 2025 Premium Rate: 0.74% of gross wages
  • Employee Share: Approximately 72.76% of the premium, or about 0.54% of gross wages
  • Employer Share: Approximately 27.24% of the premium
  • Maximum Benefit: Up to $1,546 per week in 2025

Small employers with fewer than 50 employees are not required to pay the employer share but may opt in. All employees contribute regardless of employer size. This is a payroll deduction you will see on your Washington pay stub.

For details, visit the Washington Paid Leave website.

Self-Employment Taxes in Washington

Self-employed workers in Washington benefit significantly from the lack of state income tax. However, other tax obligations remain:

  • Self-Employment Tax: 15.3% on net earnings (12.4% Social Security up to $176,100 + 2.9% Medicare on all earnings). The 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax applies to net self-employment income above $200,000.
  • Deductible Half: You can deduct 50% of self-employment tax from your adjusted gross income, reducing your federal tax burden.
  • Federal Estimated Payments: Quarterly estimated tax payments are required if you expect to owe $1,000 or more in federal taxes.
  • No State Income Tax Filing: Self-employed Washington residents do not need to file a state income tax return for their earnings.
  • B&O Tax: Washington's Business and Occupation (B&O) tax may apply depending on your business activity. Rates vary from 0.13% to 3.3% depending on classification. Many small businesses qualify for the small business B&O credit.
  • WA Cares: Self-employed workers can opt into the WA Cares Fund voluntarily but are not required to participate.

A self-employed Washington worker earning $100,000 net might pay roughly $14,000 in federal income tax and $14,100 in self-employment tax (before the deductible half), with zero state income tax. That is a significant advantage over self-employed workers in Oregon, who would owe an additional $8,000+ in state taxes on the same income.

Washington Cost of Living

Washington's generous take-home pay is partially offset by the cost of living, especially in the Seattle metro area:

  • Seattle / Bellevue / Redmond: Among the most expensive metros in the country. Median home prices exceed $800,000. Rents for a one-bedroom average $2,200+. However, tech salaries often reach $150,000 to $300,000 with equity.
  • Tacoma / Olympia: More affordable than Seattle with median homes around $450,000 to $550,000. Growing job markets and good transit connections to Seattle.
  • Spokane: Eastern Washington's largest city. Median homes around $370,000. Significantly lower cost of living with a growing tech and healthcare sector.
  • Tri-Cities (Kennewick, Richland, Pasco): Affordable housing with median homes around $380,000. Hanford nuclear site and agriculture are major employers.
  • Vancouver, WA: Portland metro access without Oregon's income tax. Median homes around $470,000. Popular for cross-border commuters.

Washington's sales tax (often 10%+ with local additions) is a factor in daily costs. Groceries are exempt from sales tax in Washington, which helps offset this. When evaluating a Washington job offer, consider the regional cost of housing, the sales tax impact, and the significant benefit of zero income tax on your salary.

Frequently Asked Questions

Why does my Washington paycheck still have so many deductions?

Even with no state income tax, federal income tax (10-37%), Social Security (6.2%), and Medicare (1.45%) still apply. Add the WA Cares Fund (0.58%) and PFML (~0.54%), and the total deduction rate typically falls between 20-30% depending on income level and filing status.

Can I opt out of the WA Cares Fund?

The initial opt-out window for workers with private long-term care insurance closed on December 31, 2022. New exemptions are limited to specific categories: veterans with service-connected disabilities of 70%+, certain non-immigrant visa holders, and workers who live outside Washington. Most workers cannot opt out.

Do I need to file a state tax return in Washington?

No. Washington does not have a personal income tax return. You only file a federal return. However, if you have capital gains exceeding $250,000 from selling stocks or bonds, you may need to file for the Washington capital gains tax.

How does the Washington minimum wage affect payroll?

Washington has one of the highest minimum wages in the U.S. at $16.66 per hour in 2025. Seattle's minimum wage is even higher at $20.76 per hour. These wages are subject to the same federal taxes, FICA, and WA deductions as any other wages.

I work remotely from Washington for a company in another state. Do I owe that state's income tax?

Generally, no. If you physically work from Washington, you owe no state income tax because Washington does not have one. However, some states have tried to tax remote workers' income earned for in-state employers. Consult a tax professional if your employer is based in a state that asserts such claims.
