Definition

A retaining wall is a structure designed and constructed to resist the lateral pressure of soil when there is a desired change in ground elevation that exceeds the angle of repose of the soil. Retaining walls are used in landscaping, civil engineering, and construction to hold back earth and prevent erosion on slopes.

How to Use This Calculator

Using this retaining wall calculator is straightforward:

  1. Measure wall length. This is the total horizontal distance your wall will span, in feet.
  2. Set wall height. Measure from the bottom of the base trench to the desired top of the wall. For stepped walls on a slope, use the tallest section.
  3. Select block type. Choose from standard sizes or enter custom dimensions. The block size determines how many courses you need and how many blocks fit per row.
  4. Review the output. The calculator provides wall blocks, cap blocks, gravel backfill, drain pipe, geogrid (if needed), base gravel, and filter fabric quantities, plus a cost estimate.

For walls with multiple straight sections at different heights, run the calculator once for each section and add the totals together.

Block Types Comparison

The material you choose affects the wall's appearance, cost, structural capacity, and installation difficulty.

Interlocking Concrete Blocks

The most popular choice for residential retaining walls. Blocks have a built-in lip or pin system that locks each course to the one below. No mortar needed. DIY-friendly with excellent structural performance.

Natural Stone

Fieldstone, limestone, or stacked flagstone create a timeless, organic look. More labor-intensive because each stone varies in size and shape. Requires careful fitting and may need mortar for structural walls.

The force that soil exerts on a retaining wall increases with the square of the wall height. A 6-foot wall does not experience twice the pressure of a 3-foot wall; it experiences roughly four times the pressure. This is why taller walls need exponentially more reinforcement.

Drainage Requirements

Every retaining wall needs drainage. Water-saturated soil behind a wall generates hydrostatic pressure that can push over even well-built walls. Here is what proper drainage looks like:

Drainage Components

  • Gravel backfill: 12 inches of clean crushed stone (3/4" minus) directly behind the wall, running the full height from the drain pipe to near the top.
  • Perforated drain pipe: 4-inch perforated PVC or corrugated pipe at the base of the wall, sitting on top of the gravel base and below the first course. Holes face down.
  • Filter fabric: Geotextile fabric separating the gravel from the native soil to prevent fines from clogging the gravel and pipe.
  • Outlet: The drain pipe must slope at least 1% (1/8" per foot) toward a daylight outlet, dry well, or storm drain connection.

For walls in areas with heavy clay soil, high water tables, or significant uphill runoff, consider adding weep holes through the wall face every 6-8 feet as a secondary drainage path. These are simply gaps left open between blocks at the base of the wall.

Geogrid and Soil Reinforcement

Geogrid transforms a gravity wall (one that relies on its own weight to resist soil pressure) into a mechanically stabilized earth (MSE) wall that ties into the soil mass behind it. This dramatically increases the wall's ability to handle lateral earth pressure.

When Geogrid Is Needed

  • Wall height exceeds 3 feet (varies by block system)
  • Surcharge loads above the wall (driveway, building, heavy equipment)
  • Sloped backfill behind the wall
  • Weak or expansive soils
  • Seismic zones

Geogrid Placement Rules

  • First layer at the second or third course from the bottom
  • Additional layers every 2-3 courses depending on wall height
  • Grid extends back from the wall face at least 60% of the total wall height (e.g., 3 feet back for a 5-foot wall)
  • Grid must sit on compacted fill, not loose soil
  • Roll ends should overlap at least 6 inches at splices

Each block manufacturer publishes specific geogrid placement tables for their systems. Follow those tables rather than general rules when building with a specific block product.

Base Preparation for Retaining Walls

The base is the foundation of your retaining wall. A poor base leads to settling, leaning, and eventual failure regardless of how carefully the rest of the wall is built.

  1. Excavate the trench. Width should be at least 24 inches (block depth + 12" for gravel backfill). Depth should accommodate 6" of compacted gravel plus burying at least half the first block course.
  2. Level the bottom. The trench floor must be flat and at the correct slope for drain pipe fall. Check with a long level.
  3. Compact the subgrade. Use a plate compactor or hand tamper on the native soil at the bottom of the trench. This prevents settling.
  4. Add base gravel. Spread 6 inches of compacted crushed stone (3/4" road base). Compact in 3-inch lifts. This should be dead level front-to-back and follow the wall line left-to-right.
  5. Place the first course. Set blocks carefully on the gravel base. This course is the most important. Check level every few feet and adjust with a rubber mallet. If the first course is off, every course above will be off too.

Gravel Backfill Guide

Gravel backfill serves two purposes: it provides drainage behind the wall, and it replaces heavy native soil with lighter material that puts less pressure on the wall.

  • Material: Use clean 3/4" crushed stone or drainage gravel. Do not use round river rock (it does not compact as well) or stone with fines (it holds water).
  • Width: 12 inches behind the wall as a minimum. For walls over 4 feet, 18-24 inches is better.
  • Height: Run gravel from the drain pipe level to within 6 inches of the top of the wall. Cap the top 6 inches with native soil to support vegetation and prevent surface water from entering the gravel zone directly.
  • Calculation: Gravel volume = wall length (ft) × backfill width (ft) × wall height (ft). One cubic foot of gravel weighs about 100 lbs, so divide total cubic feet by 20 to get tons.

Building Codes and Permits

Retaining walls are regulated by building codes in most municipalities. Ignoring code requirements can result in fines, required demolition, or liability if the wall fails and causes damage.

When You Probably Need a Permit

  • Wall height exceeds 4 feet (3 feet in some jurisdictions)
  • Wall retains a surcharge (supports a driveway, structure, or slope)
  • Wall is near a property line (setback requirements vary)
  • Wall is near a public sidewalk or right-of-way
  • Wall is in a floodplain, wetland buffer, or environmentally sensitive area

Even if a permit is not required, your wall must still meet code requirements for setback, drainage, and structural adequacy. If your wall fails and damages a neighbor's property, you are liable regardless of permit status.

For walls over 4 feet, most jurisdictions require engineered plans stamped by a Professional Engineer (PE). Expect to pay $500-2,000 for a wall engineering package, which includes foundation design, geogrid layout, and drainage specifications for your specific site conditions.

Step-by-Step Installation

Here is the general process for building a concrete block retaining wall. Specific steps vary by block system, so always follow the manufacturer's installation guide.

  1. Lay out the wall line. Use stakes and string to mark the front face of the wall. Spray paint the ground as a reference.
  2. Excavate. Dig a trench 24" wide and deep enough for 6" of gravel base plus half of the first block (typically 9-12" total depth).
  3. Compact and level the base. Compact native soil, add 6" of crushed stone in two 3" lifts, compact each lift, and screed level.
  4. Set the first course. Place blocks on the gravel base, checking level frequently. This course sets the alignment for the entire wall.
  5. Install drain pipe. Lay 4" perforated pipe behind the first course on top of the gravel base, sloping toward the outlet.
  6. Build up courses. Stack blocks with the specified setback (usually 3/4" to 1" per course). Stagger vertical joints between courses. Backfill behind each course with gravel as you go.
  7. Install geogrid. At specified intervals, lay geogrid between courses extending back into the compacted backfill.
  8. Cap the wall. Glue cap blocks to the top course with concrete adhesive. This seals the top and finishes the wall.
  9. Finish grading. Backfill the top 6 inches with native soil. Grade for drainage away from the wall.

Common Wall Failures

Understanding why retaining walls fail helps you avoid the same mistakes. The most common causes:

  • No drainage. Water saturates the soil behind the wall, increasing lateral pressure by 50% or more. The wall bulges outward and eventually topples. Fix: always install gravel backfill and a drain pipe.
  • Inadequate base. Settling causes the wall to lean or crack. Fix: excavate a proper trench and compact a 6" gravel base.
  • Too tall without reinforcement. Gravity walls have height limits. Exceeding them without geogrid leads to failure. Fix: follow manufacturer height tables and add geogrid as specified.
  • Poor backfill compaction. Loose backfill settles unevenly, creating voids and concentrated pressure points. Fix: compact backfill in 6-8 inch lifts as you build.
  • Ignoring surcharge loads. A driveway, swimming pool, or building above the wall adds significant lateral force. Fix: engineer the wall for the actual loads, not just soil pressure.
  • Frost heave. In cold climates, the base must be below the frost line or on a non-frost-susceptible gravel pad. Fix: use a generous gravel base that drains freely.

Terracing vs. Single Wall

When you need to retain a significant grade change (5+ feet), building two or three shorter terraced walls is often better than one tall wall.

Advantages of Terracing

  • Each individual wall can stay under permit height limits (3-4 ft)
  • No geogrid or engineering may be needed for shorter walls
  • Creates usable flat areas between terraces for planting beds or pathways
  • Lower lateral earth pressure on each individual wall
  • More visually appealing and less imposing than a single tall wall
  • Easier to repair if one section settles

The general rule: space terraced walls apart by at least twice the height of the lower wall. For two 3-foot walls, that means at least 6 feet of horizontal distance between them. This spacing ensures the upper wall's pressure zone does not overlap the lower wall's zone.

Maintenance and Inspection

Retaining walls need periodic inspection to catch problems early before they become expensive repairs.

  • Inspect annually in spring after freeze-thaw season. Look for leaning, bulging, cracking, or shifted blocks.
  • Check drainage outlets. Make sure water flows freely from the drain pipe outlet. Clear any debris or root intrusion.
  • Monitor the top of the wall. If the ground behind the wall is settling or pulling away from the wall, there may be erosion or void formation in the backfill.
  • Watch for vegetation. Tree roots growing near or behind a retaining wall can generate significant lateral pressure. Remove large shrubs or trees within the wall's influence zone (roughly equal to the wall height).
  • Re-level cap blocks. Frost or settling can shift cap blocks. Re-glue them as needed with concrete adhesive.

Frequently Asked Questions

How long do retaining walls last?

Concrete block retaining walls last 50-100 years when properly built with adequate drainage. Timber walls last 15-25 years. Natural stone walls can last indefinitely. The biggest factor is drainage. Walls that manage water well outlast identical walls with poor drainage by decades.

Can I build a retaining wall against my house foundation?

You should never backfill directly against a house foundation with soil retained by a separate wall. This traps moisture against the foundation. If you need grade changes near your house, consult a structural engineer who can assess the impact on your foundation's waterproofing and drainage systems.

What is the cheapest type of retaining wall?

Landscape timber walls are the cheapest at $8-15 per square foot of wall face (materials only). Standard concrete blocks run $10-18 per square foot. However, timber walls have a shorter lifespan (15-20 years vs 50-100 years for concrete), so the long-term cost favors concrete blocks.

Do I need to bury the first course of blocks?

Yes. Burying at least half of the first course below grade anchors the wall against sliding and creates a clean finished appearance. For taller walls, bury a full course. The buried portion should rest on the compacted gravel base, not directly on native soil.

How many retaining wall blocks do I need per linear foot?

For standard 16-inch wide blocks at 6 inches tall, you need 0.75 blocks per linear foot per row. A 3-foot tall wall uses 6 rows, so roughly 4.5 blocks per linear foot of wall face. Always add 10% for cutting waste and breakage.

Do I need a permit for a 4-foot retaining wall?

In most US jurisdictions, walls 4 feet or taller (measured from the bottom of the footing to the top) require a building permit and possibly engineered plans. Some areas set the threshold at 3 feet. Check with your local building department before starting.

What type of gravel should I use behind a retaining wall?

Use 3/4-inch clean crushed stone (no fines) for drainage backfill behind retaining walls. This angular material compacts well, drains freely, and does not retain water. Avoid round river rock as it does not lock together and can shift under pressure.
