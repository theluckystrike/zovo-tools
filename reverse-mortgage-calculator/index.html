DEFINITION

A reverse mortgage is a type of mortgage loan, usually secured by a residential property, that enables the borrower to access the unencumbered value of the property. The loans are typically promoted to older homeowners and typically do not require monthly mortgage payments. Borrowers are still responsible for property taxes and homeowner's insurance.

How Reverse Mortgages Work

A reverse mortgage allows homeowners aged 62 and older to convert a portion of their home equity into cash without selling the home or making monthly mortgage payments. I built this calculator to help you understand the financial mechanics of the most common type of reverse mortgage, the Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM), which is backed by the Federal Housing Administration.

Unlike a traditional forward mortgage where you borrow a lump sum and repay it monthly, a reverse mortgage works in the opposite direction. The lender pays you, and the loan balance grows over time as interest accrues. You retain full ownership and title to the home throughout the life of the loan. Repayment is deferred until you sell the home, permanently move out, or pass away.

The amount you can borrow depends on several factors. Your age is the primary driver because the lender must account for how long the loan might remain outstanding. Older borrowers receive a higher percentage of their home value. Current interest rates also play a significant role. Lower rates translate to higher available proceeds because the projected loan balance grows more slowly. Your home value, capped at the FHA lending limit, establishes the maximum claim amount that forms the basis for all calculations.

Understanding the HECM Program

The Home Equity Conversion Mortgage program was established by Congress in 1988 and is administered by the Department of Housing and Urban Development (HUD). It remains the only reverse mortgage product insured by the federal government. The FHA insurance provides two critical protections that make the program viable for both lenders and borrowers.

For borrowers, FHA insurance guarantees that you will receive your expected loan advances even if the lender becomes unable to make payments. For lenders, FHA insurance covers any shortfall if the loan balance exceeds the home value at repayment. This non-recourse protection means neither you nor your heirs will ever owe more than the home is worth, regardless of how much the loan balance has grown.

The program requires that all prospective borrowers complete a counseling session with a HUD-approved counseling agency before applying. This session covers how the reverse mortgage works, the costs involved, alternatives you might consider, and the obligations you must maintain as a borrower. I consider this requirement one of the strongest consumer protections in the mortgage industry because it ensures every borrower makes an informed decision.

HECM Eligibility Requirements

To qualify for a HECM reverse mortgage, you must meet the following criteria. Each requirement exists to protect both the borrower and the FHA insurance fund from excessive risk.

  • You must be at least 62 years old. If there are multiple borrowers, the age of the youngest borrower is used for calculations.
  • The property must be your primary residence. You must live in the home for the majority of each year.
  • You must own the home outright, or have a mortgage balance small enough to be paid off with the reverse mortgage proceeds at closing.
  • You must not be delinquent on any federal debt, including federal income taxes or federal student loans.
  • You must complete a HUD-approved counseling session with a certified counselor.
  • You must demonstrate the financial capacity to continue paying property taxes, homeowners insurance, and any homeowners association fees.
  • The property must meet FHA minimum property standards and pass an FHA appraisal.

Eligible property types include single-family homes, two-to-four unit properties where you occupy one unit, HUD-approved condominiums, and manufactured homes that meet FHA requirements. Cooperative housing is generally not eligible for a HECM.

Principal Limit Factors Explained

The principal limit factor (PLF) is the percentage of your home value (or the FHA lending limit, whichever is less) that determines how much you can potentially access through a reverse mortgage. HUD publishes PLF tables based on the borrower's age and the expected interest rate at the time of closing.

The expected interest rate for a variable-rate HECM is calculated by adding the lender's margin to the 10-year LIBOR swap rate (or the Constant Maturity Treasury rate for some programs). For a fixed-rate HECM, the expected rate equals the note rate. Higher expected rates produce lower PLFs, which means you receive less money.

The Five Reverse Mortgage Payment Options

One of the most adaptable aspects of the HECM program is the variety of ways you can receive your loan proceeds. Each option serves different financial needs and retirement strategies. Understanding the differences helps you choose the approach that best fits your situation.

1. Lump Sum Distribution

The lump sum option provides all available proceeds at closing in a single payment. This is the only option available with a fixed-rate HECM. If you choose a variable-rate HECM and select the lump sum, you are limited to receiving 60% of your principal limit in the first year unless you need more than 60% to pay off existing liens and mandatory obligations.

The lump sum works well for borrowers who need a large amount immediately, such as paying off a substantial existing mortgage, funding major home renovations, or covering a significant medical expense. However, taking everything at once means the full balance begins accruing interest immediately, which causes the loan balance to grow faster than with other options.

2. Tenure Payments

Tenure payments provide equal monthly installments for as long as at least one borrower continues to live in the home as a primary residence. This option functions like a lifetime annuity tied to your home equity. The monthly amount is calculated based on your principal limit, the expected interest rate, and actuarial life expectancy tables.

I recommend tenure payments for borrowers who want predictable supplemental income throughout retirement. Even if you live well beyond your life expectancy and the total payments exceed your home value, you continue receiving the same monthly amount. The non-recourse nature of the HECM means you cannot owe more than the home is worth.

3. Term Payments

Term payments distribute equal monthly installments over a fixed period that you select. Because the payout period is shorter and defined, monthly term payments are typically higher than tenure payments. However, payments stop at the end of the term regardless of whether you still live in the home.

Term payments suit borrowers who need higher monthly income during a specific period, such as bridging the gap between early retirement and Social Security eligibility, covering a child's college tuition costs, or supplementing income until a pension begins.

4. Line of Credit

The line of credit gives you access to your principal limit on an as-needed basis. You draw funds whenever you want, in whatever amounts you choose, up to your available credit. Interest only accrues on the amount you have actually drawn, not on the unused portion.

The most remarkable feature of the HECM line of credit is its growth rate. The unused portion of your credit line increases over time at a rate equal to the loan interest rate plus the annual mortgage insurance premium rate. This growth happens regardless of what your home value does. Even if housing prices decline, your available credit continues to expand. Financial planners sometimes recommend establishing a HECM line of credit early in retirement and letting it grow, creating a larger reserve for later years when expenses tend to increase.

5. Modified Tenure or Modified Term

Modified payment plans combine monthly payments with a line of credit. With a modified tenure plan, you receive smaller monthly payments for life and retain a line of credit for additional draws when needed. A modified term plan works similarly but with payments over a fixed period instead of for life.

These hybrid options provide both the security of regular income and the flexibility of on-demand access to funds. I find that many borrowers prefer this approach because it balances predictable cash flow with the ability to handle unexpected expenses.

Fees and Costs of a Reverse Mortgage

Understanding the cost structure is important for making an informed decision about a reverse mortgage. While the fees can be significant, most can be financed into the loan balance, meaning you typically do not pay them out of pocket.

Origination Fee

Lenders charge an origination fee for processing and underwriting the reverse mortgage. FHA caps this fee based on the home value. For homes valued at $125,000 or less, the maximum fee is $2,500. For homes above $125,000, the lender can charge 2% of the first $200,000 plus 1% of the amount above $200,000, up to a maximum of $6,000. Some lenders offer reduced or waived origination fees to remain competitive.

FHA Mortgage Insurance Premiums

HECM loans require two types of mortgage insurance. The initial mortgage insurance premium (IMIP) is 2% of the maximum claim amount, charged at closing. The annual mortgage insurance premium (MIP) is 0.5% of the outstanding loan balance, accrued monthly. These premiums fund the FHA insurance pool that provides the non-recourse protection and lender guarantees that make the HECM program possible.

Closing Costs

Standard closing costs for a HECM include the appraisal fee (typically $400 to $700), title search and insurance, recording fees, survey fees if required, credit report fees, and other settlement charges. These costs vary by location and can generally range from $2,000 to $5,000 depending on the property and jurisdiction.

Servicing Fees

Loan servicers may charge a monthly servicing fee, though many lenders today incorporate servicing costs into the interest rate rather than charging a separate fee. When charged separately, servicing fees are typically capped at $30 to $35 per month. The servicing fee set-aside is deducted from your available proceeds at closing to cover future servicing charges over the expected loan term.

Ongoing Interest

Interest accrues on the outstanding loan balance throughout the life of the loan. For variable-rate HECMs, the interest rate adjusts monthly or annually based on a benchmark index plus the lender margin. For fixed-rate HECMs, the rate remains constant. Because no payments are made during the life of the loan, interest compounds over time, which means the loan balance can grow substantially over many years.

Borrower Obligations and Responsibilities

Taking out a reverse mortgage does not eliminate all housing-related financial obligations. You must continue meeting several requirements to keep the loan in good standing and avoid potential foreclosure.

Property taxes must be paid on time. Many reverse mortgage servicers encourage or require borrowers to set aside funds from their proceeds into a tax and insurance set-aside account, similar to an escrow account, to ensure these payments are covered. If you fail to pay property taxes, the loan servicer may advance the payments on your behalf, which adds to your loan balance and could eventually trigger a default.

Homeowners insurance must be maintained continuously. Your home is the collateral for the loan, and both the lender and FHA require adequate coverage. If your property is in a flood zone, you must also maintain flood insurance.

You must keep the home in reasonable condition. This does not mean luxury renovations, but it does mean addressing necessary repairs and maintenance to preserve the home's structural integrity and value. HUD conducts occupancy checks to verify that borrowers continue to live in the home as their primary residence.

Homeowners association fees, if applicable, must remain current. Any ground rents or special assessments also fall under your responsibility as the homeowner.

Non-Recourse Protection Explained

The non-recourse feature of HECM loans is one of the most important consumer protections in the program. It guarantees that the total amount owed on the reverse mortgage can never exceed the fair market value of the home at the time the loan is repaid. This protection applies regardless of how much the loan balance has grown due to interest accrual, regardless of how long the borrower lives in the home, and regardless of any decline in the home's market value.

When the loan becomes due, if the home sells for more than the loan balance, you or your heirs keep the remaining equity. If the loan balance exceeds the home value, FHA insurance covers the difference. The lender absorbs no loss, and your heirs owe nothing beyond the home's value. This feature makes the reverse mortgage fundamentally different from other types of home equity debt, where a decline in property value could leave borrowers underwater with personal liability for the shortfall.

I consider the non-recourse protection to be the single most important feature of the HECM program for risk-averse retirees. It creates a floor beneath the worst-case scenario. No matter what happens to interest rates, home values, or the economy, you will never be asked to repay more than the home is worth.

Implications for Heirs and Estate Planning

Understanding how a reverse mortgage affects your estate helps you plan appropriately and set realistic expectations for your heirs. The loan does not transfer to your children or other beneficiaries. Instead, when the last surviving borrower passes away, the heirs have several options and a defined timeline to exercise them.

The loan servicer must notify heirs within 30 days of learning about the borrower's death. Heirs then typically have six months to repay the loan or arrange for the home's sale, with extensions available for up to 12 months if they are actively working toward a resolution. During this period, interest continues to accrue on the outstanding balance.

Options Available to Heirs

  • Sell the home, repay the loan balance, and keep any remaining equity. If the home is worth more than the loan balance, the heirs benefit from the difference.
  • Pay off the reverse mortgage balance (or 95% of the appraised value, whichever is less) using personal funds or by refinancing into a traditional mortgage. This allows heirs to keep the home.
  • Provide the lender with a deed in lieu of foreclosure if the loan balance exceeds the home value. Because of the non-recourse provision, heirs owe nothing beyond the home itself.
  • Simply walk away from the property if the loan balance exceeds the value and they have no interest in the home. No deficiency judgment can be pursued against them.

For estate planning purposes, I recommend that borrowers have open conversations with their heirs about the reverse mortgage. Understanding the timeline and options in advance reduces stress and prevents surprises during an already difficult period.

HUD Counseling Requirement

Before you can apply for a HECM, federal law requires you to complete a counseling session with an independent, HUD-approved housing counseling agency. This requirement exists to protect consumers by ensuring they fully understand the reverse mortgage product before committing to it.

The counseling session typically lasts 60 to 90 minutes and can be conducted in person or by telephone. The counselor is required to cover several specific topics, including how reverse mortgages work, the total cost of the loan over time, alternatives to a reverse mortgage that might better serve your needs, the financial and tax implications, the obligations you must maintain, and the impact on public benefits like Medicaid.

The counseling fee is typically around $125, though some agencies offer reduced rates or free counseling depending on your financial situation. You can find a HUD-approved counseling agency through the HUD website or by calling the HUD hotline. The counselor cannot recommend a specific lender and must provide objective, unbiased guidance.

After completing the counseling session, you receive a certificate that is valid for 180 days. You need this certificate to proceed with a HECM application. I strongly encourage prospective borrowers to complete counseling early in the exploration process, even before speaking with lenders. The knowledge you gain makes you a more informed consumer and helps you ask better questions when shopping for the best terms.

FHA Lending Limits and Maximum Claim Amount

The FHA sets a national lending limit that applies to all HECM reverse mortgages. For 2024, this limit is $1,149,825. The maximum claim amount for your loan is the lesser of your home's appraised value or this FHA limit. If your home is worth $800,000, your maximum claim amount is $800,000. If your home is worth $1,500,000, the maximum claim amount is capped at $1,149,825.

This limit applies uniformly across the country, unlike FHA forward mortgage limits that vary by county. It is adjusted periodically to reflect changes in housing values. Borrowers with homes exceeding the FHA limit can still obtain a HECM, but their available proceeds are calculated based on the capped amount rather than the full appraised value.

For homeowners with high-value properties who want to access more equity than the HECM limit allows, proprietary (jumbo) reverse mortgages are available from some private lenders. These products do not carry FHA insurance and have different terms, rates, and protections compared to HECMs. I generally recommend exploring the HECM option first because of its consumer protections and competitive pricing.

Pros and Cons of Reverse Mortgages

A reverse mortgage is neither universally good nor universally bad. Like any financial tool, its value depends on your specific situation, goals, and alternatives. I have worked through the numbers on hundreds of scenarios, and the following summary reflects what I consider the most relevant advantages and disadvantages.

Advantages

No monthly mortgage payments required. You retain home ownership and title. Non-recourse protection ensures you never owe more than the home is worth. Tax-free loan proceeds (consult your tax advisor). Line of credit growth feature provides increasing access over time. Multiple payment options offer flexibility. FHA insurance protects against lender failure. Can be used to eliminate existing mortgage payments. Supplements retirement income without selling the home. Proceeds do not affect Social Security or Medicare benefits.

Disadvantages

Reduces home equity over time, leaving less for heirs. Upfront costs can be significant (origination fee, MIP, closing costs). Interest compounds on the growing balance. May affect Medicaid eligibility if proceeds are not managed carefully. Requires ongoing property tax, insurance, and maintenance payments. Fixed-rate option limits you to a lump sum. Variable rates mean uncertain long-term interest costs. Home must remain your primary residence. Limits your flexibility to downsize or relocate. You must maintain the property to FHA standards.

Alternatives to a Reverse Mortgage

Before committing to a reverse mortgage, consider whether other options might better serve your financial goals. Each alternative has its own trade-offs, and the right choice depends on your income needs, risk tolerance, health situation, and long-term plans.

Home Equity Loan or Home Equity Line of Credit

A traditional home equity loan or HELOC lets you borrow against your home equity while making regular payments. Interest rates may be lower than reverse mortgage rates, and you maintain the option to pay off the balance without selling. However, you must qualify based on income and credit, and the required monthly payments may strain a fixed retirement budget.

Downsizing

Selling your current home and purchasing a smaller, less expensive property frees up equity in a single transaction. You pocket the difference and potentially reduce your ongoing housing costs. This approach works well if you are open to moving and the local housing market is favorable for sellers. The downside is the emotional and logistical disruption of relocating, plus transaction costs on both the sale and purchase.

Cash-Out Refinance

If you have sufficient income to qualify, a cash-out refinance replaces your existing mortgage with a larger one and provides the difference as cash. Rates are generally lower than reverse mortgage rates, but you take on a new monthly payment obligation. This option is more suitable for borrowers with stable ongoing income.

Government and Community Programs

Many states and localities offer property tax relief programs, energy assistance, home repair grants, and other support for seniors. These programs can reduce your monthly expenses without taking on new debt. Check with your local Area Agency on Aging or state housing finance agency for available programs.

Family Arrangements

Some families work out private arrangements where children or other relatives provide financial assistance in exchange for a future interest in the home, or the family collectively decides on a strategy for managing the parents' housing costs. While these arrangements can work well, I recommend documenting everything in writing with legal counsel to prevent misunderstandings.

Common Misconceptions About Reverse Mortgages

Misinformation about reverse mortgages remains widespread. Correcting these misconceptions helps you evaluate the product on its actual merits rather than outdated or inaccurate assumptions.

The Bank Owns Your Home

This is the most persistent myth. You retain full title and ownership of your home with a reverse mortgage. The lender has a lien on the property, just as with any mortgage, but you remain the owner. You can sell at any time, refinance, or leave the home to your heirs.

You Can Be Forced Out of Your Home

As long as you meet your borrower obligations (property taxes, insurance, maintenance, and primary residence requirement), you cannot be forced to leave your home. The loan does not become due simply because you reach a certain age or the loan balance grows large. The protections are built into the program by federal regulation.

Reverse Mortgages Are Only for Desperate Situations

While some borrowers turn to reverse mortgages during financial hardship, many financially comfortable retirees use them as strategic tools. The line of credit growth feature, the ability to defer Social Security, tax planning advantages, and portfolio longevity strategies all represent complex uses of the product that have nothing to do with financial desperation.

Your Heirs Will Be Stuck With the Debt

The non-recourse feature means heirs are never personally liable for the reverse mortgage debt. If the loan balance exceeds the home value, FHA insurance absorbs the loss. Heirs can walk away with no financial consequence, or they can purchase the home for the lesser of the loan balance or 95% of the appraised value.

Reverse Mortgage Proceeds Are Taxable

Reverse mortgage proceeds are loan advances, not income, and are generally not subject to federal income tax. This makes them an attractive source of funds for retirees who want to access cash without increasing their tax burden. However, interest paid on the reverse mortgage is not deductible until it is actually paid, which typically occurs when the loan is repaid. Consult a tax professional for guidance specific to your situation.

You Cannot Get a Reverse Mortgage if You Have an Existing Mortgage

You can obtain a HECM even if you have an existing mortgage, provided the reverse mortgage proceeds are sufficient to pay off the remaining balance at closing. In fact, one of the most common uses of a reverse mortgage is to eliminate monthly mortgage payments by paying off the existing loan.

When a Reverse Mortgage Makes Sense

After analyzing thousands of retirement scenarios, I have identified several situations where a reverse mortgage can be particularly effective as a financial planning tool.

If you plan to stay in your home for the foreseeable future and want to eliminate monthly mortgage payments, a reverse mortgage directly addresses that goal. The reduction in monthly obligations can significantly improve cash flow during retirement, especially when combined with Social Security and pension income.

If you want to delay Social Security benefits to increase your monthly check, a reverse mortgage can provide bridge income during the deferral period. Each year you delay Social Security past age 62 increases your benefit by approximately 7% to 8%, making this a potentially effective combination strategy.

If you need to fund home modifications for aging in place, such as installing grab bars, widening doorways, adding a ground-floor bathroom, or updating electrical and plumbing systems, reverse mortgage proceeds can fund these improvements without monthly repayment obligations.

If you want a financial safety net for unexpected expenses in retirement, establishing a HECM line of credit early and letting it grow provides a reserve that increases over time. You pay no interest on the unused portion, making it a relatively low-cost standby resource.

A reverse mortgage may not be the right choice if you plan to move within a few years, if you want to leave the maximum possible inheritance to your heirs, if you have other lower-cost sources of funds available, or if the upfront costs would consume too much of your available equity. Every situation is different, and there is no substitute for working through the numbers with a qualified professional.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is a reverse mortgage and how does it work? +

A reverse mortgage is a loan available to homeowners aged 62 and older that converts home equity into cash without requiring monthly mortgage payments. The most common type is the Home Equity Conversion Mortgage (HECM), insured by FHA. Unlike a traditional mortgage where you pay the lender monthly, a reverse mortgage pays you. The loan balance grows over time as interest accrues on the amount borrowed. Repayment is deferred until you sell the home, move out permanently, or pass away. You retain full ownership and title throughout the life of the loan.

How much can I borrow with a reverse mortgage? +

The amount depends on the youngest borrower's age, the home's appraised value (capped at the FHA lending limit of $1,149,825), and current interest rates. Older borrowers with more valuable homes and lower interest rates receive higher proceeds. Principal limit factors typically range from about 25% to 75% of the home value. After subtracting mandatory costs like origination fees, mortgage insurance, closing costs, and any existing mortgage balance, the remaining amount is your net available proceeds.

What are the eligibility requirements? +

To qualify for a HECM, you must be at least 62 years old, own the home as your primary residence, have sufficient equity (owning outright or with a low remaining mortgage balance), not be delinquent on any federal debt, and complete HUD-approved counseling. The property must also meet FHA minimum standards and pass an FHA appraisal. Eligible property types include single-family homes, two-to-four unit properties, HUD-approved condos, and qualifying manufactured homes.

What payment options can I choose? +

HECM reverse mortgages offer five payment options: lump sum (single payment at closing, available with fixed or variable rate), tenure (monthly payments for as long as you live in the home), term (monthly payments for a chosen period), line of credit (draw funds as needed with a growing credit line), and modified plans (combining monthly payments with a line of credit). You can change your payment option after closing for a small administrative fee.

Do I still own my home? +

Yes. You retain the title and full ownership of your home with a reverse mortgage. The lender places a lien on the property, just as with any mortgage, but you remain the homeowner. You can sell the home at any time, and you continue to benefit from any appreciation in value. The loan does not transfer ownership to the bank or any other party.

What happens when the borrower passes away? +

When the last surviving borrower passes away, heirs have several options. They can sell the home and keep any equity remaining after repaying the loan. They can refinance the reverse mortgage into a traditional mortgage to keep the property. If the loan balance exceeds the home value, heirs can surrender the home with no personal liability due to the non-recourse protection. Heirs typically have six months, with possible extensions up to 12 months, to resolve the loan.

What fees are involved? +

Key fees include the origination fee (capped at $6,000), FHA initial mortgage insurance premium (2% of the maximum claim amount), annual mortgage insurance (0.5% of the loan balance), and standard closing costs ($2,000 to $5,000). Most fees can be financed into the loan so you do not pay them out of pocket. Servicing fees may also apply, though many lenders build them into the interest rate.

Can I lose my home with a reverse mortgage? +

As long as you fulfill your borrower obligations, you cannot lose your home. However, you can face foreclosure if you fail to pay property taxes, homeowners insurance, or HOA fees, if you do not maintain the property in reasonable condition, or if you move out of the home for more than 12 consecutive months. Staying current on these responsibilities protects your right to remain in the home indefinitely.

Is HUD counseling mandatory? +

Yes, HUD-approved counseling is required by federal law before you can apply for a HECM. The session covers how reverse mortgages work, costs and financial implications, alternatives, and borrower obligations. It can be completed in person or by phone and typically costs about $125. The counselor provides an independent, unbiased assessment and cannot recommend specific lenders. Your counseling certificate is valid for 180 days.

How does the line of credit grow over time? +

The unused portion of a HECM line of credit grows at a rate equal to the loan interest rate plus the annual mortgage insurance premium (0.5%). This growth is not tied to home value appreciation. It occurs automatically, increasing your available credit regardless of housing market conditions. This feature makes the HECM line of credit unique among all financial products and particularly valuable as a long-term financial planning tool in retirement.

Are reverse mortgage proceeds taxable? +

Reverse mortgage proceeds are loan advances, not income, and are generally not subject to federal income tax. They do not count as taxable income for Social Security benefit calculations or Medicare premium surcharges. However, if proceeds are not spent in the month received, they could count as assets for purposes of means-tested programs like Medicaid. Interest is not deductible until actually paid, which typically occurs at loan repayment. Consult a tax advisor for your specific situation.

What is the FHA lending limit for HECM loans? +

The current FHA lending limit for HECM reverse mortgages is $1,149,825 (2024). Even if your home is worth more, the maximum claim amount used to calculate your available proceeds is capped at this figure. This limit applies nationally and is adjusted periodically by FHA. Homeowners with properties exceeding this limit may want to explore proprietary (jumbo) reverse mortgages from private lenders, though these lack FHA insurance protections.

Community Questions

Q Can I get a reverse mortgage if I still owe $150,000 on my home?

Yes, as long as the reverse mortgage proceeds are large enough to pay off your existing mortgage at closing. The existing mortgage is paid off first from the gross proceeds, and you receive what remains. For example, on a $400,000 home with a 72-year-old borrower at a 6.5% expected rate, the principal limit factor is approximately 0.424, giving gross proceeds of about $169,600. After paying off the $150,000 mortgage and roughly $14,500 in closing costs, you would receive approximately $5,100 in net proceeds. If your existing balance is too high relative to the available proceeds, you would not qualify.

Q How does the line of credit growth feature work in practice?

The unused portion of your HECM line of credit grows at a rate equal to the loan interest rate plus the annual MIP rate (0.5%). If your interest rate is 6% and MIP is 0.5%, the unused credit line grows at 6.5% per year. On a $100,000 initial credit line, this means $106,500 is available after year 1, $113,420 after year 2, and approximately $187,710 after year 10, without touching any of the money. This growth is guaranteed regardless of what happens to your home value, making it a powerful financial planning tool for future needs.

Q What happens if my loan balance exceeds my home value after 20 years?

Nothing changes for you. HECM reverse mortgages are non-recourse loans, meaning the maximum repayment amount is the lesser of the loan balance or 95% of the current appraised home value. If your loan balance reaches $500,000 but the home is only worth $350,000, you (or your heirs) owe only $350,000. FHA insurance covers the $150,000 difference. You funded this protection through the upfront MIP (2% of home value) and the annual MIP (0.5%) charged on the loan balance. This protection exists for the life of the loan.

Community Questions

Q

Can I lose my home with a reverse mortgage?

A reverse mortgage (HECM) is a non-recourse loan, meaning you will never owe more than the home is worth at sale. However, you can face foreclosure if you fail to pay property taxes, homeowners insurance, or maintain the home. You must also continue to live in the home as your primary residence. Moving to a nursing home for more than 12 consecutive months triggers the loan becoming due and payable.

Q

How does a reverse mortgage affect my heirs?

When the last surviving borrower passes away, the heirs have several options. They can sell the home and keep any equity above the loan balance. If the loan exceeds the home value, they can walk away owing nothing due to the non-recourse protection. They can also pay off the loan (at 95 percent of appraised value if underwater) to keep the home. HUD gives heirs 6 months to settle, with possible extensions up to 12 months.

Q

What is the HECM line of credit growth rate and why does it matter?

The unused portion of a HECM line of credit grows at the same rate as the loan interest rate plus the annual mortgage insurance premium (currently 0.5 percent). For example, at a 6.5 percent interest rate, your available credit grows at roughly 7 percent per year. This growth is guaranteed regardless of home value changes, making the line of credit option particularly valuable in a rising-rate environment since your borrowing power increases over time.
