How to Use This Speed Distance Time Calculator

I've this calculator to handle the single most common math problem people search for: given two of speed, distance, and time, find the third. The basic calculator does exactly that. Enter any two values, leave the third blank, and hit Calculate. The tool handles unit conversion automatically so you can enter speed in km/h and distance in miles if that's what you have. It figures it out.

The round trip calculator is where things get interesting because most people get the math wrong. If you drive to work at 60 km/h and drive home at 40 km/h, your average speed for the whole trip is not 50 km/h. It's 48 km/h. The reason is that you spend more time at the slower speed, which pulls the average down. This is the harmonic mean, not the arithmetic mean, and it catches people off guard constantly. I've verified this against textbook examples and the math is solid.

The Three Fundamental Formulas

Speed, distance, and time are related by the simplest equations in physics. According to Wikipedia's article on speed, the average speed of an object is defined as the distance traveled divided by the time taken. From this single definition, all three formulas follow.

Speed = Distance / Time
Distance = Speed × Time
Time = Distance / Speed

These are sometimes taught using the "SDT triangle" where you cover the quantity you find. If you cover Speed, you see Distance over Time (D/T). Cover Distance, you see Speed times Time (S×T). Cover Time, you see Distance over Speed (D/S). It's a useful mnemonic for students.

These formulas give average speed, not instantaneous speed. If you drive 100 km in 2 hours, your average speed is 50 km/h, but your instantaneous speed at any given moment could have been anywhere from 0 (stopped at a light) to 120 km/h (highway stretch). For instantaneous speed, you'd need calculus or a speedometer reading.

I've tested this calculator across a wide range of values and edge cases. It handles very small speeds (walking pace at 1.4 m/s), very high speeds (Mach numbers), very long distances (interplanetary), and very short times (milliseconds). The only limitation is that it assumes constant average speed for each calculation. For problems involving acceleration or changing speed, use the companion acceleration calculator.

The Round Trip Average Speed Trap

This is one of my favorite math problems because almost everyone gets it wrong on first attempt. Here's the scenario: you drive 100 km to a destination at 60 km/h and return the same 100 km at 40 km/h. What's your average speed for the whole trip?

Most people say 50 km/h (the arithmetic mean of 60 and 40). But that's wrong. Here's why:

  • Going: 100 km at 60 km/h takes 100/60 = 1.667 hours
  • Returning: 100 km at 40 km/h takes 100/40 = 2.5 hours
  • Total distance: 200 km
  • Total time: 1.667 + 2.5 = 4.167 hours
  • Average speed: 200/4.167 = 48 km/h

The correct formula for average speed on a round trip is the harmonic mean:

Average Speed = 2 × S1 × S2 / (S1 + S2)

Plugging in: 2 × 60 × 40 / (60 + 40) = 4800 / 100 = 48 km/h. This always gives a value lower than the arithmetic mean because you spend more time at the slower speed.

This isn't just a trick question. It has real-world implications. If you're planning a road trip and estimate your average speed using the arithmetic mean, you'll underestimate your total travel time. The round trip calculator in this tool uses the harmonic mean correctly and shows you the breakdown so you can see exactly where the time goes. There's a great discussion about this on Stack Overflow that covers the mathematical proof in detail.

Mach Number Reference

The Mach number, named after physicist Ernst Mach, is the ratio of an object's speed to the speed of sound in the surrounding medium. It's a critical concept in aerodynamics and aerospace engineering. According to Wikipedia's Mach number article, the speed of sound varies with temperature, altitude, and the medium through which sound travels.

RegimeMach Rangekm/h (sea level, 15C)Characteristics
Subsonic< 0.8< 988Normal flight, smooth airflow

Something that doesn't get mentioned often enough: the speed of sound is not a constant. At sea level and 15 degrees Celsius, it's 343 m/s (1,235 km/h). But at cruising altitude (35,000 feet where it's about minus 57 degrees Celsius), it drops to about 295 m/s (1,062 km/h). This is why aircraft Mach numbers and indicated airspeeds can differ significantly from ground speed. A Boeing 777 cruising at Mach 0.84 at altitude is moving at about 892 km/h relative to the air, but its ground speed can vary significantly depending on wind.

The knot deserves special attention. One knot equals one nautical mile per hour, and one nautical mile is exactly 1,852 meters (defined as one minute of arc of latitude). This makes it the natural unit for navigation because it ties directly to the coordinate system. If you're sailing or flying along a line of latitude, one degree equals 60 nautical miles. That's not a coincidence, it's the definition.

The mile per hour (mph) and kilometer per hour (km/h) conversion factor of 1.60934 is probably the most commonly needed conversion in everyday life. A quick mental shortcut: multiply mph by 1.6 to get km/h, or multiply km/h by 0.6 to get mph. It's not exact but it's close enough for practical purposes. I've found this handy for quick mental conversions when driving in countries that use different systems.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do you calculate speed from distance and time? ▼
Speed equals distance divided by time. For example, if you travel 100 miles in 2 hours, your average speed is 100/2 = 50 mph. Make sure your units are consistent. This calculator handles unit conversion automatically.
How do you calculate distance from speed and time? ▼
Distance equals speed multiplied by time. For example, driving at 60 km/h for 3 hours covers 60 × 3 = 180 km. Remember to convert time to hours if your speed is in km/h or mph.
Why is the average speed for a round trip not the average of the two speeds? ▼
Because you spend more time traveling at the slower speed, which pulls the average down. The correct calculation uses the harmonic mean: Average = 2 × S1 × S2 / (S1 + S2). For 60 km/h going and 40 km/h returning, that's 2 × 60 × 40 / (60+40) = 48 km/h, not 50.
What is a knot? ▼
A knot is one nautical mile per hour, equal to 1.852 km/h or 1.15078 mph. It's the standard speed unit in maritime navigation and aviation because nautical miles are tied to latitude coordinates (1 nautical mile = 1 minute of arc of latitude).
What is Mach number? ▼
Mach number is the ratio of an object's speed to the local speed of sound. Mach 1 equals the speed of sound, approximately 343 m/s or 1,235 km/h at sea level and 15 degrees Celsius. The speed of sound varies with temperature and altitude.
How do I calculate running pace? ▼
Running pace is minutes per unit distance. Divide total minutes by distance: if you run 5 km in 25 minutes, your pace is 25/5 = 5:00 min/km. To convert pace to speed: speed (km/h) = 60 / pace (min/km). So 5:00/km = 12 km/h.
How accurate is the fuel cost estimator? ▼
The fuel cost estimator provides a close approximation based on distance, fuel efficiency, and fuel price. Real-world costs vary with driving style, speed, terrain, traffic, weather, and vehicle load. Use it for planning and budgeting, not exact cost prediction.
What is the difference between US and UK gallons? ▼
A US gallon is 3.785 liters while a UK (Imperial) gallon is 4.546 liters. This means 30 MPG (US) and 30 MPG (UK) represent very different fuel efficiencies. 30 MPG (US) equals about 36 MPG (UK). Always check which gallon standard is being used.
How do I calculate speed from distance and time? ▼
Divide the distance by the time taken. If you drove 240 miles in 4 hours, your speed is 240 / 4 = 60 mph. Enter the distance and time in this speed distance time calculator and leave the speed field empty, and it returns the average speed with units converted for you.
How does an average speed calculator work? ▼
An average speed calculator divides total distance by total elapsed time, including any stops. This is why average speed is almost always lower than the speed shown on your speedometer. For a trip with several legs, use the multi-leg tab so each segment is summed before the average is calculated.
How do I find time from speed and distance? ▼
Divide the distance by the speed: time equals distance divided by speed. A 300-mile trip at 50 mph takes 300 / 50 = 6 hours. Enter the speed and distance and leave the time blank, and the calculator shows the duration in hours, minutes and seconds.

Using the Speed Distance Time Calculator to Find Any Value

This speed distance time calculator solves all three variables from a single relationship: speed equals distance divided by time. Enter any two known values and the tool returns the third instantly, so you can calculate speed from distance and time, work out how far you will travel, or find the time a journey will take. Because it converts units as you type, you can mix miles with hours, kilometres with minutes, or nautical miles with knots without doing any manual conversion first.

To calculate speed from distance and time, enter the distance you covered and the time it took, then leave the speed field blank. The result is your average speed across the whole trip, which is exactly what an average speed calculator reports. To find time from speed and distance, enter a target speed and the distance, and the tool returns the journey duration in hours, minutes and seconds. The same three-way logic powers every tab, including the round trip and multi-leg planners.

A few tips make the results more reliable:

  • Use average speed, not top speed, when estimating real travel time, since traffic and stops lower the effective rate.
  • For round trips, trust the harmonic-mean result rather than averaging the two leg speeds yourself.
  • Keep distance and speed in matching systems (metric or imperial) unless you are deliberately using the unit converter.
