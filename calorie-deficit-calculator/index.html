Understanding Your Basal Metabolic Rate

Your Basal Metabolic Rate represents the number of calories your body burns just to stay alive while completely at rest. This includes the energy needed for breathing, circulating blood, maintaining body temperature, growing and repairing cells, and keeping your brain and organs functioning. BMR typically accounts for 60 to 75 percent of your total daily calorie expenditure, making it the single largest component of your energy budget.

From Wikipedia

A calorie deficit is any shortage in the number of calories consumed relative to the number of calories required for maintenance of current body weight. A deficit can be created by reducing input (eating less), increasing output (exercising more), or a combination of both. When a sustained calorie deficit is maintained, the body draws on stored energy reserves, primarily fat, to make up the difference.

Read more on Wikipedia

This calculator uses the Mifflin-St Jeor equation, which research published in the Journal of the American Dietetic Association found to be the most accurate predictive equation for estimating BMR in both normal weight and obese individuals. For men, the formula is (10 times weight in kg) + (6.25 times height in cm) - (5 times age in years) + 5. For women, the formula is the same except the constant at the end is -161 instead of +5. These gender-specific constants account for the fact that men generally have more lean body mass than women at the same height and weight.

How TDEE Determines Your Calorie Needs

Total Daily Energy Expenditure is your BMR multiplied by an activity factor that accounts for all the physical activity you perform throughout the day. The activity multipliers range from 1.2 for sedentary individuals to 1.9 for extremely active people. A sedentary person with a BMR of 1,600 calories would have a TDEE of approximately 1,920 calories, while a very active person with the same BMR would have a TDEE of about 2,760 calories.

Choosing the correct activity level is crucial for accurate results. Most people overestimate their activity level. If you have an office job and work out for 30 to 45 minutes three times per week, the lightly active setting is most appropriate. Moderately active is suitable if you exercise at moderate intensity for 45 to 60 minutes most days. Very active applies to people who engage in intense physical training almost every day or have physically demanding jobs. Being honest about your activity level leads to more accurate calorie targets and better results.

The Science Behind Calorie Deficits

Weight loss occurs when you consistently consume fewer calories than your body expends. This is the law of energy balance, and it applies universally regardless of the specific foods you eat. Approximately 3,500 calories of energy is stored in one pound (about 0.45 kg) of body fat. To lose one pound per week, you need a daily deficit of about 500 calories. To lose half a kilogram per week, a daily deficit of approximately 550 calories is required.

, weight loss is not perfectly linear in practice. As you lose weight, your BMR decreases because there is less body mass to maintain. Your TDEE also decreases unless you increase your activity level proportionally. This means that a calorie target that initially produces steady weight loss will eventually result in a plateau as your new, lower body weight reaches equilibrium with your calorie intake. Periodic recalculation (every 4 to 6 weeks) helps keep your deficit effective as your body changes.

Setting a Safe and Sustainable Deficit

A moderate calorie deficit of 500 to 750 calories per day is generally recommended by dietitians and health organizations. This produces weight loss of approximately 0.5 to 0.75 kilograms per week, a rate that is sustainable and reduces muscle loss. Faster rates of weight loss typically require more aggressive restriction and carry higher risks of nutritional deficiencies, muscle wasting, gallstones, and metabolic adaptation.

Most health professionals recommend that women consume no fewer than 1,200 calories per day and men no fewer than 1,500 calories per day without medical supervision. These minimums exist because it is very difficult to meet essential vitamin, mineral, and macronutrient needs below these thresholds. Very low calorie diets (below 800 calories per day) should only be followed under direct medical supervision for individuals with a BMI over 30 who have failed to lose weight with conventional approaches.

This calculator will display a warning if your calculated calorie target falls below 1,200 calories per day. If you see this warning, consider reducing your target weight loss rate or increasing your activity level to create a larger TDEE, which allows a deficit while keeping absolute calorie intake at a safe level.

Macro Split Recommendations for Fat Loss

The recommended macro split of 30 percent protein, 40 percent carbohydrates, and 30 percent fat is specifically for a weight loss context. Higher protein intake (around 30 percent of calories or about 1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight) is well-supported by research for preserving lean muscle mass during a calorie deficit. Protein also has the highest thermic effect of any macronutrient, meaning your body burns more calories digesting protein than carbohydrates or fat.

The 40 percent carbohydrate allocation provides adequate fuel for physical activity and brain function. Carbohydrates are your body's preferred energy source for moderate to high intensity exercise, and adequate intake supports workout performance and recovery. The 30 percent fat allocation ensures sufficient intake of essential fatty acids and fat-soluble vitamins while keeping total fat moderate. Fat provides satiety and improves the taste of food, both of which support long-term adherence to a calorie deficit.

These percentages are starting points and can be adjusted based on personal preference and response. Some people do better with higher fat and lower carbs, while others prefer higher carbs for athletic performance. The most important factor is total calorie intake, followed by adequate protein. The carb and fat ratio is less critical as long as both are within reasonable ranges and total calories are controlled.

Understanding the Weight Loss Timeline

The projected timeline this calculator provides assumes a consistent daily calorie deficit maintained over the entire period. In reality, weight loss is rarely perfectly linear. You will likely experience weeks where weight drops quickly followed by weeks where the scale barely moves or even shows a slight increase. Daily weight fluctuations of 1 to 3 pounds are completely normal and are caused by variations in water retention, food volume in the digestive tract, and hormonal cycles.

To get an accurate picture of your progress, weigh yourself under consistent conditions (same time of day, same clothing or none, after using the bathroom) and track the weekly average rather than daily readings. A downward trend in weekly averages over a month confirms that your deficit is working, even if individual daily readings fluctuate significantly. If your weekly average stops declining for three or more consecutive weeks, it may be time to recalculate your calorie target based on your new, lower body weight.

Common Mistakes When Creating a Calorie Deficit

The most common mistake is creating too large a deficit too quickly. Cutting calories dramatically feels productive in the first week as the scale drops rapidly, but most of that initial loss is water weight rather than fat. Aggressive restriction also triggers stronger hunger signals, increases cravings, and makes long-term adherence much harder. A moderate deficit that you can maintain for months will produce better long-term results than an extreme deficit that you abandon after two weeks.

Another frequent error is not accounting for liquid calories. Beverages like sodas, juices, lattes, smoothies, and alcoholic drinks can easily add 300 to 600 calories per day without providing much satiety. Many people who believe they are in a deficit are actually at maintenance because they track their food but forget to track their drinks. Similarly, cooking oils, salad dressings, condiments, and snacking between meals can add significant untracked calories.

Not eating enough protein is a third major mistake. During a calorie deficit, your body needs amino acids from protein to maintain and repair muscle tissue. If protein intake is too low, your body will break down muscle for amino acids, resulting in a higher percentage of weight loss coming from lean tissue rather than fat. This reduces your metabolic rate and leads to a softer body composition even at a lower weight. Aim for at least 1.6 grams of protein per kilogram of body weight while in a deficit.

When to Recalculate Your Calorie Target

Recalculating every 4 to 6 weeks or after every 5 kilograms of weight loss is a good practice. As your body weight decreases, your BMR drops and your TDEE decreases proportionally. A calorie target that created a 500-calorie deficit at 90 kilograms might only create a 300-calorie deficit at 80 kilograms, significantly slowing your rate of weight loss. Regular recalculation ensures your deficit remains effective throughout your entire weight loss journey.

You should also recalculate if your activity level changes significantly. Starting a new exercise program, changing jobs to one that is more or less physically active, or recovering from an injury that limits movement all affect your TDEE. Adjusting your calorie target to reflect your current reality prevents both under-eating (which can impair recovery and performance) and over-eating (which stalls weight loss).

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Mifflin-St Jeor equation?
The Mifflin-St Jeor equation is a formula developed in 1990 that estimates Basal Metabolic Rate based on weight, height, age, and gender. It has been validated in numerous studies and is currently considered the most accurate predictive equation for BMR. The American Dietetic Association recommends it over older equations like Harris-Benedict for estimating calorie needs.
How many calories should I cut to lose weight?
A daily deficit of 500 calories below your TDEE will produce approximately 0.45 kilograms (1 pound) of fat loss per week. A deficit of 250 calories produces about half a pound per week. Most experts recommend a deficit of 500 to 750 calories per day for sustainable weight loss. Larger deficits can be used short-term but are harder to maintain and may lead to muscle loss.
Is 1200 calories a day safe?
For most women, 1,200 calories per day is considered the minimum for safe weight loss without medical supervision. For most men, the minimum is 1,500 calories. Below these levels, it becomes very difficult to consume adequate vitamins, minerals, fiber, and protein. If your calculated target falls below these thresholds, consider increasing your activity level or reducing your target loss rate.
Why does weight loss slow down over time?
As you lose weight, your body requires fewer calories to maintain its reduced mass, so your BMR and TDEE decrease. This means the same calorie intake that created a significant deficit initially will create a smaller deficit as you get lighter., your body may undergo metabolic adaptation, slightly reducing its energy expenditure beyond what weight loss alone would predict. Regular recalculation and gradual adjustments keep progress moving.
Should I eat back exercise calories?
It depends on how your TDEE was calculated. If you selected an activity level that already accounts for your exercise routine, eating back exercise calories would double-count that activity and reduce your deficit. If you selected sedentary and then exercise on top of that, eating back a portion (about 50 to 75 percent) of estimated exercise calories is reasonable. Fitness trackers tend to overestimate exercise calories, which is why eating back only a portion is recommended.
How much protein should I eat while losing weight?
Research consistently shows that higher protein intake (1.6 to 2.2 grams per kilogram of body weight) during a calorie deficit helps preserve lean muscle mass, improves satiety, and slightly increases calorie burn through the thermic effect of food. This calculator recommends 30 percent of your calories from protein, which typically falls within this range for most people.
What is TDEE and why does it matter?
TDEE stands for Total Daily Energy Expenditure. It represents the total number of calories your body burns in a day, including your BMR plus all physical activity and the thermic effect of food. TDEE is the number you eat below in order to lose weight. Eating at your TDEE maintains your current weight, eating above it causes weight gain, and eating below it causes weight loss.
Can I build muscle while in a calorie deficit?
It is possible but difficult, especially for trained individuals. Beginners to strength training, people returning after a break, and those with higher body fat percentages can often build some muscle while losing fat (called body recomposition). This requires adequate protein intake (at least 1.6 g/kg), a moderate deficit (not extreme), and progressive resistance training. Experienced lifters generally focus on either muscle gain or fat loss, not both simultaneously.
How accurate is this calorie calculator?
The Mifflin-St Jeor equation estimates BMR within about 10 percent accuracy for most individuals. The activity multiplier adds another source of variability, as actual energy expenditure during activity varies significantly between people. Treat the calculated TDEE as a starting estimate and adjust based on your real-world results over 2 to 3 weeks. If you are losing weight faster than expected, you can add calories. If slower, reduce them slightly.
Is this calculator free to use?
Yes, completely free with no registration, no ads, and no data collection. All calculations happen in your browser and no personal information is sent to any server. You can use the calculator as many times as you need.
What happens if I eat too few calories?
Consistently eating too few calories can lead to muscle loss, nutritional deficiencies, fatigue, weakened immune function, hair loss, hormonal disruption (including menstrual irregularity in women), decreased bone density, and metabolic adaptation where your body reduces its energy expenditure. These consequences make aggressive restriction counterproductive for long-term weight management. A moderate, sustainable deficit is always the better approach.
Should I take diet breaks during a calorie deficit?
Research suggests that periodic diet breaks (eating at maintenance calories for 1 to 2 weeks after every 4 to 8 weeks of dieting) may reduce metabolic adaptation, improve hormonal status, and support long-term adherence. While diet breaks temporarily pause weight loss, they do not cause fat regain if you eat at maintenance rather than above it. Many people find that the psychological relief of a planned break helps them sustain their deficit more consistently over months.
