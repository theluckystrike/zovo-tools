How Capital Gains Tax Works

Capital gains tax is what you owe when you sell an investment for more than you paid for it. I've found that many investors don't think about tax implications until they've already sold, which can lead to unpleasant surprises in April. Understanding how capital gains taxation works before you sell is one of the simplest ways to keep more of your investment returns.

The basic formula is straightforward. Your capital gain equals the sale price minus your cost basis. Cost basis is typically what you originally paid for the asset, plus any transaction costs (broker fees), plus improvements (for real estate). If you've reinvested dividends in a mutual fund, those reinvested amounts also increase your cost basis.

Capital losses (selling for less than you paid) can offset capital gains dollar for dollar. If your losses exceed your gains in a given year, you can deduct up to $3,000 of net capital losses against ordinary income ($1,500 if married filing separately). Any remaining losses carry forward to future tax years indefinitely.

The Wikipedia article on U.S. capital gains tax provides an excellent historical overview of how rates have changed over the decades. that long-term capital gains rates are currently at relatively favorable levels compared to their historical range.

Short-Term vs. Long-Term Capital Gains

The distinction between short-term and long-term capital gains is the single most important factor in determining your tax rate. The holding period determines which category your gain falls into, and the difference in tax treatment is substantial.

Short-Term Capital Gains

If you hold an asset for one year or less before selling, the gain is classified as short-term. Short-term capital gains are taxed at your ordinary income tax rate, which can be as high as 37% for high earners. This means there's no preferential tax treatment for short-term gains. They're taxed exactly like salary, wages, or freelance income.

For active traders and day traders, this is a critical consideration. Every trade that closes within a year generates short-term gains (or losses). The cumulative tax impact of frequent trading can dramatically reduce after-tax returns compared to a buy-and-hold strategy.

Long-Term Capital Gains

If you hold an asset for more than one year before selling, the gain qualifies for long-term capital gains treatment. Long-term rates are significantly lower than ordinary income rates for most taxpayers. The three rate tiers are 0%, 15%, and 20%, depending on your taxable income and filing status.

This preferential treatment exists to encourage long-term investment. The difference between short-term and long-term tax treatment can be enormous. A single filer earning $100,000 in ordinary income would pay 24% on a short-term gain but only 15% on a long-term gain. On a $50,000 profit, that's $12,000 vs. $7,500, a difference of $4,500 just for waiting an extra day past the one-year mark.

The holding period starts the day after you purchase the asset and includes the day you sell. If you buy stock on January 15, 2025, you must hold it until at least January 16, 2026 for the gain to qualify as long-term. Selling on January 15, 2026 would still be short-term.

Net Investment Income Tax (NIIT)

On top of the regular capital gains tax, high-income taxpayers may owe an additional 3.8% Net Investment Income Tax, also known as the Medicare surtax. This is one of the most commonly overlooked tax obligations I've encountered in my research.

The NIIT applies when your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) exceeds specific thresholds. For single filers, the threshold is $200,000. For married filing jointly, it's $250,000. For married filing separately, it's $125,000.

The 3.8% tax applies to the lesser of your net investment income or the amount by which your MAGI exceeds the threshold. Net investment income includes capital gains, dividends, interest, rental income, and other passive investment income.

Here's an example. If you're a single filer with $150,000 in wages and $80,000 in capital gains, your MAGI is $230,000. That exceeds the $200,000 threshold by $30,000. Your net investment income is $80,000. The NIIT applies to the lesser amount: $30,000. Your additional NIIT would be $30,000 times 3.8% equals $1,140.

This calculator automatically includes the NIIT when applicable, so your total tax figure reflects this additional obligation.

Tax-Efficient Strategies

I've analyzed dozens of tax-efficient investment strategies as part of our original research. Here are the most impactful approaches that individual investors can use to minimize capital gains tax legally.

Tax-Loss Harvesting

This involves deliberately selling losing positions to generate capital losses that offset your gains. If you have $30,000 in gains and $20,000 in losses from other positions, your net taxable gain is only $10,000. The key constraint is the wash-sale rule, which prevents you from buying a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale.

Discussions about tax-loss harvesting algorithms appear frequently on Hacker News, where developers share automated approaches using portfolio management tools. The npm registry also has tax calculation packages that can help model these scenarios programmatically.

Hold for Long-Term Treatment

If you're close to the one-year mark, it's often worth waiting a few extra days or weeks to qualify for long-term rates. The tax savings can be substantial. On a $100,000 gain, the difference between 24% (short-term) and 15% (long-term) is $9,000.

Use Tax-Advantaged Accounts

Investments held in 401(k)s, IRAs, Roth IRAs, and other tax-advantaged accounts aren't subject to capital gains tax when you sell within the account. This is why many advisors recommend holding high-growth (and therefore high-gain) investments in tax-advantaged accounts when possible.

Charitable Giving

Donating appreciated assets directly to a qualified charity lets you avoid capital gains tax entirely while taking a deduction for the full fair market value. If you hold stock with a $50,000 gain and donate it, you pay zero capital gains tax and get a $50,000+ charitable deduction. This strategy works best for people who were planning to donate anyway.

Specific Lot Identification

If you've purchased shares of the same stock or fund at different times and prices, you can choose which specific lots to sell. By selling your highest-cost-basis lots first, you minimize the taxable gain. Most brokerages now support specific lot identification, though you designate the lots at the time of sale, not after.

Qualified Opportunity Zones

You can defer and potentially reduce capital gains tax by reinvesting gains into a Qualified Opportunity Zone Fund within 180 days of the sale. If you hold the QOZ investment for at least 10 years, any additional gains on the QOZ investment itself are tax-free. This strategy requires careful due diligence on the specific fund and properties involved.

Special Situations

Not all capital gains follow the standard rules. I've outlined several special situations that apply to common types of investments.

Real Estate (Primary Residence)

If you sell your primary residence, you can exclude up to $250,000 in capital gains ($500,000 for married filing jointly) from taxation, provided you've owned and lived in the home for at least 2 of the past 5 years. This is one of the most generous tax exclusions in the entire tax code. You don't even reinvest the proceeds.

Collectibles

Long-term gains on collectibles (art, coins, precious metals, antiques) are taxed at a maximum rate of 28%, higher than the standard 15-20% long-term rates. Short-term gains on collectibles are still taxed at ordinary income rates. This higher rate for collectibles is something many new investors in gold or art don't anticipate.

Inherited Assets

When you inherit an asset, your cost basis is "stepped up" to the fair market value at the date of the decedent's death. This means if your parent bought stock for $10,000 and it's worth $100,000 when they pass away, your basis is $100,000. If you sell immediately, you'd owe little to no capital gains tax. This stepped-up basis is one of the most valuable estate planning provisions in the tax code.

Cryptocurrency

The IRS treats cryptocurrency as property, meaning the same capital gains rules apply. Selling, trading, or using crypto to purchase goods triggers a taxable event. The holding period and basis rules work the same as for stocks. However, crypto-to-crypto trades (like exchanging Bitcoin for Ethereum) are also taxable events, which catches many crypto traders off guard.

The tax-related discussions on Stack Overflow are surprisingly informative for developers who calculate capital gains programmatically, especially for crypto portfolios with hundreds of transactions.

Frequently Asked Questions

What counts as a capital gain?

A capital gain occurs whenever you sell a capital asset for more than your cost basis. Capital assets include stocks, bonds, mutual funds, ETFs, real estate, cryptocurrency, collectibles, and even personal-use property (though gains on personal property are taxable while losses generally aren't deductible). The gain is the difference between your sale price and your adjusted cost basis, which includes the original purchase price plus transaction costs and improvements.

How long do I hold an investment for long-term treatment?

More than one year. The holding period begins the day after you acquire the asset and includes the day you sell it. If you purchase stock on March 15, 2025, you must wait until at least March 16, 2026 to sell for long-term treatment. Selling on March 15, 2026 (exactly one year) would still be short-term. The "more than" language is precise and matters.

Can I offset capital gains with capital losses?

Yes. Capital losses offset capital gains dollar for dollar. Short-term losses first offset short-term gains, and long-term losses first offset long-term gains. Any remaining net losses can offset the other type of gain. If your total losses exceed total gains, you can deduct up to $3,000 of net capital losses against ordinary income per year ($1,500 if married filing separately). Unused losses carry forward indefinitely.

What's the wash-sale rule?

The wash-sale rule prevents you from claiming a tax loss if you buy a "substantially identical" security within 30 days before or after the sale. If you sell a stock at a loss and buy it back within this 61-day window (30 days before, the sale day, and 30 days after), the loss is disallowed. Instead, the disallowed loss is added to the cost basis of the replacement shares. The rule applies across all your accounts, including IRAs, though enforcement in IRAs is inconsistent.

Do I owe state capital gains tax too?

It depends on your state. Seven states (Alaska, Florida, Nevada, South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Wyoming) have no state income tax at all. Washington and New Hampshire don't tax earned income but do tax some investment income. The remaining states tax capital gains, usually at the same rate as ordinary income. California has the highest state capital gains rate at 13.3%. This calculator handles federal tax only, so you'll account for state tax separately.

How are crypto capital gains taxed?

Cryptocurrency is treated as property by the IRS, so the same capital gains rules apply. Selling crypto for cash, trading one crypto for another, and using crypto to purchase goods or services are all taxable events. Your gain or loss is the difference between the fair market value at the time of the transaction and your cost basis. The short-term vs. long-term distinction based on holding period applies exactly as it does for stocks.

What happens if I sell an investment at a loss?

A capital loss can be used to offset capital gains. If your total losses exceed your gains, you can deduct up to $3,000 per year against ordinary income. Any excess loss carries forward to future years. There's no limit to how many years you can carry forward losses. Some investors deliberately harvest losses late in the year to offset gains taken earlier, a strategy known as tax-loss harvesting.

Industry Standards and References for Capital Gains Planning

The Internal Revenue Service publishes comprehensive guidance on capital gains taxation through various publications and forms that serve as the authoritative reference for taxpayers and tax professionals. IRS Publication 550 covers investment income and expenses in detail, including the rules for determining holding periods, calculating cost basis, and reporting capital gains and losses. Schedule D of Form 1040 is the primary form for reporting capital gains and losses, supplemented by Form 8949 which provides the detailed transaction-by-transaction accounting. For real estate transactions, additional forms including Form 4797 for business property and Form 6252 for installment sales may apply. Tax professionals rely on these publications along with Treasury regulations, revenue rulings, and court decisions to navigate the complex landscape of capital gains taxation and develop strategies that legally minimize their clients' tax obligations.

Professional financial planning organizations including the CFP Board, AICPA, and National Association of Tax Professionals publish continuing education materials and practice standards that address capital gains planning as a core competency. The Certified Financial Planner certification examination includes extensive coverage of tax-efficient investment strategies, timing of gain recognition, charitable giving techniques, and estate planning considerations related to capital gains. Tax-loss harvesting, asset location optimization across taxable and tax-advantaged accounts, and step-up in basis planning at death are among the advanced strategies that qualified professionals employ. For individuals with complex investment portfolios or significant unrealized gains, working with a qualified tax professional or financial planner who specializes in tax planning can produce savings that far exceed the cost of professional advice.

Common Mistakes to Avoid in Capital Gains Tax Planning

Failing to account for the wash sale rule is one of the most common and costly mistakes in tax-loss harvesting strategies. The wash sale rule disallows a loss deduction if you purchase a substantially identical security within 30 days before or after selling at a loss. This means that selling a stock to harvest a loss and immediately repurchasing the same stock, or purchasing a substantially similar ETF that tracks the same index, may trigger the wash sale rule and negate the tax benefit entirely. The disallowed loss is added to the cost basis of the replacement purchase, deferring rather than eliminating the tax benefit, but the planning value of controlling the timing of loss recognition is lost. Investors must plan substitute positions carefully, choosing securities that provide similar market exposure without being substantially identical. The IRS has not provided a precise definition of substantially identical for all situations, creating a gray area that tax professionals navigate through conservative interpretation and established precedent.

Overlooking state capital gains taxes is another frequent planning gap that can significantly affect after-tax returns. While federal long-term capital gains rates top out at 23.8 percent including the net investment income tax, many states tax capital gains as ordinary income at rates up to 13.3 percent in California. Some states, like Washington, have recently enacted capital gains taxes that affect high-income residents. Other states, including Texas, Florida, and Nevada, have no state income tax and therefore no state capital gains tax. The combined federal and state tax rate on long-term gains can range from 15 percent in no-tax states for moderate-income taxpayers to over 37 percent in high-tax states for high-income earners. This dramatic variation makes state tax considerations an essential component of capital gains planning, particularly for individuals considering relocation, those with flexibility in the timing of gain recognition, and those evaluating the after-tax attractiveness of different investment strategies.

Estate Planning and Capital Gains Considerations

The step-up in basis at death is one of the most significant tax planning opportunities related to capital gains. When a person passes away and bequeaths appreciated assets to heirs, the cost basis of those assets is stepped up to the fair market value at the date of death, effectively eliminating all accumulated capital gains. An investor who purchased stock for 10,000 dollars that has grown to 500,000 dollars would face capital gains tax on 490,000 dollars of gain if sold during their lifetime. If held until death, the heir receives the stock with a basis of 500,000 dollars and can sell it immediately with no capital gains tax. This powerful planning tool incentivizes holding appreciated assets rather than selling them, and it significantly impacts estate planning strategies. Proposals to eliminate or limit the step-up in basis have been debated by lawmakers but have not been enacted as of the current writing, though the possibility of future changes underscores the importance of staying current with tax law developments.
