Understanding Texas Taxes

This video explains how Texas funds state services without an income tax and what it means for your paycheck.

The to Texas Take-Home Pay

Texas stands out among American states for one simple reason: there is no state income tax. Zero. Not a penny of your paycheck goes to Austin for income tax purposes. This isn't a temporary policy or a recent change. The Texas Constitution explicitly prohibits a personal income tax unless voters approve it in a statewide referendum, and in 2019, voters strengthened that prohibition by passing Proposition 4, which requires a two-thirds supermajority in both chambers of the legislature before such a referendum could even be placed on the ballot.

I this Texas salary calculator to show exactly what that means for your paycheck. When I compare a $75,000 salary in Texas to the same salary in California, Georgia, or New York, the difference is stark. A Texan keeps an extra $3,000-$6,000 per year compared to workers in states with moderate income taxes, and up to $8,000-$10,000 more than workers in high-tax states. That's real money, and it compounds over a career.

What Texas Workers Actually Pay

Just because Texas has no state income tax doesn't mean your paycheck arrives untouched. Federal taxes still apply, and they represent the bulk of what comes out of a Texas paycheck. Here's the breakdown:

This is your largest deduction. The 2026 federal brackets for single filers range from 10% on the first $11,925 of taxable income to 37% on income over $626,350. For a typical Texas worker earning $75,000, federal income tax is approximately $8,600-$9,800 depending on filing status and W-4 elections. The standard deduction for 2026 is $15,700 for single filers and $31,400 for married filing jointly, which means the first $15,700 (or $31,400) of your income isn't subject to federal tax at all.

Social Security (FICA): 6.2% of your gross pay up to the 2026 wage base of $176,100. On a $75,000 salary, that's $4,650. This is a flat tax with no deductions or exemptions. Your employer pays a matching 6.2%. Once you earn above $176,100, Social Security tax stops, which is why higher earners have a lower effective FICA rate.

Medicare: 1.45% of all gross pay with no income cap. On $75,000, that's $1,087.50. An additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies to earnings over $200,000 for single filers or $250,000 for married filing jointly. Your employer matches the base 1.45% but not the surtax.

The Tradeoff Property Taxes and Sales Taxes

Texas has to fund government services somehow, and the primary mechanisms are property taxes and sales taxes. It's important to understand these because they affect your overall financial picture, even though they don't show up on your pay stub.

Texas has some of the highest property taxes in the nation. The average effective rate is approximately 1.60%, compared to the national average of about 0.99%. On a median-valued Texas home ($300,000), that's about $4,800 per year. In some counties, rates exceed 2.0%. Compare this to Florida (0.80%) or Wyoming (0.55%), both of which also have no income tax. If you're a homeowner, property taxes partially offset the income tax savings. If you rent, you're indirectly paying through higher rent prices, though the impact is diluted. I've found that for most workers earning under $100,000, the income tax savings still outweigh the higher property taxes, but it's not as clear-cut as the headlines suggest.

Texas has a 6.25% state sales tax, and most localities add 1-2%, bringing the combined rate to 8.00-8.25% in most areas. This is higher than the national average but comparable to other no-income-tax states like Washington (9.29%) and Tennessee (9.55%). Since sales tax is consumption-based, higher earners who save more of their income pay a lower effective sales tax rate, making Texas's overall tax structure somewhat regressive.

Pre-Tax Deductions in Texas

Pre-tax deductions reduce your federal taxable income, lowering your federal tax bill. In Texas, you don't get the "double benefit" that workers in income-tax states enjoy (where pre-tax deductions reduce both federal and state taxes). But the federal savings alone are substantial.

401(k) Up to $23,500 in 2026 ($31,000 if age 50+). At a 22% marginal federal rate, every $1,000 contributed saves $220 in federal taxes. If your employer matches, the effective return is even higher. I've consistently found that maxing out employer-matched 401(k) contributions is the single best financial move for most Texas workers.

$4,300 for self-only coverage, $8,550 for family coverage in 2026. HSAs offer triple tax benefits: contributions are pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and qualified medical withdrawals are tax-free. Texas doesn't tax HSA contributions at the state level (because Texas doesn't tax income at all), so there's no additional state benefit, but the federal benefit is the same as anywhere.

Health Employer-sponsored health insurance premiums are typically deducted pre-tax through a Section 125 cafeteria plan. This reduces both your income tax and your FICA obligation. On a $200/month premium, you save approximately $528 annually in federal income tax (at 22%) and $183 in FICA taxes.

Who Benefits Most From Texas's No-Tax Policy?

The income tax exemption benefits all wage earners, but the magnitude varies by income level. At $40,000, a Texas worker saves roughly $1,500-$2,000 compared to moderate-tax states. At $150,000, the savings jump to $7,000-$12,000. At $300,000, a Texas worker can save $15,000-$25,000 annually compared to high-tax states like California or New Jersey.

This progressive benefit structure is why Texas attracts high-income professionals and why companies relocate here. I've tracked over 20 major corporate relocations to Texas since 2020 (Tesla, Oracle, Caterpillar, Charles Schwab, among others), and the tax environment is consistently cited as a primary factor. For remote workers who can choose their state of residence, Texas is increasingly competitive, especially for tech workers earning $150,000 or more.

The flip side is that Texas's reliance on sales and property taxes makes the overall tax structure regressive: lower-income residents pay a higher percentage of their income in total state and local taxes. According to the Institute on Taxation and Economic Policy, the bottom 20% of Texas earners pay approximately 13% of their income in state and local taxes, while the top 1% pay about 3.1%. This is a legitimate concern, though it doesn't change the payroll calculation that this tool computes.

Marginal vs. Effective Federal Tax Rate

A common misconception is that if you're "in the 22% bracket," you pay 22% on all your income. That's not how marginal brackets work. Your effective federal rate is always lower than your marginal rate because the first portion of your income is taxed at 10%, the next portion at 12%, and so on.

For a single Texas filer earning $75,000 with the standard deduction, the taxable income is $59,300. The federal tax computation is: 10% on the first $11,925 ($1,192.50) + 12% on the next $36,550 ($4,386.00) + 22% on the remaining $10,825 ($2,381.50) = $7,960. That's an effective federal rate of about 10.6%, far lower than the 22% marginal bracket. This is exactly why I the bracket detail table into this calculator. It shows exactly how much income falls in each bracket, so you can see your true federal tax burden. Most paycheck estimators don't provide this level of transparency, and it won't surprise you to learn that I've seen numerous online calculators get this wrong.

Frequently Asked Questions

Does Texas have state income tax?
No, Texas has no state income tax on wages, salaries, or any form of personal income. This is enshrined in the Texas Constitution and was further protected by Proposition 4 in 2019, which requires a two-thirds legislative supermajority to even propose an income tax referendum. Texas is one of 9 states with no income tax on wages.
How much will I take home from a $75,000 salary in Texas?
On a $75,000 annual salary as a single filer with standard deduction and no pre-tax contributions, you'll take home approximately $59,500-$61,000. Your deductions are federal income tax (~$8,000-$9,800), Social Security ($4,650), and Medicare ($1,088). With no state tax, your effective total tax rate is approximately 19-21%.
What taxes does a Texas employee pay?
Texas employees pay federal income tax (10%-37% marginal brackets), Social Security (6.2% up to $176,100 in 2026), and Medicare (1.45% plus 0.9% surtax on earnings over $200,000/$250,000). There is no state income tax, no local income tax, and no city wage tax. Property taxes and sales taxes are paid separately and do not affect your paycheck.
How does Texas compare to Florida for take-home pay?
Take-home pay on wages is identical in Texas and Florida, since neither state has income tax. The differences lie elsewhere: Texas has much higher property taxes (1.60% vs 0.80%) but slightly lower insurance costs. Florida has higher homeowner's insurance and slightly higher cost of living in major metros. For renters and moderate earners, the take-home pay difference is negligible.
Why are Texas property taxes so high?
Texas funds public services (schools, roads, public safety) primarily through property taxes and sales taxes instead of income taxes. The average effective property tax rate is about 1.60%, compared to the 0.99% national average. This is the primary trade-off for having no income tax. For homeowners with expensive properties, the property tax bill can partially offset or even exceed the income tax savings.
Do pre-tax 401(k) contributions save less in Texas?
Pre-tax 401(k) contributions provide the same federal tax savings in Texas as in any state., in states with income tax, 401(k) contributions reduce state tax. In Texas, there's no state tax to reduce, so you miss the "double benefit." Despite this, 401(k) contributions are still highly valuable. At a 22% federal marginal rate, every $1,000 contributed saves $220 in federal taxes.
What is the 2026 Social Security wage base?
The 2026 Social Security wage base is $176,100. You pay 6.2% Social Security tax on earnings up to this limit, after which Social Security tax stops. For a Texas worker earning exactly $176,100, the maximum Social Security tax is $10,918.20. Earnings above this amount are only subject to Medicare tax (1.45% base + 0.9% surtax above $200,000/$250,000).
