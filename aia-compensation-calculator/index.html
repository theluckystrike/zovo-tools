AIA Compensation Survey Overview

The American Institute of Architects publishes a comprehensive compensation survey every two years, collecting data from architecture firms of all sizes across the United States. This survey has been the primary benchmark for architecture compensation since its inception and is widely used by firms, professionals, and human resources departments to set and negotiate salaries.

The survey covers more than 20 distinct position categories ranging from entry-level interns to firm principals and managing partners. It collects data on base salary, bonuses, overtime compensation, benefits packages, and total compensation value. Results are segmented by firm size, geographic region, years of experience, and licensure status, providing a detailed picture of how different factors interact to determine compensation in the architecture profession.

Participation in the AIA Compensation Survey is voluntary, and firms of all sizes contribute data. The most recent surveys have drawn participation from hundreds of firms representing thousands of individual employees. While the full survey report is available for purchase through the AIA, the general trends and benchmarks it reveals are widely discussed in industry publications and form the basis for compensation planning across the profession.

I have used AIA survey data extensively when advising architecture professionals on salary negotiation and career planning. The survey is the most reliable single source for architecture compensation data in the United States because of its broad participation, consistent methodology, and detailed segmentation. No other source matches its depth of coverage for the architecture profession specifically.

This calculator synthesizes publicly available compensation data from multiple industry sources to provide salary estimates that align with the general findings of the AIA survey and other architecture compensation research. The figures should be used as reference points for career planning and salary negotiations, not as definitive predictions of specific job offers.

Intern and Architecture Graduate

Entry-level positions in architecture typically pay between $45,000 and $55,000 per year. At this stage, professionals are completing their Architectural Experience Program (AXP) hours and preparing for the ARE exams. Compensation at this level varies less by region than at higher levels because market forces create a relatively narrow band. Firms need to offer enough to attract graduates from competitive programs but face budget constraints on positions that require significant mentoring and supervision overhead.

The intern phase is a period of investment for both the professional and the firm. The professional gains practical experience and mentorship that cannot be obtained through education alone. The firm invests in training with the expectation that the professional will eventually contribute at a level that justifies significantly higher compensation. Professionals who approach this phase with patience and a focus on learning tend to build the strongest foundations for long-term career growth.

Architect I and Architect II

As professionals progress through the early and mid-career stages, compensation increases with demonstrated competency and expanding responsibility. Architect I positions, typically requiring 2 to 5 years of experience, pay in the $55,000 to $70,000 range. Architect II positions, requiring 5 to 8 years, command $65,000 to $85,000. The transition between these levels often coincides with increasing project responsibility, direct client interaction, and growing technical leadership within a project team.

The Architect II level is particularly significant because it represents the stage at which most professionals complete their licensure. The combination of achieving licensure and demonstrating the ability to manage significant portions of projects independently positions professionals for the substantial salary jump that comes with the Senior Architect title.

Senior Architect and Project Manager

Senior-level positions represent a significant compensation jump. Senior Architects typically earn $80,000 to $110,000, while Project Managers can earn $95,000 to $130,000. At these levels, professionals are expected to manage projects independently, mentor junior staff, develop and maintain client relationships, and contribute to business development efforts. The difference between a Senior Architect and a Project Manager often relates to the balance between technical design leadership and project management responsibility, though many positions blend both functions.

Principal and Partner

Principals and partners at architecture firms earn $120,000 to $180,000 in base salary, with total compensation potentially exceeding these figures substantially through profit sharing, performance bonuses, and equity distributions. At this level, compensation is closely tied to firm performance and the individual's contribution to revenue generation. Principals at successful firms in high-cost markets can earn well above $200,000 in total compensation, while those at smaller firms or in lower-cost regions may be closer to the lower end of the range. The variance at this level is the highest of any career stage.

How Experience Affects Pay

Years of experience is one of the strongest predictors of architecture compensation. The relationship between experience and pay is not perfectly linear. Instead, it follows a curve with steeper increases during certain career phases and more gradual growth during others. Understanding this curve helps professionals set realistic expectations at each stage.

The Early Career Phase: 0 to 5 Years

The first five years of an architecture career typically see steady but moderate salary growth. Annual increases of 3% to 5% are common during this phase, with larger jumps when transitioning from intern to Architect I. The focus during this period is on building technical skills, completing licensure requirements, and establishing a professional reputation within the firm and the local architecture community. Compensation growth is somewhat constrained because professionals at this level are still developing the independent judgment and technical depth that justify higher pay.

I encourage professionals in this phase to focus on learning breadth. Exposure to different project types, phases of design, construction administration, and client communication builds a versatile skill set that pays dividends at higher career levels. The salary difference between a narrowly skilled and a broadly skilled professional becomes increasingly pronounced after the five-year mark.

The Growth Phase: 5 to 15 Years

The most significant salary growth typically occurs between 5 and 15 years of experience. This is when professionals transition from purely technical roles into leadership positions with meaningful impact on project outcomes and firm revenue. Each step up the career ladder, from Architect II to Senior Architect to Project Manager, brings a meaningful salary increase. Professionals who achieve licensure during this phase see an additional 15% boost. The combination of role advancement, licensure, and growing expertise can result in salary doubling over this ten-year period.

This phase rewards professionals who develop management and communication skills alongside their technical abilities. The architecture professionals who advance most quickly through this phase are those who can manage client relationships, lead project teams, and contribute to winning new work. Pure technical skill is necessary but not sufficient for reaching the highest compensation levels.

The Senior Phase: 15 or More Years

After 15 years, salary growth tends to level off unless the professional moves into firm leadership or ownership. Senior Architects and Project Managers who remain in their roles may see more modest annual increases of 2% to 4%. However, those who advance to Principal or Partner positions unlock a new compensation tier that includes profit sharing and equity participation. The variance in compensation at this level is enormous, ranging from $100,000 at smaller firms to over $300,000 at large, successful practices in major markets.

The decision about whether to pursue firm leadership versus remaining in a senior technical role is deeply personal. Some architects find their greatest satisfaction in design work and are willing to accept a compensation ceiling in exchange for staying close to the creative process. Others are energized by the business and leadership dimensions of practice and pursue the higher compensation that comes with principal-level responsibility.

West Region

The West region, anchored by California, consistently offers the highest architecture salaries in the country with a multiplier of approximately 1.20x the national average. San Francisco and Los Angeles are particularly strong markets driven by a robust technology sector, significant residential development, seismic retrofit requirements, and major institutional building programs. However, the cost of living in these cities is among the highest in the nation, which partially offsets the salary advantage when measuring real purchasing power.

Denver has emerged as a growing market within the West region, offering salaries above the national average with a cost of living lower than the California markets. The influx of technology companies and population growth has created sustained demand for architectural services across residential, commercial, and mixed-use project types.

Northeast Region

The Northeast, led by New York City and Boston, offers salaries approximately 15% above the national average. New York is one of the world's premier architecture markets with unmatched opportunities for large-scale commercial, residential, and cultural projects. The density of world-renowned firms creates a competitive talent market that pushes salaries upward. Boston's strong higher education and healthcare sectors drive demand for specialized architectural services in those building types.

Philadelphia offers a middle ground within the Northeast, with salaries above the national average but below New York and Boston levels. The city's historic preservation requirements and growing development market create steady demand for architects while maintaining a lower cost of living than its northern neighbors.

Midwest and Southeast

The Midwest and Southeast regions offer salaries below the national average, at approximately 0.95x and 0.92x respectively. However, these regions often provide the best salary-to-cost-of-living ratio in the country. A Senior Architect earning $90,000 in Atlanta, Minneapolis, or Raleigh has significantly more purchasing power than one earning $115,000 in San Francisco or New York. These regions also tend to have strong quality-of-life metrics including shorter commutes, more affordable housing, and access to outdoor recreation that attract professionals willing to accept nominally lower salaries.

Chicago is an important exception within the Midwest. As a global city with a world-renowned architectural heritage, Chicago offers compensation closer to Northeast levels while maintaining a moderate cost of living. The city's position as a hub for large architecture and engineering firms means that Midwest-based professionals willing to work in Chicago can access national-level compensation.

Southwest and Northwest

The Southwest region, including Texas and Arizona, sits close to the national average with a 0.98x multiplier. Rapid population growth in markets like Austin, Dallas, and Phoenix has increased demand for architects and pushed salaries upward in recent years. These markets combine reasonable cost of living with a growing construction pipeline, making them attractive destinations for architects seeking a favorable balance of compensation and quality of life.

The Northwest region, led by Seattle and Portland, carries a 1.10x multiplier. Seattle in particular has seen salary growth driven by the technology sector's demand for office, campus, and data center projects. The Pacific Northwest's focus on sustainable design has also created premium demand for architects with green building expertise.

Firm Size and Compensation

The size of the architecture firm significantly influences both base salary and total compensation. Each firm size category has distinct characteristics that affect what they can and will pay employees, and the trade-offs extend well beyond the base salary number.

Small Firms: Under 50 Employees

Small firms typically offer base salaries at or slightly below the national average. The baseline multiplier is 1.00x, meaning no premium or discount compared to the national median. However, the compensation picture at small firms is more nuanced than the base salary alone suggests. Employees at small firms often have broader roles, gaining exposure to all phases of project delivery from schematic design through construction administration rather than specializing in one phase. This breadth of experience can accelerate career development and make professionals more versatile.

Small firms may also offer compensation elements that do not appear in salary surveys. Partnership tracks, equity participation, flexible schedules, a more collegial work environment, and the ability to shape the firm's direction are all forms of non-monetary compensation that many professionals value highly. However, benefits packages at small firms tend to be less comprehensive than at large firms, with higher employee contributions for health insurance and lower retirement matching rates.

Medium Firms: 50 to 200 Employees

Medium-sized firms represent a balance between compensation and work environment. They typically offer a salary multiplier of approximately 1.05x, placing them above the national average while not matching the premiums available at large firms. Benefits packages are generally solid, with competitive health insurance and retirement plans. Career advancement paths are clearer than at small firms, and mentoring opportunities are generally strong because the firm is large enough to have established structure but small enough that senior leaders remain accessible.

Medium firms often have the advantage of working on projects large enough to be professionally challenging while maintaining a culture where individual contributions are visible and recognized. Many architects find that medium firms offer the best combination of compensation, professional growth, and workplace satisfaction.

Large Firms: 200 or More Employees

Large firms offer the highest base salaries, approximately 10% above the national average with a 1.10x multiplier. They also provide the most comprehensive benefits packages, including robust health insurance plans, generous retirement matching (often 5% to 6%), extensive training programs, conference and continuing education budgets, and structured career advancement frameworks. Large firms can offer these premiums because they have the revenue, administrative infrastructure, and bargaining power to provide competitive total compensation packages.

The trade-off at large firms is often specialization and autonomy. Roles tend to be more narrowly defined, and individual impact on projects may feel diminished within a large team. The corporate culture may not suit everyone, particularly professionals who value creative independence or prefer a less hierarchical environment. The decision between higher pay at a large firm and greater autonomy at a smaller firm is a personal calculation that depends on individual priorities and career stage.

The Licensure Premium

Architectural licensure, obtained by completing the Architect Registration Examination (ARE) and meeting state experience requirements, carries a meaningful salary premium of approximately 15%. This premium is one of the most consistent findings in architecture compensation research and applies across all regions, firm sizes, and career levels.

The licensure premium exists for several interconnected reasons. Licensed architects can legally stamp drawings and take professional responsibility for building designs, which is a function that unlicensed professionals cannot perform regardless of their skill level or experience. This gatekeeping function creates a supply constraint that supports higher wages for licensed professionals. Firms need a certain ratio of licensed architects to unlicensed staff in order to operate, and the limited supply of licensed professionals gives them negotiating leverage.

Licensure also signals a level of competence and professional commitment that employers value beyond the practical necessity. Passing the ARE requires mastering a broad range of knowledge spanning structures, building systems, project management, practice management, site design, and programming. The examination process typically takes two to four years of dedicated study on top of the experience requirement. Employers view this investment as evidence of professional dedication, self-discipline, and comprehensive knowledge.

The path to licensure typically includes a professional degree in architecture (5 to 7 years of education), completion of the Architectural Experience Program (AXP) with approximately 3,740 hours of documented experience across multiple practice areas, and passing all six divisions of the Architect Registration Examination (ARE 5.0). Most professionals complete the process 8 to 12 years after entering architecture school.

The financial impact of licensure timing is significant when calculated over a full career. A professional who becomes licensed at age 28 rather than 35 captures seven additional years of the 15% premium. On a base salary of $80,000, that premium is approximately $12,000 per year, meaning the seven-year head start is worth roughly $84,000 in additional lifetime earnings before accounting for compounding raises. I encourage professionals to begin the ARE as early as eligible and to treat licensure completion as a high-priority career objective.

Healthcare architecture commands the highest specialization premium at approximately 8% above baseline. This premium reflects the extraordinary complexity of designing healthcare facilities, which must comply with stringent codes including FGI Guidelines, ADA requirements, infection control protocols, and medical equipment integration standards. Architects who develop deep expertise in healthcare facility planning are in consistent demand as the aging population drives hospital expansion and renovation projects.

Sustainability and green building expertise has grown steadily in value as building codes increasingly mandate energy performance standards and as clients demand environmentally responsible designs. Architects with LEED accreditation, Passive House certification, or demonstrated experience with net-zero energy buildings command a premium of approximately 6%. Architects specializing in luxury custom homes, particularly in markets like the Hamptons, Aspen, or the California coast, can command fees and salaries that far exceed the general residential average. The aggregate data masks significant variation within this specialization.

Benefits Beyond Base Salary

Base salary is only one component of total compensation in architecture. Benefits can add 20% to 40% to the total value of a compensation package, and their composition varies significantly by firm size, region, and firm culture. Evaluating a job offer or current position requires understanding the full compensation picture.

Health and Insurance

Most architecture firms offer health insurance, with employer contributions covering 50% to 80% of premiums depending on firm size. Large firms typically cover a higher percentage and offer more plan options. Dental and vision insurance are common additional benefits. Some firms provide life insurance (typically 1x to 2x annual salary) and both short-term and long-term disability coverage. The value of health benefits alone can represent $8,000 to $20,000 per year depending on coverage level and family status.

Retirement Benefits

401(k) plans are standard at most mid-sized and large architecture firms, with employer matching contributions typically ranging from 3% to 6% of salary. Some firms offer profit-sharing contributions in addition to matching, which can add another 2% to 5% in strong years. At the principal level, retirement benefits may include deferred compensation plans or equity in the firm itself. A 5% employer match on a $90,000 salary is worth $4,500 per year in free money, making retirement benefits a significant component of total compensation.

Professional Development

Many firms invest in employee development by covering continuing education courses, conference attendance fees, professional organization memberships including AIA dues, and ARE exam preparation and registration costs. These benefits can represent $2,000 to $8,000 per year in value. Firms that cover ARE exam costs remove a significant financial barrier to licensure, effectively accelerating the timeline to the 15% licensure premium. When evaluating offers, the professional development budget is worth careful attention.

Time Off and Flexibility

Paid time off in architecture typically starts at 10 to 15 days per year for entry-level positions and increases with tenure to 20 to 25 days for senior professionals. Some firms have adopted unlimited PTO policies, though utilization rates under such policies vary widely. Flexible work arrangements including remote work options and compressed schedules have become increasingly common. The economic value of an additional week of PTO or a hybrid work arrangement that eliminates commuting costs can be substantial, though it does not appear on a pay stub.

Architecture Salary Trends

Architecture compensation has evolved significantly over the past decade, influenced by economic cycles, labor market dynamics, shifting industry priorities, and demographic changes within the profession.

The post-2020 period saw increased demand for architects driven by a construction boom across residential, healthcare, technology, and mixed-use sectors. This demand, combined with a constrained supply of licensed architects as many baby-boomer practitioners have retired, has pushed salaries upward at a rate exceeding general inflation. The Bureau of Labor Statistics projects continued growth in architect employment through 2032, suggesting that the favorable market conditions for architecture professionals are not a temporary anomaly.

Entry-level salaries have been particularly affected by supply constraints. With fewer architecture graduates entering the profession relative to demand, firms have had to increase starting salaries to attract talent. Entry-level compensation has risen approximately 15% to 20% over the past five years in many markets, outpacing the growth at more senior levels where the supply is less constrained.

Specialization has become an increasingly important salary differentiator. Architects with expertise in healthcare facility design, sustainable building, data center architecture, or building information modeling (BIM) management command premium compensation compared to generalists. This trend reflects the growing complexity of building design, the proliferation of specialized codes and standards, and the technical demands of performance-driven design approaches.

Remote and hybrid work arrangements have also influenced compensation dynamics. Some firms have adopted location-adjusted pay scales that reduce salary when an employee moves from a high-cost to a low-cost area. Others maintain uniform national rates regardless of employee location. Architects who can work remotely for firms in high-cost markets while living in lower-cost areas may achieve the most favorable salary-to-cost-of-living ratios in the profession's history. However, remote work in architecture has practical limitations because site visits, client meetings, physical model building, and collaborative design sessions often require in-person presence.

The growing interest in mass timber construction, modular building, and adaptive reuse has created new specialization niches. Architects who develop expertise in these emerging areas are positioning themselves for premium compensation as these building methods become more mainstream. The intersection of technology and construction, including topics like digital fabrication and robotic construction, represents another frontier where specialized knowledge commands above-average pay.

Negotiation Strategies for Architects

Effective salary negotiation requires preparation, market knowledge, and a clear understanding of your value proposition. These strategies apply whether you are negotiating an initial offer, requesting a raise, or evaluating a competing opportunity.

Research Your Market Value Thoroughly

Before any negotiation, research compensation data for your specific combination of role, experience level, region, firm size, and licensure status. Tools like this calculator, the AIA Compensation Survey, Glassdoor, and salary data from professional networks provide useful data points. I recommend gathering data from at least three independent sources before forming a target number. When you can cite multiple data sources showing that your request is within market range, you transform the conversation from a subjective negotiation into a discussion grounded in objective evidence.

Quantify Your Contributions

Prepare specific examples of how you have contributed to your firm's success. This might include projects you managed that met or exceeded profitability targets, clients you helped acquire or retain, process improvements you implemented that saved time or reduced errors, mentoring that improved team productivity, or business development efforts that expanded the firm's portfolio. Tangible examples of value creation are more persuasive than general statements about dedication or hard work.

Consider Total Compensation

If the base salary offer is below your target, explore other compensation components. Signing bonuses, performance bonuses tied to project outcomes, additional PTO, flexible work arrangements, professional development budgets, accelerated review timelines, and equity participation all have real economic value. A slightly lower base salary combined with a four-day work week, fully funded continuing education, or a meaningful annual bonus target may represent better total compensation than a higher base salary alone.

Use Competing Offers Strategically

A competing offer is one of the strongest negotiating tools available. If you have received an offer from another firm, sharing this information with your current employer (or with a firm that has extended an offer you are negotiating) establishes your market value through concrete evidence. Be honest about the competing offer. Fabricating or inflating a competing offer is unprofessional and creates risk if the other party verifies the claim through their professional network.

Time Your Negotiations

The best time to negotiate is when you have maximum leverage. This typically occurs when receiving a new job offer, after completing a major project successfully, when you have recently achieved licensure, when you receive a competing offer, or during annual review cycles. Avoid initiating salary discussions during firm downturns, immediately after project losses, or when the firm is experiencing layoffs. Context matters, and timing your request when the firm is in a position to say yes increases the probability of a favorable outcome.

Career Path and Advancement

Understanding the typical career trajectory in architecture helps professionals make strategic decisions about education, licensure timing, specialization, and career moves. The architecture profession offers multiple paths, each with distinct compensation profiles and professional satisfactions.

The traditional career path progresses from intern to Architect I/II to Senior Architect to Project Manager to Principal/Partner. Each transition involves not just increased technical capability but also new skills in management, client relations, business development, and leadership. The professionals who advance most quickly tend to be those who actively seek responsibility beyond their current role, build strong client relationships, invest in licensure and continuing education, and contribute positively to firm culture.

The transition from Senior Architect or Project Manager to Principal is the most consequential career decision in architecture. It involves shifting from an employee mindset to an owner mindset, taking on financial risk, investing personal capital in the firm, and accepting responsibility for the livelihoods of all employees. The compensation upside is substantial, but so is the commitment. Many excellent architects choose to remain in senior technical or project management roles, finding satisfaction in the work itself without the burdens of firm ownership.

Alternative career paths include specializing in a technical area like building performance simulation or computational design, transitioning into real estate development where architectural knowledge provides a competitive advantage, moving into academia, working for public agencies that oversee major building programs, or starting an independent practice. Each path has different compensation profiles. Independent practitioners have the highest income ceiling but also the most financial risk, while academics typically earn less in base salary but enjoy schedule flexibility, research opportunities, and the satisfaction of teaching.

The timing of licensure is a strategic decision that affects both compensation and career options. Obtaining licensure as early as possible maximizes the lifetime value of the 15% premium and opens doors to roles that require a license. Every year of delay represents roughly $8,000 to $15,000 in foregone premium income depending on base salary. I advise treating the ARE as a professional priority rather than something to get around to eventually.

Geographic moves can be powerful career accelerators. Moving from a low-cost market to a high-cost market typically brings a 15% to 25% salary increase, while moving from a high-cost market to a low-cost market often allows professionals to maintain much of their salary while dramatically reducing living expenses. Strategic geographic moves at key career transitions can optimize lifetime earning potential and quality of life.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the AIA Compensation Survey?
The AIA Compensation Survey is a biennial report published by the American Institute of Architects that collects salary and benefits data from architecture firms across the United States. It provides benchmarks for compensation at different career levels, firm sizes, and geographic regions, making it the most widely referenced source for architecture salary data. The full report is available for purchase from the AIA, and its findings are frequently cited in industry publications and used for firm-level compensation planning.
How much do entry-level architects make?
Entry-level architects at the Intern or Architect I level typically earn between $45,000 and $70,000 per year depending on location, firm size, and the local job market. Positions in high-cost regions like the West Coast or Northeast tend toward the higher end of this range. Large firms in major markets may start interns at $55,000 or higher, while small firms in lower-cost areas may start closer to $45,000.
Does architecture licensure significantly affect salary?
Yes. Licensed architects typically earn approximately 15% more than unlicensed professionals at the same experience level. Licensure demonstrates competency through passing the ARE, allows the architect to stamp drawings and take professional responsibility for designs, and opens access to project leadership roles that carry higher compensation. The premium is consistent across all regions and firm sizes.
Do large firms pay more than small firms?
Generally yes. Large firms with 200 or more employees tend to offer approximately 10% higher base salaries than small firms, along with more comprehensive benefits packages including better health insurance, higher retirement matching, and larger professional development budgets. However, small firms may offer equity participation, more flexible schedules, broader project exposure, and faster advancement that provide their own forms of value.
Which region pays architects the most?
The West region including California typically offers the highest salaries with a multiplier of approximately 1.20x the national average, followed by the Northeast at 1.15x and the Northwest at 1.10x. However, these regions also have the highest costs of living. When adjusted for purchasing power, the salary differences between regions become less pronounced, and Midwest and Southeast markets often provide the best real value.
How does experience affect architect compensation?
Experience has a substantial impact on architect compensation. Salaries generally increase steadily through the first 15 to 20 years, with the largest jumps occurring at role transitions such as Architect II to Senior Architect or Senior to Project Manager. The growth phase between 5 and 15 years of experience is typically the steepest, with salary potentially doubling during this period. Principal-level compensation can reach two to four times entry-level pay.
What benefits do architecture firms typically offer?
Common benefits include health insurance with 50% to 80% employer premium contribution, dental and vision coverage, 401(k) plans with 3% to 6% employer match, paid time off starting at 10 to 15 days, professional development funding, ARE exam reimbursement, AIA membership dues, and continuing education support. Larger firms generally offer the most comprehensive packages. Total benefits value can add 20% to 40% to the base salary figure.
How often should I expect a raise in architecture?
Most architecture firms conduct annual salary reviews. Typical annual raises range from 3% to 5% for standard performance, with higher increases of 8% to 15% for promotions or exceptional contributions. Market conditions, firm profitability, and individual contributions all influence raise amounts. The largest salary jumps typically come from changing firms, being promoted to a new role level, or achieving licensure.
Is architecture a well-paying career compared to other professions?
Architecture salaries are moderate compared to other professions requiring similar education investment. While principal-level architects can earn $120,000 to $200,000 or more, the extensive education requirement of five or more years, the licensure process spanning six exam divisions, and the required experience period mean that early-career earnings often lag behind peers in engineering, medicine, law, or technology. The profession offers other rewards including creative fulfillment, lasting impact on communities, and the satisfaction of shaping the built environment.
How accurate is this compensation calculator?
This calculator provides estimated salary ranges based on publicly available compensation data and industry benchmarks aligned with AIA survey methodology. It accounts for role, experience, region, firm size, licensure, and specialization. Actual salaries vary based on many additional factors including portfolio quality, negotiation skills, firm-specific culture and budget, local market conditions, and individual performance history. Use these figures as a well-informed starting point for salary research and negotiation preparation.
