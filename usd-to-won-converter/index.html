How to Convert USD to Korean Won

Converting US Dollars to South Korean Won is straightforward multiplication. Take the Dollar amount and multiply by the current exchange rate. With a rate of approximately 1,380 KRW per 1 USD:

  • Formula (USD to KRW): Amount in USD x 1,380 = Amount in KRW
  • Formula (KRW to USD): Amount in KRW / 1,380 = Amount in USD

For example, if you want to know how much 500 US Dollars is in Korean Won, multiply 500 by 1,380. The answer is 690,000 KRW. Going the other direction, if something costs 50,000 Won, divide by 1,380 to get approximately $36.23 USD.

Quick mental math trick

For rough mental conversions, you can use the shortcut of dividing Won prices by 1,400 (close enough to the actual rate). So a 14,000 Won meal is about $10, a 70,000 Won hotel is about $50, and a 1,400,000 Won item is about $1,000. This works well enough for quick price comparisons while shopping or dining.

The 1997 Asian Financial Crisis

The most dramatic event in the Won's history was the 1997 Asian Financial Crisis. The Won plunged from about 950 per Dollar to over 1,700 in a matter of weeks. South Korea was forced to accept a $58 billion IMF bailout package, the largest at that time. The crisis led to major structural reforms in South Korea's financial system, corporate governance, and economic policies. Koreans refer to this period as the "IMF Crisis" and it remains a defining economic memory for the country.

The 2008 Global Financial Crisis

During the 2008 global financial crisis, the Won again weakened significantly, moving from about 1,000 to nearly 1,500 per Dollar. However, South Korea's response was much faster than in 1997. The central bank used its foreign exchange reserves effectively, and the currency recovered more quickly. This experience reinforced the Bank of Korea's commitment to maintaining large foreign exchange reserves.

Recent movements (2022-2026)

The Won weakened from about 1,200 to over 1,400 between 2022 and 2026, primarily driven by US Federal Reserve interest rate hikes that made the Dollar stronger against most currencies. South Korea's interest rates stayed below US levels, creating a rate differential that favored the Dollar. As of 2026, the Won has found relative stability in the 1,350-1,400 range.

Economic Factors Affecting the Won

Several key factors drive the Korean Won's value against the US Dollar. Understanding these helps you anticipate movements and time your exchanges better.

US Dollar strength

The Won tends to weaken when the US Dollar strengthens globally (and vice versa). US Federal Reserve interest rate decisions are the primary driver of Dollar strength. When the Fed raises rates, capital flows toward US assets for higher returns, strengthening the Dollar and weakening the Won. The interest rate gap between the US and South Korea is one of the most important factors for the exchange rate.

Export performance

South Korea is a major export economy, with semiconductors, automobiles, electronics, ships, and petrochemicals as key exports. When Korean exports are strong, more foreign currency flows into the country, supporting the Won. Samsung Electronics alone accounts for a significant portion of Korean exports. The global semiconductor cycle is therefore closely linked to Won movements.

China's economy

China is South Korea's largest trading partner, accounting for roughly 25% of Korean exports. When China's economy is growing strongly, demand for Korean goods rises, supporting the Won. When China slows down, Korean exports decline and the Won tends to weaken. This makes the Won unusually sensitive to Chinese economic data.

Risk sentiment

The Korean Won is considered a "high-beta" currency in Asia, meaning it tends to move more than other regional currencies in response to changes in global risk sentiment. When investors feel confident about the global economy, they invest in Korean assets (stocks, bonds), supporting the Won. When fear rises, they pull money out, weakening the Won. This is partly because South Korea's stock market is easily accessible to foreign investors.

Bank of Korea policy

The Bank of Korea (BOK) sets monetary policy, including the base interest rate. The BOK also intervenes in currency markets (known as "smoothing operations") to limit excessive volatility. While the BOK does not target a specific exchange rate, it works to prevent sharp, disorderly moves. The BOK's foreign exchange reserves (over $400 billion) give it substantial firepower for such interventions.

Geopolitical factors

North Korean tensions can occasionally affect the Won, though the impact has diminished over time as markets have become accustomed to periodic escalations. Major geopolitical events (missile tests, diplomatic breakthroughs) can cause short-term Won volatility, but the effect is usually temporary.

Travel Tips for South Korea

South Korea has become one of Asia's hottest tourist destinations, fueled by the global wave of Korean popular culture (K-pop, K-drama, Korean food). Here is practical guidance for handling money during your trip.

Best ways to get Korean Won

  • ATMs in South Korea: Global ATMs are available at all convenience stores (CU, GS25, 7-Eleven) and bank branches. Look for ATMs labeled "Global" or with Visa/Mastercard logos. Most dispense Won and accept international cards. Withdrawal fees vary by your home bank.
  • Money changers in Seoul: The best rates in Seoul are found at licensed money changers in Myeongdong, Dongdaemun, and Itaewon. These small exchange booths offer rates very close to the mid-market rate, often better than banks. No commission is charged; the margin is built into the rate.
  • Banks: Major banks like KEB Hana, Shinhan, and Woori offer reliable exchange services. Rates are fair but not as competitive as specialized money changers. You will need your passport.
  • Incheon Airport: Exchange counters at Incheon International Airport are convenient but offer rates that are 2-4% worse than city rates. Exchange only what you need for the bus or train to the city, then exchange the rest at a better rate later.

Using cards in South Korea

South Korea is one of the most cashless societies in the world. Credit card penetration is among the highest globally, and cards are accepted almost everywhere, from major department stores to small street food stalls. Visa and Mastercard work universally. American Express has limited acceptance. Many Koreans use mobile payment apps like Samsung Pay and Kakao Pay, and some stores accept Apple Pay as well.

T-money card

The T-money card is a rechargeable transit card used throughout South Korea. It works on subways, buses, taxis, and at many convenience stores. You can buy one at any convenience store or subway station for 2,500 KRW and recharge it as needed. It is the most convenient way to pay for public transit and saves about 100 Won per ride compared to paying cash.

Tipping in South Korea

  • Restaurants: Tipping is not expected or customary in South Korea. Leaving a tip can even cause confusion. Service charges may be included at high-end restaurants and hotels.
  • Taxis: No tip expected. The metered fare is the full price.
  • Hotels: Tipping is not standard, though international hotels may have staff accustomed to tips from foreign guests. A small amount (1,000-2,000 KRW) for porters is appreciated but not required.
  • Delivery: No tipping for food delivery or other services.

Shopping and VAT refunds

Foreign tourists spending over 15,000 KRW (roughly $11 USD) at a single tax-free store can claim a VAT refund of about 10% at the airport upon departure. Look for stores with "Tax Free" signs. Keep your receipts and present them at the airport refund counter or kiosk before checking in. For purchases at department stores, you can often get an immediate refund at the customer service desk.

The biggest savings for Americans in Korea come from food (especially local Korean food), public transport, and healthcare. A satisfying meal at a Korean restaurant often costs half or less what a similar meal would cost in the US, and the quality is outstanding. Public transport in Seoul is among the best and cheapest in the world. On the other hand, housing in central Seoul is expensive (though still cheaper than New York or San Francisco), and imported Western goods carry premium prices.

The jeonse deposit system

South Korea has a unique rental system called "jeonse" where tenants pay a large lump-sum deposit (often 50-80% of the property's value) instead of monthly rent. The landlord invests the deposit and returns it when the lease ends. This system means that many Koreans pay little or no monthly rent but need a large upfront sum. As a short-term visitor or expat, you will typically use the monthly rent ("wolse") system, but understanding jeonse helps explain Korean housing economics.

Wire Transfers and Sending Money

If you need to send money between the US and South Korea, several options are available. South Korea's well-developed financial system makes international transfers relatively straightforward.

Online transfer services

  • Wise (formerly TransferWise): Typically offers the best exchange rates for USD to KRW transfers. Fees are low and transparent. Transfers to Korean bank accounts usually arrive within 1-2 business days. This is the recommended option for most people.
  • OFX: Good for larger transfers (over $1,000) with competitive rates and no transfer fees. Good for business payments or large personal transfers.
  • Remitly: Competitive for smaller amounts with fast delivery. Supports bank deposit and cash pickup options in Korea.
  • PayPal/Xoom: Xoom offers transfers to Korean bank accounts. Convenient if you already have a PayPal account, though rates may not be the best.

Bank wire transfers

Traditional SWIFT bank transfers work well between US and Korean banks. Major Korean banks (KEB Hana, Shinhan, Woori, KB Kookmin) all process international wires. Fees are typically $25-50 from the US side, and the Korean receiving bank may charge a fee as well. Processing takes 2-4 business days. For large sums (over $10,000), bank wires offer the security and documentation that some people prefer.

Korean bank accounts for foreigners

If you are staying in South Korea for an extended period, opening a Korean bank account makes receiving and managing money much easier. Foreigners with an Alien Registration Card (ARC) can open accounts at most banks. Without an ARC, some banks still allow account opening with a passport, though features may be limited. KEB Hana and Shinhan banks are particularly foreigner-friendly and have English-speaking staff at main branches.

Tips for saving on transfers

  1. Always compare total cost (fee plus exchange rate markup) across at least 3 services
  2. Send larger amounts less frequently to minimize per-transaction fees
  3. Avoid weekend transfers when spreads may be wider
  4. Set up rate alerts to transfer when the USD/KRW rate is favorable
  5. Consider forward contracts for planned future transfers (available through some services)

The South Korean Economy

South Korea's economic story is one of the most remarkable in modern history. In just a few decades, it transformed from one of the world's poorest countries to a top-15 global economy. This context matters for understanding the Won's position.

Key economic indicators

  • GDP: Approximately $1.7 trillion (nominal), ranking as the 12th-13th largest economy globally
  • GDP per capita: About $33,000, comparable to Southern European countries
  • Population: Around 52 million
  • Major exports: Semiconductors (Samsung, SK Hynix), automobiles (Hyundai, Kia), electronics, ships, petrochemicals, steel
  • Major trading partners: China (largest), United States, Japan, Vietnam, Hong Kong

The role of chaebols

South Korea's economy is dominated by large family-controlled conglomerates called "chaebols." The largest include Samsung, Hyundai, SK, LG, and Lotte. Samsung alone accounts for about 20% of South Korea's GDP. These conglomerates drive exports, investment, and employment. Their performance directly affects the Won, as their export earnings are a major source of foreign currency inflows.

US-Korea economic relationship

The United States is South Korea's second-largest trading partner and a major source of foreign investment. The two countries have a free trade agreement (KORUS FTA), which has deepened economic ties. Bilateral trade exceeds $150 billion annually. US military presence in South Korea (approximately 28,500 troops) also creates economic connections. This strong relationship supports the Won through trade and investment flows.

Semiconductor industry

South Korea is a global leader in semiconductor manufacturing, particularly memory chips (DRAM and NAND flash). Samsung Electronics and SK Hynix together control over 70% of the global DRAM market. The semiconductor cycle (boom and bust in chip demand) has a significant impact on the Won. When the chip market is strong, Korean export revenue surges and the Won tends to strengthen. When chip demand weakens, the opposite occurs.

Payment Methods in South Korea

South Korea is one of the world's most digitally advanced countries when it comes to payments. Understanding the payment landscape helps you manage money effectively during your visit.

Credit and debit cards

Card acceptance in South Korea is near universal. Even small street food vendors, taxi drivers, and convenience stores accept card payments. Visa and Mastercard are accepted everywhere. JCB (Japanese card network) also has good coverage. South Koreans use cards for an estimated 70%+ of all consumer transactions, one of the highest rates in the world.

Mobile payments

Samsung Pay (using MST technology that works with any card terminal), Kakao Pay (linked to Korea's dominant messaging app), and Naver Pay are the most popular mobile payment platforms. As a foreign visitor, you can use Apple Pay (increasingly accepted) or Samsung Pay if your card is compatible. Many restaurants and shops now also accept QR code payments.

Cash

While Korea is very card-friendly, you should still carry some cash. Traditional markets (like Namdaemun and Gwangjang), some smaller restaurants, older shops, and certain transportation situations may require cash. Carrying 50,000-100,000 KRW ($36-$72) in cash as a backup is a good practice.

Foreign transaction fees

When using a US credit or debit card in South Korea, be aware of foreign transaction fees. Many US cards charge 1-3% on international purchases. Cards with no foreign transaction fees (like Chase Sapphire, Capital One Venture, or similar travel cards) save you money on every purchase. The card's exchange rate is typically very close to the mid-market rate, making card payments often better than cash exchange.

Common Mistakes When Exchanging Won

Avoid these frequent errors to get the most value when converting between USD and KRW.

Exchanging all your money at the airport

Incheon Airport's exchange rates are typically 2-4% worse than city rates. Exchange only enough for transportation to your hotel (about 30,000-50,000 KRW), then exchange the rest at a money changer in Myeongdong or Dongdaemun where rates are significantly better.

Not using a no-fee credit card

If you use a credit card that charges 3% foreign transaction fees, you are effectively paying a 3% surcharge on everything. Getting a travel credit card with no foreign transaction fees before your trip saves money on every purchase. The application process usually takes 1-2 weeks.

Accepting dynamic currency conversion

When paying by card in Korea, you may be asked whether to pay in Won or Dollars. Always choose Won. If you choose Dollars (called dynamic currency conversion or DCC), the merchant's bank sets the exchange rate, which is typically 3-7% worse than what your own bank would charge. This is the single easiest way to save money on a trip.

Forgetting about the large numbers

Because 1 USD equals roughly 1,380 KRW, prices in Korea involve large numbers that can cause confusion. A 15,000 Won meal is about $10.87, not $150. Take a moment to do the mental conversion before assuming something is expensive. The quick trick: divide by 1,400 for a rough Dollar estimate.

Exchanging too many small amounts

Each exchange transaction may include a minimum fee or a less favorable rate for small amounts. Rather than exchanging $20 at a time, exchange enough for several days at once. This is particularly true at banks, where processing takes time and fees may apply.

Frequently Asked Questions

What is the Won symbol?

The Korean Won uses the symbol &; (a letter W with two horizontal strokes). The ISO currency code is KRW. In Korean text, you will also see the Hangul character "원" (won) after the number.

Is South Korea expensive for tourists?

South Korea is moderately priced. Local food, public transport, and convenience items are notably cheaper than the US. Accommodation ranges widely, from affordable guesthouses (30,000-50,000 KRW/night) to luxury hotels ($300+/night). A mid-range daily budget of $80-120 covers accommodation, food, transport, and attractions comfortably.

How much cash should I bring to South Korea?

Because card acceptance is so widespread, you do not need much cash. Bringing $200-300 in USD to exchange upon arrival is sufficient for most trips. You can supplement with ATM withdrawals as needed. The bulk of your spending can go on a no-fee credit card.

Can I use Japanese Yen in South Korea?

No, Japanese Yen is not accepted for purchases in South Korea. You need Korean Won. However, you can exchange Japanese Yen for Korean Won at banks and exchange offices in South Korea. Some exchange offices in areas like Myeongdong offer competitive Yen-to-Won rates.

What is the Korean Won made of?

Korean Won banknotes are made of cotton-based paper (the 1,000, 5,000, and 10,000 Won notes) or polymer (the 50,000 Won note, introduced in 2009). Korean coins are made of various metal alloys. The 50,000 Won note features Shin Saimdang, a noted artist and calligrapher from the Joseon Dynasty, making it one of the few banknotes worldwide to feature a woman.

Why does the Won have so many zeros?

The large numbers in Korean Won reflect the currency's history. After the Korean War, high inflation eroded the currency's value. While some countries address this through redenomination (removing zeros), South Korea has not done so. There have been periodic discussions about creating a "new Won" worth 1,000 current Won, but no change has been implemented. The large numbers are simply a matter of scale and do not indicate weakness.

Is it better to exchange money in the US or in Korea?

Almost always better in Korea. US banks and exchange services typically offer poor rates for Korean Won, as it is not a commonly held currency. Money changers in Seoul offer rates very close to the mid-market rate. The exception is if you find a particularly good deal through an online service like Wise, which may be competitive with Korean money changers.

Can I take Korean Won out of the country?

You can take up to the equivalent of $10,000 USD in Korean Won out of South Korea without declaration. Amounts exceeding this must be declared at customs. For most travelers, this limit is more than sufficient. Any Won remaining after your trip can be exchanged back to USD at the airport (though the rate will not be great) or saved for a future visit.
