What Is CSE Citation Style

CSE citation style is the formatting standard developed by the Council of Science Editors for use in scientific publications across biology, chemistry, physics, earth sciences, and medicine. Originally known as the CBE (Council of Biology Editors) style, it was renamed in 2000 when the organization broadened its scope beyond biology. The current reference is Scientific Style and Format: The CSE Manual for Authors, Editors, and Publishers, 8th edition.

What distinguishes CSE from other citation styles is its flexibility. Rather than mandating a single approach to referencing, CSE provides three distinct documentation systems. Each system serves different publishing conventions and disciplinary preferences. Researchers in ecology might use name-year format, while biomedical journal submissions typically require citation-sequence. Understanding which system to use and how each one formats citations is essential for anyone publishing in the sciences.

CSE style emphasizes brevity and precision. Journal names are abbreviated according to standardized lists, author names use initials without periods, and titles generally follow sentence case. These conventions reflect the scientific writing tradition of conveying maximum information in minimum space.

The Three CSE Documentation Systems

Citation-Sequence System

In the citation-sequence system, each source receives a number based on the order in which it first appears in the text. The first source cited becomes number 1, the second becomes number 2, and so on. These numbers appear in the text as superscript numerals or numbers in brackets. The reference list at the end of the paper is ordered numerically, matching the sequence of first citation in the text.

If the same source is cited again later in the paper, it retains its original number. This system is common in medical and biomedical journals, where it minimizes disruption to reading flow by replacing lengthy author-date citations with compact numbers.

In-text: "Recent studies1,2 have confirmed..." or "Recent studies [1,2] have confirmed..."
Reference list entry: 1. Author. Title. Journal. Year;Volume(Issue):Pages.

Citation-Name System

The citation-name system also uses numbered references in the text, but the numbering is derived from the alphabetical order of the reference list rather than the order of appearance. First, the reference list is compiled and sorted alphabetically by the first author's last name. Then each entry receives a sequential number. Those numbers are used as in-text citations.

This hybrid approach combines the compactness of numbered citations with the organizational logic of alphabetical ordering. Some biology journals prefer this system because it allows readers to predict roughly where a reference falls in the list based on the author's name.

In-text: Same as citation-sequence (superscript or bracketed numbers)
Reference list: Alphabetically ordered, then numbered sequentially

Name-Year System

The name-year system functions similarly to APA or Harvard referencing. In-text citations include the author's last name and the year of publication in parentheses: (Darwin 2024). The reference list is arranged alphabetically by author name without numbers. This is the most familiar system for researchers who have worked with social science citation formats.

The name-year system is widely used in ecology, evolution, and environmental science journals. It provides immediate author and date context for the reader without requiring them to cross-reference a numbered list.

In-text: (Author Year) or Author (Year)
Reference list: Author. Year. Title. Journal. Volume(Issue):Pages.

CSE Journal Article Formatting

Journal articles are the dominant source type in scientific literature. Each CSE system formats them differently, particularly regarding the placement of the year.

Name-Year Format

Author(s). Year. Article title. Journal Abbrev. Volume(Issue):Pages.

Example: Watson JD, Crick FH. 1953. Molecular structure of nucleic acids. Nature. 171(4356):737-738.

Citation-Sequence Format

Author(s). Article title. Journal Abbrev. Year;Volume(Issue):Pages.

Example: Watson JD, Crick FH. Molecular structure of nucleic acids. Nature. 1953;171(4356):737-738.

Citation-Name Format

The citation-name format uses the same reference structure as citation-sequence. The only difference is the ordering of the reference list (alphabetical rather than by order of appearance).

Key formatting details apply across all three systems. The article title uses sentence case with no quotation marks or italics. The journal name is abbreviated according to NLM or BIOSIS conventions. A semicolon separates the year from the volume in citation-sequence and citation-name, while the name-year system uses a period after the year.

Books, Websites, and Reports

Books

Book citations in CSE include the place of publication with the state or country abbreviation in parentheses, followed by the publisher name. The format for name-year is:

Author(s). Year. Title. Place (State): Publisher. Total pages p.

For citation-sequence/citation-name:

Author(s). Title. Place (State): Publisher; Year. Total pages p.

Note the semicolon before the year in numbered systems compared to the period-year-period structure in name-year. Book titles use sentence case and are not italicized in CSE style, which is a notable departure from most other citation formats.

Websites and Online Sources

Online sources in CSE require specific qualifiers and dates. The format includes an [Internet] designation, an access date in brackets, and the full URL introduced by "Available from:"

Name-year: Author/Organization. Year. Title. [Internet]. Place: Publisher; [cited Year Mon Day]. Available from: URL
Citation-sequence: Author/Organization. Title. [Internet]. Place: Publisher; Year [cited Year Mon Day]. Available from: URL

The cited date records when you accessed the source. This is mandatory for all online references because web content can change or be removed at any time. Format the date as Year Mon Day, using the three-letter month abbreviation.

Reports

Government and institutional reports follow book formatting conventions but may include report numbers. If the organization is both the author and the publisher, it appears in both positions. The report number follows the title, and page count is typically included at the end.

Organization. Year. Title. Place: Publisher. Report No.: xxx. Total p.

Journal Abbreviation Rules

One of the most distinctive features of CSE style is its requirement for abbreviated journal names. Unlike APA or MLA, which use full journal names, CSE follows the conventions established by the National Library of Medicine (NLM) and BIOSIS.

The abbreviation rules are systematic. Single-word journal names are not abbreviated (e.g., Nature, Science, Cell). For multi-word titles, articles (the, a, an), prepositions (of, in, for), and conjunctions (and, or) are omitted. Each remaining word is abbreviated to a standardized form.

Author Listing and Et Al. Rules

CSE has specific rules for how many authors to include in a citation, and these rules differ between in-text citations and reference list entries.

Reference List

In the reference list, include all authors if there are 10 or fewer. List them in the format: Last Name Initials, with no periods after initials and commas separating each author. Use "et al." only when there are more than 10 authors. In that case, list the first 10 followed by "et al."

1-10 authors: List all (e.g., Smith AB, Jones CD, Williams EF.)
11+ authors: List first 10, then et al.

In-Text Citations (Name-Year Only)

For name-year in-text citations, the rules depend on the number of authors. One or two authors: include both names. Three or more authors: use the first author followed by "et al." This parallels conventions in other author-date systems like APA.

1 author: (Smith 2024)
2 authors: (Smith and Jones 2024)
3+ authors: (Smith et al. 2024)

Author Name Format

CSE uses a specific format for author names that differs from most other styles. Last names appear first, followed by initials with no periods and no spaces between initials: Watson JD, not Watson, J. D. This compact format is standard across all three CSE systems.

Scientific Writing Best Practices

Proper citations are one component of effective scientific writing. The following practices, drawn from the CSE manual and general scientific writing principles, will strengthen any research paper.

Use Primary Sources

Whenever possible, cite the original research rather than secondary summaries, textbooks, or review articles. Primary sources provide the actual data and methodology that support your claims. If you must cite a review article, do so to reference the synthesis or theoretical framework it provides, not the individual studies it summarizes.

Cite Recent Literature

Scientific knowledge advances rapidly. While foundational works remain relevant, your reference list should demonstrate awareness of current research. A reference list dominated by sources from 20 or more years ago may signal to reviewers that you have not adequately surveyed the recent literature, unless your paper is specifically historical in scope.

Be Precise with Claims

Every factual claim that is not common knowledge should be supported by a citation. This includes statistical figures, specific findings, and theoretical assertions. Vague references to "studies have shown" without specific citations weaken your argument and frustrate readers who want to verify your claims.

Organize References Carefully

In the citation-sequence system, the order of your references reflects the structure of your paper. Ensure that numbers are assigned consistently and that no gaps or duplicates exist. In the name-year and citation-name systems, double-check alphabetization, particularly for authors with prefixes, hyphenated names, or non-English naming conventions.

Verify Abbreviations

Incorrect journal abbreviations are a common cause of manuscript rejection or revision requests. Always verify abbreviations against the NLM Catalog or BIOSIS Serial Sources rather than guessing. A misspelled or incorrect abbreviation can prevent readers from locating the cited source.

Common CSE Citation Errors

Scientific manuscripts frequently contain citation formatting errors. Awareness of these common mistakes can help you catch them during revision.

Using Full Journal Names

The most frequent CSE error is failing to abbreviate journal names. Unlike APA, CSE requires abbreviated forms. Writing "Journal of Biological Chemistry" instead of "J Biol Chem" does not conform to CSE standards.

Adding Periods After Author Initials

CSE author initials do not include periods or spaces. Writing "Smith, J. K." follows APA conventions, not CSE. The correct CSE format is "Smith JK" with no punctuation between the last name and initials except a space.

Mixing Documentation Systems

Some writers accidentally use name-year formatting in the reference list while using numbered citations in the text, or vice versa. Choose one system and apply it consistently throughout the entire paper. Mixing systems confuses readers and will be flagged by journal editors.

Omitting Cited Dates for Online Sources

CSE requires a [cited Year Mon Day] date for every internet source. Omitting this element is a formatting error. The cited date should reflect when you actually accessed the source, not the publication date.

Incorrect Year Placement

In name-year format, the year follows the author immediately. In citation-sequence and citation-name, the year appears after the journal name, preceded by a semicolon for journals or after the publisher for books. Placing the year in the wrong position is a system-specific error that indicates unfamiliarity with the chosen format.

Forgetting the [Internet] Qualifier

Online sources must include the [Internet] designation after the title. This qualifier tells readers that the source is a digital resource and sets the expectation for a URL and access date in the citation.

Frequently Asked Questions

What are the three CSE documentation systems?
CSE provides three systems: citation-sequence (sources numbered in order of first appearance), citation-name (reference list alphabetized then numbered), and name-year (author-date format with alphabetical reference list). Each system has different rules for in-text citations and reference list formatting. The choice depends on your journal's requirements or instructor's preference.
Which CSE system should I use?
Check your target journal's submission guidelines or your instructor's requirements. Citation-sequence is most common in biomedical and clinical journals. Name-year is preferred in ecology, evolutionary biology, and environmental science. Citation-name is used by some biology and chemistry publications. When no preference is specified, name-year is often the safest default.
How do I abbreviate journal names in CSE style?
CSE uses standardized journal abbreviations from the NLM Catalog or BIOSIS Serial Sources. Single-word journal names are not abbreviated. For multi-word names, omit articles, prepositions, and conjunctions, then abbreviate the remaining words. For example, "Journal of Biological Chemistry" becomes "J Biol Chem." Always verify abbreviations against official sources.
How do I handle et al. in CSE citations?
In the reference list, list all authors when there are 10 or fewer. For 11 or more authors, list the first 10 followed by "et al." For name-year in-text citations, use et al. for works with three or more authors. For numbered systems, the in-text citation is simply a number, so et al. only applies to the reference list.
What is the difference between CSE and APA?
CSE is designed for natural sciences while APA serves social sciences. Key differences: CSE abbreviates journal names (APA uses full names), CSE omits periods from author initials (APA includes them), CSE offers three documentation systems (APA uses only author-date), and CSE does not italicize or use quotation marks for article titles. The reference list formatting and punctuation patterns differ significantly.
Do I need access dates in CSE citations?
Yes, CSE requires a cited date for all internet and online sources. Format it as [cited Year Mon Day] using three-letter month abbreviations. This is mandatory because online content can change or be removed. The date records when you verified the information was available at the given URL.

Yes, this tool is completely free with no signup or registration required. You can generate unlimited CSE citations in any of the three documentation systems for journal articles, books, websites, and reports.
How do I cite a website in CSE format?
CSE website citations include: Author or Organization. Title. [Internet]. Place: Publisher; Date [cited Year Mon Day]. Available from: URL. The [Internet] qualifier and the cited date are both required. If no individual author exists, use the organization name in both the author and publisher positions.
What edition of CSE is current?
The current edition is the 8th edition of Scientific Style and Format: The CSE Manual for Authors, Editors, and Publishers, published in 2014. It is the comprehensive reference for all CSE citation and formatting rules, covering documentation systems, manuscript preparation, and scientific nomenclature.
Can I use CSE for non-science papers?
CSE is designed specifically for the natural and physical sciences. While technically any paper could use it, most non-science disciplines have their own preferred styles (APA for social sciences, MLA for humanities, Chicago for history). Use CSE only when your journal, course, or publisher specifically requires it.
