Understanding Net Income vs Gross Income

Your gross income is the total amount your employer pays you before anything is taken out. Your net income, sometimes called take-home pay, is what you actually receive after federal taxes, state taxes, Social Security, Medicare, and any other deductions are subtracted. The gap between these two numbers is often larger than people expect, and understanding it is important for budgeting, financial planning, and negotiating compensation.

I've found that most people can roughly guess their gross salary, but surprisingly few can accurately state their net income. When someone says "I make $85,000 a year," they're quoting gross. After all deductions, a single filer in Maryland earning $85,000 takes home closer to $62,000. That's roughly 73% of gross, meaning 27 cents of every dollar goes to various taxes and withholdings. For budgeting purposes, the net number is the only one that matters.

This concept differs from a standard salary calculator in an important way. A salary calculator typically converts between hourly, weekly, monthly, and annual gross amounts. A net income calculator goes further by applying all the tax and deduction layers to show you the actual spendable amount. If you're evaluating a job offer, comparing cost of living between states, or planning a major purchase, you need the net figure.

As the Wikipedia article on net income explains, this concept applies to both individuals and businesses, though the deductions differ. For personal finance, understanding your net income is the foundation of every budgeting framework, from 50/30/20 to zero-based budgeting.

FICA Taxes Explained

FICA stands for Federal Insurance Contributions Act, and it funds Social Security and Medicare. Unlike income tax, FICA is a flat-rate tax that applies from the first dollar you earn, with no standard deduction or personal exemption. This makes it particularly impactful for lower and middle-income earners.

Social Security tax is 6.2% on wages up to $176,100 in 2025. Your employer pays a matching 6.2%, so the total Social Security tax on your wages is 12.4%. Once your wages exceed $176,100, no additional Social Security tax is withheld for the rest of the year. This is why you might notice slightly larger paychecks toward the end of the year if you earn above this threshold.

Medicare tax is 1.45% on all wages with no cap. High earners pay an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax on wages exceeding $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). Unlike Social Security, there's no ceiling on Medicare tax. The total FICA burden for most workers is 7.65% (6.2% + 1.45%), which is a substantial chunk of income that often gets overlooked when people think about their tax burden.

One thing most people don't realize: FICA is separate from income tax and can't be reduced by deductions like the standard deduction. Even if your income tax is zero because of deductions and credits, you'll still owe FICA on every dollar of wages. There are discussions about this on Stack Overflow's payroll tag where developers building payroll systems frequently address the nuances of FICA calculation.

Net Income Calculator vs Salary Calculator

People often confuse these two tools, and it's important to understand what each one does. A salary calculator converts your pay between different time periods and formats: hourly to annual, monthly to weekly, and so on. It deals with gross numbers only. A net income calculator, which is what you're using here, takes your gross salary and subtracts all applicable taxes and deductions to show you the actual spendable amount.

If someone asks "what's my salary?" they usually mean gross. If someone asks "what do I actually make?" they mean net. The distinction matters when you're creating a budget, since your rent, groceries, and bills come out of net income, not gross. I've seen too many budget plans built on gross salary numbers that fall apart because the person didn't account for the 20-30% that goes to taxes.

For developers building financial calculators, npm has several income tax calculation packages that handle the bracket logic for multiple countries. The U.S. progressive system is conceptually simple but has enough edge cases (FICA caps, additional Medicare, AMT) that using a tested library is often wiser than building from scratch.

The FICA calculation properly handles the Social Security wage base ($176,100 for 2025) and the additional Medicare tax on wages exceeding $200,000. Pre-tax deductions reduce taxable income for both federal and state purposes but not for FICA, which matches how employer withholding actually works.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between net income and gross income?
Gross income is your total earnings before any deductions. Net income is what remains after subtracting federal income tax, state income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and any pre-tax deductions like 401(k) contributions. Net income is your actual take-home pay, the amount deposited into your bank account. For most workers, net income is 65-85% of gross income depending on tax bracket and state.
Why is my take-home pay so much less than my salary?
Multiple layers of taxes apply to your paycheck. Federal income tax takes 10-37% (progressively), FICA takes 7.65% (Social Security 6.2% + Medicare 1.45%), and state income tax can add another 0-13.3% depending on where you live. Combined, these deductions typically reduce your gross pay by 20-35%. Pre-tax deductions like health insurance and retirement contributions further reduce your paycheck but provide other benefits.
How can I increase my net income without a raise?
Several strategies can boost take-home pay without a salary increase. increase pre-tax deductions like HSA contributions (triple tax advantage). Adjust your W-4 withholding if you're consistently getting large tax refunds (that's your money being loaned to the government interest-free). Take advantage of all available tax credits, especially the Earned Income Tax Credit, Child Tax Credit, and education credits. Moving to a lower-tax state is the most impactful change for remote workers.
Does a 401(k) contribution reduce my net income?
A traditional 401(k) contribution reduces your taxable income, which lowers your income tax. However, it also reduces your take-home pay since the money goes to your retirement account instead of your bank account. The net effect is that each dollar contributed costs you less than a dollar in reduced take-home pay because of the tax savings. For someone in the 24% bracket, a $1,000 401(k) contribution only reduces take-home pay by about $760 (after the $240 tax savings).
What's the Social Security wage base for 2025?
The Social Security wage base for 2025 is $176,100. This means you pay the 6.2% Social Security tax on the first $176,100 of wages. Income above this threshold is not subject to Social Security tax, which is why higher earners may notice slightly larger paychecks later in the year after reaching the cap. Medicare tax (1.45%) has no wage base limit and applies to all earnings. An additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies on wages over $200,000 for single filers.
How does this differ from a paycheck calculator?
A paycheck calculator typically shows the amount for a single pay period (biweekly, semi-monthly, etc.) and may include employer-specific deductions like health insurance premiums. This net income calculator focuses on the annual picture and breaks it down into all common pay periods. Both tools are useful, but the net income view gives you a better foundation for annual budgeting and financial planning. We provide monthly, biweekly, and weekly breakdowns as well.
Are bonuses taxed differently?
Bonuses are subject to the same tax rates as regular income, but withholding is often different. Employers typically use the "percentage method" (22% flat federal withholding for bonuses under $1 million) or the "aggregate method" (treating the bonus as if it were a regular paycheck). The higher withholding on bonuses often makes people think they're taxed more, but at tax filing time, the actual tax is the same as any other income. If too much was withheld, you'll get it back as a refund.

Understanding Pre-Tax vs Post-Tax Deductions

The distinction between pre-tax and post-tax deductions has a significant impact on your net income, and many employees don't fully understand how each type works. Pre-tax deductions reduce your taxable income before federal and state income taxes are calculated. Post-tax deductions are taken after taxes have been withheld.

Common Pre-Tax Deductions

Traditional 401(k) contributions are the most common pre-tax deduction. If you earn $75,000 and contribute $6,000 to your 401(k), your taxable income drops to $69,000, which reduces both your federal and state tax liability. At a 22% marginal federal rate, that $6,000 contribution effectively costs you only $4,680 because you save $1,320 in taxes. Health insurance premiums paid through your employer's plan are also typically pre-tax through a Section 125 cafeteria plan.

Health Savings Account (HSA) contributions are triple-tax-advantaged: contributions are pre-tax, growth is tax-free, and withdrawals for qualified medical expenses are tax-free. The 2025 contribution limits are $4,300 for individual coverage and $8,550 for family coverage. If your employer offers an HSA-eligible high-deductible health plan, maximizing your HSA contribution is one of the most tax-efficient strategies available.

Flexible Spending Accounts (FSAs) for healthcare and dependent care are also pre-tax. The healthcare FSA limit is $3,300 for 2025, and the dependent care FSA limit is $5,000. Unlike HSAs, FSAs are "use it or lose it" (with some employers offering a $640 carryover or 2.5-month grace period), so estimate your expenses carefully before enrolling.

Common Post-Tax Deductions

Roth 401(k) contributions are post-tax, meaning they don't reduce your current taxable income. However, qualified withdrawals in retirement are completely tax-free, including the investment growth. If you expect to be in a higher tax bracket in retirement, Roth contributions may be the better choice despite the lack of current tax savings.

Union dues, charitable contributions through payroll, life insurance premiums beyond the employer-provided amount, and wage garnishments are all typically post-tax deductions. Understanding which category each deduction falls into helps you accurately estimate your take-home pay.

Anatomy of a Paycheck

When you look at your pay stub, the journey from gross pay to net pay involves multiple deductions at different stages. Understanding this flow helps you verify that your employer is withholding correctly and identify opportunities to optimize your take-home pay.

The calculation sequence for a typical biweekly paycheck works as follows. Start with gross pay (annual salary divided by 26 pay periods). Subtract pre-tax deductions (401k, health insurance, HSA, FSA). The result is your taxable gross. Apply federal income tax withholding based on your W-4 elections. Apply state income tax withholding (if applicable). Apply Social Security tax at 6.2% (on income up to $176,100 in 2025). Apply Medicare tax at 1.45% (plus 0.9% Additional Medicare Tax on income above $200,000 for single filers). Subtract any post-tax deductions. The final result is your net pay, the amount deposited into your bank account.

Strategies to Optimize Net Income

While you can't change the tax rates, there are several legitimate strategies to increase the amount that reaches your bank account.

Optimize Your W-4

The W-4 form determines how much federal income tax your employer withholds from each paycheck. Many employees fill it out once when hired and never revisit it. If you consistently receive large tax refunds (more than $500), you're having too much withheld, which means you're giving the government an interest-free loan throughout the year. Reducing your withholding increases your net pay on each check. Conversely, if you owe a large amount at tax time, you should increase your withholding to avoid underpayment penalties.

The IRS Tax Withholding Estimator tool can help you determine the optimal W-4 settings. Major life events (marriage, divorce, having a child, buying a home, starting a side business) are all triggers to review and update your W-4.

Maximize Employer Benefits

If your employer offers a 401(k) match, contributing at least enough to capture the full match is one of the most valuable financial decisions you can make. A typical match of 50% on the first 6% of salary means your employer adds $2,250 per year to a $75,000 salary. That's a 100% return on the first $4,500 you contribute, and the contribution itself reduces your tax bill.

Other employer benefits that affect net income include commuter benefits (pre-tax transit and parking), employee stock purchase plans (ESPP, which often offer a 15% discount), wellness program incentives, and tuition reimbursement. Each of these either reduces your taxable income or provides a financial benefit that increases your effective compensation without additional tax liability.

Consider Filing Status Carefully

Your filing status affects which tax brackets apply to your income. Married filing jointly nearly doubles the width of each bracket compared to single filing, which can provide significant tax savings for couples where one spouse earns significantly more than the other. Head of household status provides wider brackets and a larger standard deduction than single filing for qualifying individuals with dependents. Choosing the optimal filing status is one of the simplest ways to reduce your effective tax rate.

Where you live has a dramatic effect on your net income. Nine states have no income tax at all: Alaska, Florida, Nevada, New Hampshire (interest and dividends only through 2024, fully eliminated in 2025), South Dakota, Tennessee, Texas, Washington, and Wyoming. Living in one of these states effectively gives you a 3% to 13% raise compared to high-tax states, depending on your income level and the state you're comparing against.

Over a 10-year career, that difference compounds to $30,000 to $60,000 in additional after-tax income, not counting the potential for investing those savings.

FICA Taxes Explained

FICA (Federal Insurance Contributions Act) taxes fund Social Security and Medicare. Every W-2 employee pays FICA taxes, and understanding how they work is essential for accurate net income calculations.

Social Security Tax

The Social Security tax rate is 6.2% for employees, with an equal 6.2% paid by the employer. This tax applies only to earnings up to the Social Security wage base, which is $176,100 for 2025. Once your year-to-date earnings exceed this threshold, no more Social Security tax is withheld for the remainder of the year. This means high earners effectively get a raise in the paycheck where they cross the wage base limit.

For a $100,000 salary, the full $100,000 is subject to Social Security tax, resulting in $6,200 in annual employee contributions. For a $200,000 salary, only $176,100 is taxable, resulting in $10,918 in contributions (the same maximum regardless of how high your salary goes above the cap).

Medicare Tax

The Medicare tax rate is 1.45% with no income cap, meaning all earnings are subject to this tax regardless of how much you earn. Also, the Additional Medicare Tax of 0.9% applies to earnings above $200,000 for single filers ($250,000 for married filing jointly). This additional tax is only paid by the employee and is not matched by the employer.

For a $250,000 salary (single filer), the Medicare calculation would be: $250,000 x 1.45% = $3,625 base Medicare, plus ($250,000 - $200,000) x 0.9% = $450 Additional Medicare Tax, for a total of $4,075.

Self-Employment Tax Comparison

Self-employed individuals pay both the employee and employer portions of FICA, totaling 15.3% (12.4% Social Security + 2.9% Medicare). However, they can deduct the employer-equivalent portion (7.65%) when calculating their adjusted gross income, which partially offsets the higher rate. If you're comparing a W-2 position to freelance work, the additional 7.65% in self-employment tax is a significant cost to factor into your rate calculation. Salary vs Hourly Net Income Comparison

Whether you're paid a salary or an hourly rate affects your net income calculation in subtle but important ways. Understanding these differences helps you compare job offers and evaluate whether a switch from hourly to salary (or vice versa) is financially beneficial.

Salaried employees receive a fixed amount per pay period regardless of hours worked. Your annual salary divided by the number of pay periods gives your gross pay per period. The advantage is income predictability. The disadvantage is that overtime (hours beyond 40 per week) is typically not compensated with additional pay for exempt salaried employees.

Hourly employees are paid for each hour worked, with overtime (1.5x base rate) required by federal law for non-exempt employees working more than 40 hours per week. An hourly employee earning $36.06 per hour and working exactly 40 hours per week earns $75,004.80 annually, which is roughly equivalent to a $75,000 salary. However, if that hourly employee works an average of 45 hours per week, their annual income jumps to $84,380.40 due to the overtime premium.

The Real Value of a Raise

When your employer offers a raise, the amount that reaches your bank account is always less than the headline number due to progressive taxation. Understanding this helps you set realistic expectations and negotiate more effectively.

A $5,000 raise for someone earning $75,000 doesn't add $5,000 to your net income. That $5,000 falls in the 22% federal bracket (for single filers in 2025), so $1,100 goes to federal income tax. Social Security takes another $310 (6.2%), Medicare takes $72.50 (1.45%), and state tax (at an estimated 5%) takes $250. The total deductions on the raise are $1,732.50, leaving you with a net increase of $3,267.50, or about 65.4% of the gross raise.

As you move into higher brackets, the percentage you keep decreases. A $5,000 raise for someone earning $185,000 falls in the 32% federal bracket, and with FICA and state taxes, you might keep only about 55% of the raise. Understanding your marginal tax rate helps you appreciate the true value of salary increases, bonuses, and other forms of additional compensation.

This same logic applies in reverse when evaluating pre-tax deductions. If you increase your 401(k) contribution by $5,000, it doesn't cost you $5,000 in take-home pay. At the 22% federal bracket, the $5,000 contribution reduces your net pay by about $3,267.50 because you save $1,732.50 in taxes. This is why pre-tax retirement contributions are so powerful: the government effectively subsidizes a portion of your savings.

For negotiation purposes, I recommend calculating the after-tax value of any compensation offer before making a decision. A $5,000 raise might sound significant, but when it translates to roughly $130 extra per biweekly paycheck after all deductions, you can better assess whether it meets your needs. Similarly, comparing a raise offer to the value of non-cash benefits (additional PTO, remote work flexibility, better health insurance) helps you make complete compensation decisions rather than focusing solely on the gross salary number.

Another useful frame for evaluating raises is the real wage growth perspective. If inflation is running at 3% and your raise is 3%, your purchasing power hasn't actually increased. A true "raise" in economic terms is the portion above the inflation rate. On a $75,000 salary with 3% inflation, you need a raise of at least $2,250 just to maintain your current standard of living. Any amount above that represents genuine income growth. This context helps set expectations and provides a data-driven foundation for salary negotiations.
