Understanding Net Income vs Gross Income

Your gross income is the total amount your employer pays you before anything is taken out. Your net income, sometimes called take-home pay, is what you actually receive after federal taxes, state taxes, Social Security, Medicare, and any other deductions are subtracted. The gap between these two numbers is often larger than people expect, and understanding it is crucial for budgeting, financial planning, and negotiating compensation.

I've found that most people can roughly guess their gross salary, but surprisingly few can accurately state their net income. When someone says "I make $85,000 a year," they're quoting gross. After all deductions, a single filer in Maryland earning $85,000 takes home closer to $62,000. That's roughly 73% of gross, meaning 27 cents of every dollar goes to various taxes and withholdings. For budgeting purposes, the net number is the only one that matters.

This concept differs from a standard salary calculator in an important way. A salary calculator typically converts between hourly, weekly, monthly, and annual gross amounts. A net income calculator goes further by applying all the tax and deduction layers to show you the actual spendable amount. If you're evaluating a job offer, comparing cost of living between states, or planning a major purchase, you need the net figure.

As the Wikipedia article on net income explains, this concept applies to both individuals and businesses, though the deductions differ. For personal finance, understanding your net income is the foundation of every budgeting framework, from 50/30/20 to zero-based budgeting.

This is one of the most effective tax reduction strategies available to W-2 employees, and it's something I recommend everyone evaluate carefully.

FICA Taxes Explained

FICA stands for Federal Insurance Contributions Act, and it funds Social Security and Medicare. Unlike income tax, FICA is a flat-rate tax that applies from the first dollar you earn, with no standard deduction or personal exemption. This makes it particularly impactful for lower and middle-income earners.

Social Security tax is 6.2% on wages up to $176,100 in 2025. Your employer pays a matching 6.2%, so the total Social Security tax on your wages is 12.4%. Once your wages exceed $176,100, no additional Social Security tax is withheld for the rest of the year. This is why you might notice slightly larger paychecks toward the end of the year if you earn above this threshold.

Medicare tax is 1.45% on all wages with no cap. High earners pay an additional 0.9% Medicare surtax on wages exceeding $200,000 (single) or $250,000 (married filing jointly). Unlike Social Security, there's no ceiling on Medicare tax. The total FICA burden for most workers is 7.65% (6.2% + 1.45%), which is a substantial chunk of income that often gets overlooked when people think about their tax burden.

One thing most people don't realize: FICA is separate from income tax and can't be reduced by deductions like the standard deduction. Even if your income tax is zero because of deductions and credits, you'll still owe FICA on every dollar of wages. There are discussions about this on Stack Overflow's payroll tag where developers building payroll systems frequently address the nuances of FICA calculation.

State Tax Impact by State

Where you live has a dramatic impact on your net income. The difference between living in a no-income-tax state like Texas or Florida versus a high-tax state like California can be thousands of dollars per year. I've calculated the net income for an $85,000 single filer across several states to illustrate the range.

The spread between the highest and lowest tax states for an $85,000 earner is roughly $6,500 per year. That's over $540 per month. When you're comparing job offers in different states, this difference alone can outweigh a significant salary differential. Of course, cost of living, property taxes, and sales taxes are also factors, but state income tax is one of the most straightforward to calculate and compare.

It's worth noting that states without income tax often compensate through higher property taxes or sales taxes. Texas, for example, has notably high property taxes. Washington state has one of the highest sales tax rates in the country. The no-income-tax advantage is real but should be evaluated in the context of total tax burden, not in isolation. For a broader view, Hacker News regularly features salary and cost-of-living comparison discussions, especially among remote workers choosing where to base themselves.

Net Income Calculator vs Salary Calculator

People often confuse these two tools, and it's important to understand what each one does. A salary calculator converts your pay between different time periods and formats: hourly to annual, monthly to weekly, and so on. It deals with gross numbers only. A net income calculator, which is what you're using here, takes your gross salary and subtracts all applicable taxes and deductions to show you the actual spendable amount.

If someone asks "what's my salary?" they usually mean gross. If someone asks "what do I actually make?" they mean net. The distinction matters when you're creating a budget, since your rent, groceries, and bills come out of net income, not gross. I've seen too many budget plans built on gross salary numbers that fall apart because the person didn't account for the 20-30% that goes to taxes.

For developers building financial calculators, npm has several income tax calculation packages that handle the bracket logic for multiple countries. The U.S. progressive system is conceptually simple but has enough edge cases (FICA caps, additional Medicare, AMT) that using a tested library is often wiser than building from scratch.

Our Testing Methodology

This calculator uses 2025 federal tax brackets from IRS Revenue Procedure 2024-40, FICA rates current as of January 2025, and state income tax rates sourced from each state's department of revenue. I've validated the results against the IRS withholding estimator, ADP's paycheck calculator, and SmartAsset's income tax calculator.

State taxes are approximated using a flat effective rate for simplicity, since most states have their own progressive bracket systems. This gives a reasonable estimate for planning purposes but may differ from exact state tax liability. For precision, consult your state's tax authority or use a state-specific calculator.

The FICA calculation properly handles the Social Security wage base ($176,100 for 2025) and the additional Medicare tax on wages exceeding $200,000. Pre-tax deductions reduce taxable income for both federal and state purposes but not for FICA, which matches how employer withholding actually works.

Frequently Asked Questions

What's the difference between net income and gross income?
Gross income is your total earnings before any deductions. Net income is what remains after subtracting federal income tax, state income tax, Social Security, Medicare, and any pre-tax deductions like 401(k) contributions. Net income is your actual take-home pay, the amount deposited into your bank account. For most workers, net income is 65-85% of gross income depending on tax bracket and state.
Why is my take-home pay so much less than my salary?
Multiple layers of taxes apply to your paycheck. Federal income tax takes 10-37% (progressively), FICA takes 7.65% (Social Security 6.2% + Medicare 1.45%), and state income tax can add another 0-13.3% depending on where you live. Combined, these deductions typically reduce your gross pay by 20-35%. Pre-tax deductions like health insurance and retirement contributions further reduce your paycheck but provide other benefits.
How can I increase my net income without a raise?
Several strategies can boost take-home pay without a salary increase. Maximize pre-tax deductions like HSA contributions (triple tax advantage). Adjust your W-4 withholding if you're consistently getting large tax refunds (that's your money being loaned to the government interest-free). Take advantage of all available tax credits, especially the Earned Income Tax Credit, Child Tax Credit, and education credits. Moving to a lower-tax state is the most impactful change for remote workers.
Does a 401(k) contribution reduce my net income?
A traditional 401(k) contribution reduces your taxable income, which lowers your income tax. However, it also reduces your take-home pay since the money goes to your retirement account instead of your bank account. The net effect is that each dollar contributed costs you less than a dollar in reduced take-home pay because of the tax savings. For someone in the 24% bracket, a $1,000 401(k) contribution only reduces take-home pay by about $760 (after the $240 tax savings).
What's the Social Security wage base for 2025?
The Social Security wage base for 2025 is $176,100. This means you pay the 6.2% Social Security tax on the first $176,100 of wages. Income above this threshold is not subject to Social Security tax, which is why higher earners may notice slightly larger paychecks later in the year after reaching the cap. Medicare tax (1.45%) has no wage base limit and applies to all earnings. An additional 0.9% Medicare surtax applies on wages over $200,000 for single filers.
How does this differ from a paycheck calculator?
A paycheck calculator typically shows the amount for a single pay period (biweekly, semi-monthly, etc.) and may include employer-specific deductions like health insurance premiums. This net income calculator focuses on the annual picture and breaks it down into all common pay periods. Both tools are useful, but the net income view gives you a better foundation for annual budgeting and financial planning. We provide monthly, biweekly, and weekly breakdowns as well.
Are bonuses taxed differently?
Bonuses are subject to the same tax rates as regular income, but withholding is often different. Employers typically use the "percentage method" (22% flat federal withholding for bonuses under $1 million) or the "aggregate method" (treating the bonus as if it were a regular paycheck). The higher withholding on bonuses often makes people think they're taxed more, but at tax filing time, the actual tax is the same as any other income. If too much was withheld, you'll get it back as a refund.
