How to Use the Tank Volume Calculator

This calculator determines the total capacity and partial fill volume for five common tank shapes. Select your tank type from the tabs above, enter the internal dimensions, and set the fill level as a percentage. The tool returns the total capacity, the volume of liquid at your specified fill level, and the remaining empty space, all displayed in cubic inches, cubic feet, US gallons, and liters.

I built this tool because I have dealt with tank sizing questions for water storage projects, fuel tank replacements, and aquarium setups over the years. Getting the partial fill volume right is particularly important for horizontal cylindrical tanks, where the relationship between liquid depth and volume is not linear. A tank that is half full by depth is not necessarily half full by volume unless it is a vertical cylinder or a rectangular box.

Tank Volume Formulas

Horizontal Cylindrical Tank

The horizontal cylinder is the most common shape for above-ground fuel storage tanks, propane tanks, and transport tankers. The total volume is straightforward to calculate, but the partial fill volume requires a more involved formula because the cross-section changes with depth.

Total Volume

V = π × R² × L

Where R is the internal radius (half the diameter) and L is the total length of the cylinder.

Partial Fill Volume

V = L × [ R² × arccos((R − h) / R) − (R − h) × √(2Rh − h²) ]

This formula calculates the cross-sectional area of the filled segment and multiplies it by the length. The variable h represents the liquid depth measured from the bottom of the tank. When h equals the diameter, the tank is full. When h equals R (the radius), the tank is exactly half full.

The arccos function returns a value in radians, which is important for getting the correct area. This formula is derived from the integral of the circular cross-section. For anyone working through this by hand, note that arccos expects the ratio (R-h)/R to fall between -1 and 1, which is guaranteed as long as h stays between 0 and the diameter.

For a horizontal tank that appears half full by sight, the actual volume is exactly 50% only when the liquid level sits at the center line. Above or below the center, small changes in depth produce larger or smaller volume changes than you might expect.

Vertical Cylindrical Tank

Vertical cylinders are the most common shape for water storage tanks, brewing vessels, and chemical process tanks. The volume calculation is simpler than the horizontal orientation because the cross-sectional area stays constant regardless of fill level.

Total Volume

V = π × R² × H

Where R is the internal radius and H is the total height. For partial fill, simply replace H with the actual liquid height h. This makes vertical cylinders particularly convenient for gauging, as a dipstick measurement maps linearly to volume.

Partial Fill Volume

V = π × R² × h

The percentage fill for a vertical cylinder is directly proportional to the percentage of height filled. If your tank is 60% full by height, it holds exactly 60% of the total volume. This linearity is one reason vertical tanks are preferred when precise volume measurement is a priority.

Rectangular Tank

Rectangular tanks (also called box tanks or cube tanks) are used for aquariums, industrial sumps, rainwater harvesting, and agricultural applications. The volume formula is the simplest of all shapes.

Total Volume

V = L × W × H

Where L is the length, W is the width, and H is the height. Like vertical cylinders, the partial fill volume scales linearly with depth.

Partial Fill Volume

V = L × W × h

For aquariums, subtract the volume occupied by substrate, decorations, and equipment to estimate the actual water volume. A common approach is to fill the tank, mark the water level, remove decorations, and measure the drop in level.

Spherical Tank

Spherical tanks are used for pressurized gas storage (LNG, LPG), space applications, and some specialized chemical storage. The sphere provides the best volume-to-surface-area ratio, meaning it requires the least material for a given capacity.

Total Volume

V = (4/3) × π × R³

Partial Fill Volume (Spherical Cap)

V = π × h² × (3R − h) / 3

This formula gives the volume of a spherical cap, where h is the liquid depth measured from the bottom and R is the sphere radius. When h equals the diameter (2R), this simplifies to the full sphere volume.

Spherical tanks have a non-linear relationship between depth and volume. The volume changes most rapidly near the equator (at h = R) and most slowly near the top and bottom. This makes level gauging in spherical tanks more challenging than in cylindrical or rectangular vessels.

Conical Tank

Conical tanks are used in brewing (conical fermenters), grain storage (hoppers), and settling tanks in water treatment. The cone shape allows gravity to collect solids at the bottom point.

Total Volume

V = (1/3) × π × R² × H

Where R is the radius at the top (widest point) and H is the total height.

Partial Fill Volume

V = (1/3) × π × (R × h / H)² × h

Because the radius at any depth is proportional to the height at that point, the partial fill volume scales with the cube of the fill height ratio. This means that the bottom 50% of a cone holds only 12.5% of the total volume, while the top 50% holds 87.5%. This dramatic non-linearity is why conical tanks are effective for sediment collection but poor for even distribution.

Practical Applications of Tank Volume Calculations

Fuel Delivery and Inventory Management

Fuel distributors and facilities managers rely on tank volume calculations daily. When a delivery truck arrives, you need to know how much fuel the tank can accept without overfilling. This means calculating the current volume based on a stick reading (dipstick measurement), subtracting from the total capacity, and leaving room for thermal expansion. Petroleum products expand approximately 0.06% per degree Fahrenheit, so a 10,000-gallon tank at 60°F will contain about 10,060 gallons worth of product at 70°F. Most tank gauging systems account for this, but manual calculations should include a 2-5% expansion allowance.

Water Treatment and Distribution

Municipal water systems use large cylindrical and rectangular tanks as reservoirs, equalization basins, and treatment vessels. Knowing the volume at various levels is critical for calculating detention times (the average time water spends in the tank), which directly affects treatment effectiveness. A chlorine contact tank, for example, needs a minimum detention time of 30 minutes at peak flow. If the tank volume at the operating level is too small, disinfection will be inadequate. I have seen facilities struggle with this calculation when tanks have irregular shapes or internal baffles that reduce the effective volume.

Brewing and Fermentation

Brewers and winemakers need precise volume measurements for recipe scaling, yeast pitching rates, and tax reporting. A conical fermenter is particularly interesting because the working volume (the amount of beer above the yeast cake) decreases as fermentation progresses and yeast settles into the cone. A 15-barrel conical fermenter with a 60-degree cone angle might have a total capacity of 18 barrels, but the usable volume above the cone is only about 15 barrels. Understanding the geometry of your vessels directly affects batch consistency.

Chemical Process Engineering

In chemical plants, tank volume calculations feed into process design, safety analysis, and regulatory compliance. The EPA requires secondary containment capable of holding 110% of the largest tank volume in a tank farm. Calculating this requires knowing the exact volume of each tank, including the space occupied by internal components like heating coils, agitators, and baffles. For spherical storage vessels holding pressurized gases, the volume calculation also determines the maximum allowable fill level, which is typically 80-85% of total capacity to allow for thermal expansion.

Agricultural Applications

Farmers use tank volume calculations for irrigation planning, fertilizer mixing, and livestock water management. A cattle water tank that serves 50 head needs to hold at least 500-750 gallons for a day's supply (10-15 gallons per cow per day). Knowing the volume at various fill levels helps set float valve heights and plan refill schedules. For spray tanks, precise volume measurement ensures correct pesticide dilution ratios, which affects both crop safety and environmental compliance.

Tank Material Considerations

Steel Tanks

Carbon steel is the most common material for large storage tanks. Typical wall thicknesses range from 3/16" to 1/2" for above-ground tanks and 1/4" to 5/8" for underground tanks. When calculating internal volume, subtract twice the wall thickness from the external diameter. For a tank with a 48" external diameter and 1/4" walls, the internal diameter is 47.5", which reduces the volume by about 2% compared to using external dimensions. Corrosion allowances add another consideration. Most steel tank specifications include a 1/16" corrosion allowance, meaning the initial wall is thicker than the minimum required. Over time, corrosion reduces the internal dimensions slightly, but the effect on volume is negligible.

Fiberglass (FRP) Tanks

Fiberglass reinforced plastic tanks have thicker walls than steel (typically 3/8" to 3/4"), which means the difference between internal and external volume is more significant. A 10,000-gallon FRP tank might have 5-8% less internal volume than the same external dimensions in steel. FRP tanks are popular for chemical storage because they resist corrosion from acids, caustics, and saltwater. The trade-off is lower pressure ratings and temperature limits (typically under 200°F).

Polyethylene Tanks

Rotomolded polyethylene tanks are widely used for water storage, chemical feed, and agricultural applications. Wall thicknesses vary from 3/16" to 1/2" depending on the size and pressure rating. These tanks come in many shapes beyond simple cylinders and rectangles, including vertical with flat bottoms, cone bottoms, and dome tops. For oddly shaped poly tanks, the manufacturer's rated capacity is often the most dependable volume figure, as the actual internal geometry may not conform to simple formulas.

precise Measurement Tips

Getting dependable tank volume calculations starts with precise measurements. Here are the practices I follow when measuring tanks in the field.

Measuring Diameter

For accessible tanks, measure the internal diameter directly using a tape measure across the widest point. Take at least three measurements at different points along the length to check for ovality. For pressurized or enclosed tanks, measure the external diameter and subtract twice the wall thickness (check the tank nameplate or manufacturer's specifications for wall thickness). On older tanks where the nameplate is unreadable, ultrasonic thickness gauges can measure wall thickness without draining the tank.

Measuring Length and Height

For horizontal tanks, measure the length of the cylindrical section only, excluding the heads (end caps). Standard heads add volume that the simple cylinder formula does not account for. A flat head adds no extra volume, a dish head adds about 1-3% to the cylinder volume, and a hemispherical head adds about 5-8%. For precision work, calculate the head volumes separately using the appropriate formulas. For vertical tanks, measure from the internal bottom to the overflow level, not to the top of the shell.

Accounting for Tank Heads

Most cylindrical tanks have dished or elliptical heads rather than flat ends. The two most common types are 2:1 semi-elliptical heads and ASME flanged and dished heads. A 2:1 semi-elliptical head on a 48" diameter tank adds approximately 4,340 cubic inches per head. The formula for a 2:1 elliptical head is V = (2/3) × π × R² × (D/4), where D is the diameter. For precise calculations, add the volume of both heads to the cylinder volume.

Dipstick Calibration

For horizontal tanks, a simple dipstick provides a liquid depth reading, but converting that depth to volume requires the partial fill formula. Many tank operators use strapping charts, which are pre-calculated tables that map depth to volume for a specific tank. These charts account for the actual tank geometry including heads, internal obstructions, and any tilt in the tank installation. If your tank sits on a slope, even a 1-degree tilt can cause a significant difference between the actual volume and the calculated volume based on a center dipstick reading.

Industry Standards and Regulations

UL 142 Above-Ground Tanks

Underwriters Laboratories Standard 142 covers steel above-ground tanks for flammable and combustible liquids. This standard specifies minimum wall thicknesses, weld requirements, and testing procedures. When selecting a tank, the UL 142 rating ensures the tank meets safety requirements for fire codes and insurance compliance. These tanks typically come in standard sizes (275, 500, 1000, 2000, 5000, 10000, and 20000 gallons), which simplifies the volume calculation since the manufacturer provides exact capacities.

API 650 Large Storage Tanks

The American Petroleum Institute Standard 650 covers welded tanks for oil storage with capacities from a few hundred barrels to over a million barrels. These are the large cylindrical tanks you see at refineries and tank farms. API 650 tanks are almost exclusively vertical cylinders with floating or fixed roofs. The standard includes detailed formulas for calculating shell thickness based on the liquid density, tank diameter, and design wind and seismic loads. Volume calculations for these tanks are straightforward (V = π × R² × H) but must account for roof displacement, internal floating roof volume, and bottom sump capacity.

NFPA 30 Fire Code Requirements

The National Fire Protection Association Standard 30 governs the storage and handling of flammable and combustible liquids. It requires that tank volumes be known accurately for purposes of secondary containment sizing, fire suppression design, and emergency response planning. The secondary containment volume must equal at least 110% of the largest single tank volume, calculated at the maximum fill level. For diked areas containing multiple tanks, the containment must hold the volume of the largest tank plus the displacement volume of the other tanks below the dike wall height.

Worked Example Calculations

Example 1 · Horizontal Fuel Tank at 65% Fill

A 275-gallon home heating oil tank has external dimensions of 27" diameter × 44" length. Assuming 1/4" steel walls, the internal dimensions are 26.5" diameter × 43.5" length. At 65% fill, what is the volume of oil in the tank?

Radius R = 26.5 / 2 = 13.25 inches. Length L = 43.5 inches. The fill height at 65% is h = 0.65 × 26.5 = 17.225 inches.

Using the partial fill formula for a horizontal cylinder:

V = 43.5 × [13.25² × arccos((13.25 − 17.225) / 13.25) − (13.25 − 17.225) × √(2 × 13.25 × 17.225 − 17.225²)]

Computing step by step: (R − h) = 13.25 − 17.225 = -3.975. The ratio (R − h)/R = -3.975/13.25 = -0.3. arccos(-0.3) = 1.8755 radians. The term under the square root: 2 × 13.25 × 17.225 − 17.225² = 456.2625 − 296.7006 = 159.5619. The square root is 12.632. Putting it together: V = 43.5 × [175.5625 × 1.8755 − (-3.975) × 12.632] = 43.5 × [329.26 + 50.21] = 43.5 × 379.47 = 16,506.9 cubic inches. Converting to gallons: 16,506.9 × 0.004329 = 71.44 gallons. This makes sense because 65% of 275 gallons is about 178.75 gallons using external dimensions, but our internal dimensions give a smaller total capacity of about 102 gallons for this specific geometry. The actual rated capacity of a standard 275-gallon tank uses external measurements including the heads, which add significant volume.

Example 2 · Vertical Water Tank at 40% Fill

A vertical cylindrical water tank has an internal diameter of 60 inches and a height of 72 inches. At 40% fill, how many gallons of water does it contain?

R = 30 inches. Total volume = π × 30² × 72 = π × 900 × 72 = 203,575.2 cubic inches. At 40% fill, the liquid height is 0.4 × 72 = 28.8 inches. Partial volume = π × 900 × 28.8 = 81,430.1 cubic inches. Converting: 81,430.1 × 0.004329 = 352.5 gallons (approximately 1,334.5 liters).

Example 3 · Rectangular Aquarium Volume

A standard 55-gallon aquarium measures 48" × 13" × 21" (L × W × H). With 2 inches of substrate and the water level 1 inch below the rim, what is the actual water volume?

Effective water height = 21 − 2 (substrate) − 1 (below rim) = 18 inches. Water volume = 48 × 13 × 18 = 11,232 cubic inches. Converting: 11,232 × 0.004329 = 48.6 gallons. This is why a "55-gallon" aquarium typically holds closer to 48-50 gallons of water in practice.

Example 4 · Spherical Propane Tank

A spherical storage vessel has an internal diameter of 40 feet (480 inches). At 80% fill (the maximum recommended level for LPG), what is the liquid volume?

R = 240 inches. Fill height h = 0.8 × 480 = 384 inches. Partial volume = π × 384² × (3 × 240 − 384) / 3 = π × 147,456 × (720 − 384) / 3 = π × 147,456 × 336 / 3 = π × 16,514,880 = 51,878,505 cubic inches. Converting: 51,878,505 × 0.004329 = 224,582 gallons. This is approximately 5,347 barrels of liquid product.

Troubleshooting Common Issues

Discrepancy Between Calculated and Measured Volume

If the calculated volume does not match the actual measured volume when filling or draining a tank, check these potential causes. First, verify that you are using internal dimensions, not external. Second, account for tank heads (end caps) on cylindrical tanks. Third, check for internal components that displace liquid, such as heating coils, agitators, dip tubes, or internal floating roofs. Fourth, confirm the tank is level. A tilted tank will give different dipstick readings depending on where the measurement is taken. Fifth, for older tanks, check for sludge or sediment accumulation at the bottom, which reduces the effective volume.

Temperature Effects on Volume

Liquid volumes change with temperature. Water is most dense at 39.2°F (4°C) and expands as it warms. Petroleum products expand more than water, typically 0.04-0.08% per degree Fahrenheit depending on the product density. When comparing calculated volumes to metered volumes (such as during a fuel delivery), ensure both measurements reference the same temperature. The petroleum industry standardizes volumes to 60°F (15.56°C) using API volume correction factors.

Units Confusion

The most common error in tank volume calculations is mixing units. Entering a diameter in inches and a length in feet will produce a wildly incorrect result. Always convert all dimensions to the same unit before calculating. This calculator handles unit consistency automatically, but for manual calculations, I recommend converting everything to inches first, computing the volume in cubic inches, and then converting to gallons or liters at the end.

Historical Context of Tank Volume Measurement

The challenge of measuring liquid volumes in tanks goes back centuries. Excise officers in 18th and 19th century England needed to calculate the volume of beer in brewing vessels to assess taxes. They developed early "gauging" techniques using wooden dipsticks and lookup tables. The formulas they used for horizontal cylinders were surprisingly precise given the mathematical tools available. Modern tank gauging has evolved to include radar level measurement, servo-operated float gauges, and ultrasonic sensors, but the underlying geometric formulas remain the same. Understanding these formulas matters because electronic gauging systems can fail, and manual calculations serve as the backup verification method in virtually every tank farm operating procedure I have encountered.

The petroleum industry standardized tank volume measurement in the early 20th century through the American Petroleum Institute. API Manual of Petroleum Measurement Standards Chapter 2 covers tank calibration methods, including manual strapping (measuring circumference at multiple heights), optical triangulation, and electro-optical distance ranging. These methods determine the internal dimensions of a tank with sub-millimeter precision, which is necessary when millions of dollars worth of product are transferred based on volume measurements.

modern Considerations

Tanks with Dished Heads

Most cylindrical tanks have dished or elliptical heads rather than flat ends. The volume contribution of each head must be added to the cylinder volume for an precise total. For a 2:1 semi-elliptical head (the most common type in ASME pressure vessels), the volume of each head is V_head = (2/3) × π × R² × (R/2) = π × R³ / 3. For a standard flanged and dished head (ASME F&D), the volume is approximately V_head = 0.0847 × D³, where D is the diameter. On a 48" diameter tank, each 2:1 elliptical head adds about 14,476 cubic inches (62.6 gallons), and each ASME F&D head adds about 9,366 cubic inches (40.5 gallons). For two heads on a 500-gallon nominal tank, this can represent 15-25% of the total volume.

Tanks on Slopes

When a horizontal tank is installed on a slope (intentional or due to settling), the liquid distribution shifts toward the lower end. A 1-degree slope on a 10-foot tank causes the liquid level to differ by about 2.1 inches between ends. For a dipstick reading taken at the center of the tank, the error is relatively small (typically under 1%), but for readings taken near either end, the error can be significant. The most precise approach is to take readings at both ends and average them, or to take the reading at the exact center of the tank length.

Multiple Compartment Tanks

Some tanks, particularly fuel transport tankers, have internal baffles that divide the tank into separate compartments. Each compartment must be calculated individually. The baffles themselves also displace a small amount of volume. For a tanker with five compartments divided by 1/4" steel baffles in a 72" diameter tank, each baffle displaces approximately π × 36² × 0.25 = 1,017.9 cubic inches (4.4 gallons). With four baffles, that is 17.6 gallons of lost capacity.

Thermal Expansion and Overfill Prevention

Every tank operator needs to account for thermal expansion when determining the maximum fill level. Liquids expand as they warm, and a tank filled to 100% capacity at a cool morning temperature may overflow by afternoon if exposed to direct sunlight or rising ambient temperatures. The coefficient of thermal expansion varies by liquid type. Water expands at roughly 0.00021 per degree Celsius (0.000116 per degree Fahrenheit) near room temperature. Gasoline expands at about 0.00095 per degree Celsius, which is more than four times the rate of water. Diesel fuel falls in between at approximately 0.00083 per degree Celsius.

For a 10,000-gallon diesel tank filled to capacity at 50 degrees F, a 30-degree temperature rise to 80 degrees F would increase the volume by approximately 10,000 times 0.000116 times 30 = 34.8 gallons. That expansion has to go somewhere. In a sealed tank, it increases the pressure. In a vented tank, it causes overflow. This is why the petroleum industry standard maximum fill level is 95% for above-ground tanks and why underground tanks with tight fill connections require vapor recovery systems.

Calculating Tank Weight When Full

Understanding the weight of a full tank is critical for structural support design, transportation, and installation planning. Water weighs 8.34 pounds per US gallon (1 kg per liter). Diesel fuel weighs approximately 7.1 pounds per gallon. Gasoline is lighter at about 6.3 pounds per gallon. To calculate the total weight, multiply the liquid volume in gallons by the weight per gallon, then add the empty tank weight (available from the manufacturer).

A 500-gallon water tank, for example, holds 500 times 8.34 = 4,170 pounds of water. If the empty polyethylene tank weighs 120 pounds, the total filled weight is 4,290 pounds, which is over two tons. This weight needs to be distributed over a suitable foundation. A concrete pad should be designed to handle the load, and the soil beneath must have adequate bearing capacity. I have seen cases where a homeowner placed a 1,000-gallon water tank on a wooden deck that was rated for 40 pounds per square foot. The tank, sitting on a 4-foot square footprint, concentrated over 500 pounds per square foot on the deck structure, which failed within weeks.

Underground Tank Volume Considerations

Underground storage tanks (USTs) present additional calculation considerations beyond the basic geometry. Backfill material occupies volume in the excavation but not in the tank itself, yet the total excavation volume matters for installation planning. A typical UST installation requires an excavation that extends 12 inches beyond the tank on all sides and 6 inches below the bottom. For a 10,000-gallon horizontal tank measuring 96 inches in diameter by 20 feet long, the excavation would be roughly 120 inches wide, 108 inches deep, and 264 inches long.

USTs also require ullage (empty space above the liquid) for vapor management. EPA regulations mandate that USTs for petroleum products have overfill prevention devices that stop filling at 95% capacity. The remaining 5% provides space for thermal expansion and vapor accumulation. For a 10,000-gallon tank, this means the maximum usable volume is 9,500 gallons. When planning fuel deliveries, the available volume is the tank capacity at 95% minus the current inventory, not the full geometric volume minus the current level.

Digital Tank Gauging Systems

Modern tank farms use electronic gauging systems that continuously monitor liquid levels and automatically calculate volumes using stored calibration tables. The three primary technologies are radar, magnetostrictive, and hydrostatic pressure systems.

Radar gauges sit on top of the tank and measure the distance to the liquid surface using microwave pulses. They are precise to within 1 millimeter and work with virtually any liquid, including corrosive chemicals and high-temperature products. Guided wave radar uses a probe that extends into the liquid, while non-contact radar transmits through the vapor space. The volume calculation uses the measured distance from the gauge reference point to the liquid surface, subtracts from the tank height to get the fill level, and looks up the corresponding volume in a pre-calculated strapping table.

Magnetostrictive gauges use a float on a vertical guide tube. The float position is detected by timing a torsional wave along a magnetostrictive wire inside the tube. These systems are precise to within 0.5 millimeters and can simultaneously measure the interface between two liquids (such as water under oil). They are the standard for custody transfer measurements in the petroleum industry.

Hydrostatic pressure gauges measure the pressure at the bottom of the tank, which is proportional to the liquid height multiplied by the liquid density. For a tank with a known geometry and a consistent liquid density, the volume can be calculated directly from the pressure reading. However, temperature changes that affect density require compensation, and these systems cannot distinguish between different liquids in a multi-product tank. Despite these limitations, hydrostatic gauges are popular for water and wastewater tanks because they are inexpensive, have no moving parts, and require minimal maintenance.

Frequently Asked Questions

How do I calculate the volume of a horizontal cylindrical tank? ▼

For the total volume, use V = π × R² × L, where R is the radius and L is the length. For partial fill volumes, use the segment formula: V = L × [R² × arccos((R-h)/R) - (R-h) × sqrt(2Rh - h²)], where h is the liquid depth from the bottom. This calculator handles both calculations automatically.

How many gallons does a 1000 liter tank hold? ▼

A 1000 liter tank holds approximately 264.17 US gallons. To convert liters to US gallons, divide by 3.78541. For Imperial gallons, divide by 4.54609. This calculator displays results in both gallons and liters automatically.

What is the partial fill formula for a vertical cylinder? ▼

For a vertical cylinder, the partial fill volume is V = π × R² × h, where R is the radius and h is the liquid height. Unlike horizontal cylinders, the relationship between depth and volume is perfectly linear, making vertical tanks easier to gauge.

How do I account for tank wall thickness? ▼

Always use internal dimensions for volume calculations. Subtract twice the wall thickness from the outer diameter to get the inner diameter. For example, a 48" outer diameter tank with 0.25" walls has a 47.5" inner diameter. Check the tank nameplate or manufacturer specifications for wall thickness.

What is the standard tank size for residential water storage? ▼

Residential water storage tanks typically range from 50 to 500 gallons (190 to 1890 liters). A family of four generally needs 200 to 300 gallons for emergency storage. Well pressure tanks range from 20 to 80 gallons. The right size depends on your daily water usage, which averages 80-100 gallons per person per day in the United States.
