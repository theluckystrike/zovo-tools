What is A1C?

The A1C test (also called hemoglobin A1C, HbA1c, or glycated hemoglobin test) measures the percentage of your red blood cells that have glucose-attached hemoglobin. This provides a reliable picture of your average blood sugar over the previous 2-3 months.

When glucose circulates in your bloodstream, some naturally attaches to hemoglobin (the oxygen-carrying protein in red blood cells). The higher your blood sugar has been, the more hemoglobin becomes glycated, resulting in a higher A1C percentage.

Medical Standards (ADA 2026)

  • Normal: Below 5.7%
  • Prediabetes: 5.7% to 6.4%
  • Diabetes: 6.5% or higher

How the A1C Conversion Works

This calculator uses the clinically validated ADAG (A1C-Derived Average Glucose) formula established by Nathan et al. in 2008. The study included 507 participants using continuous glucose monitors for precise average glucose measurements.

ADAG Formulas

A1C to Glucose:
eAG (mg/dL) = 28.7 × A1C - 46.7
eAG (mmol/L) = 1.59 × A1C - 2.59
Glucose to A1C:
A1C (%) = (eAG mg/dL + 46.7) / 28.7

Formula Accuracy

  • Correlation coefficient: 0.84 (explains 84% of variance)
  • Standard error: ±15.4 mg/dL
  • Validated across multiple populations and diabetes types

Understanding Your A1C Results

The A1C test measures the percentage of hemoglobin proteins in your blood that are coated with sugar. Red blood cells live for approximately 120 days, so the test provides a weighted average of your blood glucose levels over the past two to three months. The most recent 30 days contribute roughly half of the final A1C value, meaning recent changes in blood sugar management will have a noticeable impact on your next test.

Normal A1C levels fall below 5.7 percent. Results between 5.7 and 6.4 percent indicate prediabetes, while 6.5 percent or higher on two separate tests signals type 2 diabetes. For most adults with diabetes, the American Diabetes Association recommends maintaining an A1C below 7 percent. However, your doctor may set a different target based on your age, health conditions, and risk of hypoglycemia.
